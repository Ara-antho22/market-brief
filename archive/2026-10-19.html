Macro Update

FOMC Decision Lands on a Split Read of Inflation and Labor

The Fed's decision this week arrives with economists openly divided, per ABC News, and inflation sitting at a three-year high even as the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.20%. Markets are leaning toward a pause, according to reporting on investor positioning, but the policy outlook remains unsettled enough that both the 10-year and 2-year yields fell this morning as traders priced the uncertainty rather than a clean outcome. That tension between hot inflation and softening labor is exactly the setup that makes this decision a swing factor for the front end.

Read more

CPI is running at 3.72% year over year with the index at 336.30, up 0.65% on the month, a pace ABC7 Los Angeles frames as a three-year high heading into the Fed's decision. At the same time the unemployment rate rose to 4.20%, up 2.44% on the month, which gives the committee a genuinely two-sided data set rather than a clear mandate to tighten or ease. ABC News reports economists are divided on whether the Fed moves at all this year, and separate reporting describes markets betting on a pause while flagging that the policy outlook is far from settled.

This is why the long end moved before the decision rather than after it. The 10-year yield fell 1.73% to 5.10% and the 2-year fell 1.04% to 4.77%, consistent with the market leaning toward a hold or dovish tilt rather than further tightening, while SOFR at 3.88% was little changed, suggesting front-end rate expectations are anchored even as duration adjusts. When the inflation print and the labor print point in opposite directions, the yield curve does the pricing work the Fed itself has not yet done.

For credit, the signal matters because so much leveraged paper is floating rate. IG OAS was unchanged at 0.84 and HY OAS tightened modestly to 3.23, which tells you credit desks are not pricing an aggressive hike as the base case. That matters directly for refinancing math on SOFR-linked term loans and for the forward calendar of HY issuance, since a pause keeps the cost of carry on existing floating-rate debt roughly where it is rather than stepping it up again.

The thing to watch is the statement language itself, specifically whether the committee leans harder on the inflation print or the softening labor data, and how the dot plot and press conference shift expectations for the next meeting. A hawkish surprise would show up first in the 2-year reversing higher and in HY OAS giving back today's tightening.

CoreWeave at 15% Yield Shows Where AI Credit Is Actually Stressed

CoreWeave's bonds now yield 15% as investors reassess AI infrastructure financing risk, per Yahoo Finance, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index just fell for an eighth straight session, its longest losing streak since 2008. The divergence from broad credit is the real story: HY OAS actually tightened to 3.23 this morning, meaning the stress is concentrated in AI-linked paper and equity rather than showing up index-wide yet. WSJ's reporting on weak points in AI bonds and Goldman's note on higher yields cracking the weakest links point at the same seam.

Read more

CoreWeave's bond yield reaching 15% is a specific, name-level repricing of AI infrastructure credit risk, reported by Yahoo Finance as investors reassess the financing behind AI buildouts. Alongside that, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index has now fallen for eight consecutive sessions, the longest streak since 2008, which BigGo Finance ties to crowded AI trades unwinding. WSJ separately reports that the weak points in AI bonds are becoming clearer, and Goldman Sachs, per the Dark Side of the Boom note, argues the market has found its pain threshold where higher yields start to crack the weakest links in the capital structure.

The mechanism here is capital structure specific. AI infrastructure issuers like CoreWeave are financing capex-heavy buildouts against contracted but front-loaded revenue assumptions, so when funding costs rise the market repriced the credit risk of that specific balance sheet rather than the asset class broadly, which is why a 15% yield on one name can coexist with a 3.23 HY OAS on the index. The semiconductor equity weakness is the read-through on the capex side of the same trade, since slower or more uncertain AI spending compresses the revenue assumptions underlying both the equity multiples and the debt coverage ratios.

For financing desks the second-order question is contagion into adjacent structures, including GPU-backed asset financing, private credit exposure to AI capex, and any securitized or special-purpose vehicle paper tied to hyperscaler buildouts. Dispersion of this kind, where a single stressed name trades at a steep premium while the benchmark index stays tight, is exactly the setup that later shows up in CLO tranche pricing and loan fund flows if it broadens, so the gap between CoreWeave's yield and the HY OAS is worth tracking as a spread, not just a headline.

What confirms the read is whether the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index snaps its losing streak and whether other AI-adjacent issuers see similar yield blowouts, which would signal the stress is migrating beyond one credit. What breaks it is Mag7 earnings this week delivering capex guidance that reassures the market on AI revenue visibility, which would be consistent with the semis stabilizing without the broad HY index ever having to move.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · WSJ · BigGo Finance · The Dark Side Of The Boom

French Political Unrest Repricing Debt, Spilling Into the Euro

CNBC reports French political unrest is compounding debt crisis fears, pushing Paris-listed stocks, French bonds, and the euro lower, with one analyst framing it as either the bond market or the public forcing a reckoning. The broader Eurostoxx was down a modest 0.30% to 65.85, which suggests the damage is still largely contained to French-specific assets rather than bleeding across the region. The dollar index firmed 0.13% to 121.60, consistent with euro softness on the other side of that trade.

Read more

CNBC's reporting ties the move directly to French political instability reigniting fears around the country's debt trajectory, with Paris-listed equities, French bonds, and the euro all falling together, the classic signature of a sovereign risk repricing rather than a broad risk-off move. The Eurostoxx 600 itself was only down 0.30% to 65.85, a modest print by the index's own standards, which tells you the selling is concentrated in French names and French paper rather than spreading uniformly across European equities.

The mechanism is straightforward sovereign risk transmission. When political unrest raises doubt about a government's ability to pass fiscal measures or stabilize its debt path, investors demand a higher yield to hold that government's bonds, and that higher government borrowing cost flows through to the cost of capital for domestic corporates and banks that price off the sovereign curve. The euro's weakness is the currency-side expression of the same doubt, since capital that might otherwise sit in euro assets reprices the currency lower, which also explains the modest firming in the dollar index.

For a leveraged finance book, the read-through is the cost of capital for European sponsor-backed deals with French exposure, where a widening OAT-to-bund spread raises the discount rate on French corporate credit and can slow primary issuance or widen new-issue concessions until the political picture stabilizes. Euro softness also matters for any cross-currency financing structure, since European issuers that need to service or hedge dollar-denominated debt face a less favorable exchange rate at the margin.

The thing to watch is whether the Eurostoxx drawdown broadens beyond its current 0.30% or stays contained to Paris-listed names, and whether there is any confidence vote, government announcement, or fiscal measure out of Paris this week that either calms or escalates the political standoff referenced in the CNBC piece.

Sources: CNBC · The Economic Times

Dispersion inside the sector is the real signal. EnergyNow reports that Exxon and Chevron profits are being eroded even as oil prices soar, which points to cost inflation and refining margin pressure at the integrateds outpacing the benefit of a higher realized crude price. That is a different story than the pure upstream names highlighted in Yahoo Finance's piece on stocks with direct exposure to higher natural gas and oil, where a higher strip flows straight through to cash flow with none of the downstream cost drag. XLE's 2.52% print is likely being driven disproportionately by those E&P and exposure names rather than the majors, which explains why the headline move looks strong even with Exxon and Chevron facing margin headwinds.

The setup into earnings adds a financing and capital allocation angle worth watching. Barron's energy earnings preview flags the sector heading into results with crude near 102, which at these levels generally supports free cash flow generation, debt paydown capacity and buyback sustainability for leveraged E&P credits, even as the integrateds have to explain margin compression despite a favorable tape. With HY OAS at 3.23, down slightly on the day, broad credit isn't signaling stress, so any energy-specific spread moves this week are more likely to come from individual earnings prints than from a systemic repricing of the sector's cost of capital.

From here the catalyst list is concrete: earnings prints from the majors that will show whether the Exxon and Chevron margin squeeze EnergyNow is flagging is a one-quarter issue or a trend, and any further detail on the scope of the Russian sanctions waiver that knocked oil down in Asian trading. A durable move toward the $110 level referenced in the TradingView piece would meaningfully change the E&P cash flow math and the capital discipline conversation, while a walk-back on sanctions or a ceasefire headline out of the Iran conflict is the fastest way to deflate today's premium.
