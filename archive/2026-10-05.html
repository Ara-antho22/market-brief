Macro Update

Fed Cuts a Third Time Into a 4.2% Unemployment Print

The Fed delivered its third rate cut of the year as unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1% and September payrolls came in at just 29,000, a labor market that is now visibly cracking rather than merely cooling. The 2-year fell 2.05% to 4.78% on the print, the clearest market confirmation that the easing path is real. For leveraged credit, this is the difference between a soft landing narrative and a defensive one.

Read more

Unemployment rose to 4.2% from 4.1%, an unexpected move, alongside wage growth that cooled more than forecasts, and September payrolls added only 29,000 jobs. The Fed responded with its third rate cut of the year, and the 2-year yield dropped 2.05% to 4.78% on the day, the sharpest move anywhere in the rates complex.

The mechanism here is straightforward: a weakening labor market reduces the Fed's tolerance for holding short rates high, and the front end of the curve re-prices faster than the long end because it is most directly tied to the fed funds path. SOFR at 3.88%, up 0.26% on the day, shows funding markets have not yet fully caught down to where futures are pricing, which is the gap that collapses as cuts are delivered rather than merely signaled.

For leveraged issuers, a falling 2-year matters directly: floating rate term loans reprice off SOFR, and even a modest downshift compounds across a loan book over several quarters. The risk is that the labor data is not just cooling, it is breaking, and HY OAS widening 3.85% to 3.24 alongside IG OAS up 2.38% to 0.86 suggests credit is already pricing a scenario where cuts are a symptom of deterioration rather than a gift to borrowers.

Watch the next payrolls print and any revision to September's 29,000 figure. If wage growth continues decelerating faster than headline CPI (3.35% year over year), real rates stay restrictive even as nominal cuts continue, which is the setup that keeps HY spreads leaking wider even as equities rally.

The Long End Isn't Following the Fed Down

The 10-year fell only 0.95% to 5.24% even as the 2-year dropped 2.05%, a bear-steepening-into-cuts pattern that reflects fiscal concern rather than growth optimism. Federal debt just topped $40 trillion and France's debt burden is hitting the euro directly, while reports describe foreign allies turning net sellers of Treasuries. For anyone structuring or pricing leveraged paper, the long end setting the floor on financing costs matters more than the Fed's policy rate.

Read more

The 10-year yield sits at 5.24%, down only 0.95% on the day versus a 2.05% drop in the 2-year, meaning the curve is steepening even as the Fed cuts. Federal debt has crossed $40 trillion, France's fiscal position is dragging on the euro, and separate reporting describes U.S. allies turning net sellers of Treasuries, pushing yields to multi-decade highs on the long end specifically.

The transmission mechanism runs through term premium, not policy expectations: when the market demands more compensation to hold duration because of supply concerns or reduced foreign demand, long yields can rise or stay elevated independent of what the Fed does with the overnight rate. That is distinct from a growth-driven selloff, and it is why equities can rally on Fed cuts while the long bond refuses to follow.

This is the mechanism that matters most for leveraged finance: term loan pricing may track SOFR down, but any bond tranche, refinancing, or new issue priced off the 10-year is anchored to a yield that isn't falling with the policy rate. IG OAS widening 2.38% to 0.86 and HY OAS widening 3.85% to 3.24 on the same day as a Fed cut is the market telling you spread compensation is rising even as the risk-free short rate falls, which keeps all-in borrowing costs sticky for issuers who need the long end.

Watch whether foreign official-sector buying returns at the next Treasury auctions, and whether France's political and fiscal situation stabilizes enough to stop bleeding into broader European sovereign spreads. A sustained bid for duration would compress the 10-year independent of Fed action; continued seller pressure keeps the bear-steepener intact and keeps credit spreads under upward pressure regardless of how many cuts the Fed delivers.

Crude's 3.23% Drop Gives the Easing Cycle Cover

WTI fell 3.23% to $96.16 on the same day global equities advanced, with reporting attributing the move to easing inflation worries even as eurozone producer prices accelerated to 8.2%, the highest since February 2023. The divergence between falling U.S. energy input costs and still-hot European producer inflation matters for how much room the Fed and ECB each have to keep cutting without reigniting headline CPI.

Read more

WTI dropped 3.23% to $96.16 alongside a broad equity advance, with coverage explicitly linking the move to fading inflation anxiety. At the same time, eurozone producer price inflation accelerated to 8.2%, the highest reading since February 2023, and the European Commission confirmed industrial producer prices rose 1.9% across both the euro area and the EU.

The mechanism is that oil is a direct input to headline CPI with a short transmission lag, so a 3%-plus single-day drop flows into gasoline and transport costs within weeks, giving the Fed more room to validate the cuts the market is already pricing at the front end. The ECB faces the opposite setup: producer price acceleration in Europe means input costs are still working their way into consumer prices even as the ECB's Lane argues demand destruction will mute the pass-through.

For energy-exposed HY issuers, a sustained break below $96 pressures cash flow coverage on E&P and oilfield services names precisely when HY OAS is already widening (up 3.85% to 3.24), compounding the spread pressure in that sector specifically rather than across the index broadly. It also reduces one of the few remaining arguments for the Fed to pause, which is why equities can rally on an oil selloff rather than selling off on growth-scare logic.

Watch whether the eurozone producer inflation acceleration starts showing up in euro area CPI prints over the next one to two months, which would force the ECB to choose between Nagel's view that second-round effects aren't yet evident and Lane's demand-destruction thesis. On the U.S. side, watch whether WTI holds below $96 through the next CPI print, since that is the level at which the energy disinflation narrative either gets confirmed or reverses.
