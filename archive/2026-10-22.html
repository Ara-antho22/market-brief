Newsletter Summary

CPI is running 3.72% year over year and unemployment ticked up to 4.20%, the dual-mandate tension the Fed has to resolve this week, with the midterms adding political noise to the timing.

Macro Update

Fed Walks Into Its Decision With Inflation and Unemployment Both Elevated

CPI is running 3.72% year over year while unemployment rose to 4.20%, leaving the Fed with a genuine two-sided problem heading into this week's decision. BofA estimates a further 1% hike would cost the Treasury an additional $95 billion a year in debt service, a figure that explains why the White House is pushing for a more dovish successor. VIX fell 6.13% to 17.31 into the decision, signaling the market expects a benign outcome, even as the curve sends a more mixed message.

Read more

Unemployment rose to 4.20% while CPI held at 3.72% year over year, the combination Barron's and the Virginia Tech piece frame as the Fed's core dilemma: inflation that has not fully retreated alongside a labor market that is visibly cooling. SOFR held flat at 3.88%, meaning the policy rate itself has not moved yet, but the curve has: the 2-year yield rose 0.42% to 4.79% while the 10-year fell 0.58% to 5.12%, a flattening that suggests the market sees near-term policy staying tight even as longer-run growth and inflation expectations soften.

The transmission here runs through the cost of money at every duration. A front end anchored near 4.8% keeps short-dated funding, including leveraged loan pricing off SOFR, expensive regardless of what happens at the long end. Gold slipped to a two-week low before stabilizing, up 0.34% to $378.95 today, after an earlier oil rally had stoked inflation expectations; crude's 1.93% pullback to $107.89 today removes some of that pressure but has not fully unwound the move, which is why gold has not recovered it either.

The fiscal angle sharpens the stakes. BofA's $95 billion figure ties every additional point of Fed tightening directly to the federal deficit, which is part of why there is open political pressure for a more dovish Fed chair. For leveraged borrowers, the read-through is that policy uncertainty is now entangled with fiscal politics, not just the inflation print, which argues for wider variance in rate expectations than the VIX's 17.31 level currently implies.

The read is confirmed if the Fed holds or leans dovish while acknowledging the rise in unemployment, consistent with the vol compression already priced in. It breaks if the decision or accompanying language leans hawkish on the back of the 3.72% CPI print, which would be a surprise relative to current positioning. Watch the jobless claims print CNBC flagged as pending, and the core PCE data referenced ahead of the decision, for the next read on which side wins.

Sources: Virginia Tech News · Fortune · Reuters · CNBC

Five-Year High Treasury Yields Are Starting to Show Up in Credit Spreads

The 10-year sits at 5.12% even after today's 0.58% pullback, and HY OAS widened 1.22% to 3.33 while IG OAS held flat at 0.87, an early sign that spread cushion is thinning as absolute yields stay elevated. Japanese investors have now sold foreign bonds for five straight weeks, pulling a historically steady source of duration demand out of the market. Separate commentary is now drawing a direct line between US fiscal dynamics and the kind of sovereign credit-spread concerns usually associated with France.

Read more

The 10-year yield closed at 5.12%, down 0.58% on the session but still near levels that have kept financing costs elevated across the credit complex for months. HY OAS rose 1.22% to 3.33 while IG OAS was unchanged at 0.87, and HYG itself was roughly flat, up 0.20% to 76.98. The Economic Times reported Japanese investors have extended their foreign bond selling streak to five consecutive weeks, a flow data point that matters because Japanese institutional demand has historically been a reliable buyer of duration in both Treasuries and dollar credit.

The mechanism is straightforward: when a steady buyer of long-duration paper steps back, term premium has to rise to clear the market, which keeps the base rate that issuers price spread against elevated. That dynamic transmits directly into new-issue HY and IG pricing, since coupons are set off the Treasury curve plus spread, and a 5.12% 10-year means all-in yields for new issuance stay high even if spreads themselves barely move.

For leveraged finance desks the second-order effect is on refinancing math. With SOFR flat at 3.88% and HY OAS now wider at 3.33, sponsors facing near-term maturities face a higher all-in cost of a new deal relative to amending and extending an existing facility, which argues for more amend-and-extend activity over fresh primary issuance in the near term. The Substack piece comparing US fiscal dynamics to France's credit-spread problem is notable less for the comparison itself than for the fact that sovereign debt sustainability questions are starting to bleed into how corporate risk premia get priced.

Confirmation would be HY OAS continuing to widen past 3.33 alongside a sixth straight week of Japanese foreign bond selling, both of which would point to a genuine duration demand problem rather than a one-off data blip. A break would be IG OAS beginning to move off its 0.87 floor, which so far shows no sign of stress, or a Japanese bond flow report with a reversal before the five-week streak extends further.

Sources: The Economic Times · Substack · TheStreet Pro · Chase Bank

US Vol Compression Sits Against a Weaker Asia and a Rattled Gulf

The VIX fell 6.13% to 17.31 as the S&P and Nasdaq both advanced, but the Nikkei dropped 2.65% to 62,907.84 and Shanghai fell 0.67%. Indian shares extended losses on oil-driven inflation worries even as WTI itself fell 1.93% today, and Gulf markets dropped after fresh Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia raised regional risk. The divergence matters because US risk pricing is currently assuming an unusually quiet macro backdrop that the rest of the world is not experiencing.

Read more

US indices rose broadly, led by the Nasdaq's 1.05% gain, while the VIX fell 6.13% to 17.31, a meaningful vol compression heading into a Fed decision. Over the same session the Nikkei fell 2.65% and Shanghai slipped 0.67%, Indian shares extended losses on inflation concerns tied to oil, and Gulf markets dropped after fresh Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia raised regional risk, per Reuters. WTI itself fell 1.93% to $107.89 on the day, a pullback that has not yet shown up in calmer Asian or Gulf trading.

The mechanism is a timing mismatch. Oil's retreat should eventually ease the inflation-linked pressure that has been weighing on import-heavy economies like India, but the immediate equity divergence reflects a US market pricing low event risk into the Fed decision on the back of mega-cap and tech strength, while Asian markets are still digesting both the prior oil overhang and the fresh escalation around Saudi Arabia that has not been priced into US assets.

A VIX falling into a Fed decision is a specific bet that is worth noting on the credit side. Calmer implied vol typically supports HY issuance windows and loan syndication appetite, but that is sitting awkwardly next to HY OAS widening 1.22% to 3.33 the same day, which suggests credit investors are pricing more caution than the options market currently reflects.

The read holds if Nikkei weakness stays contained and oil's 1.93% drop extends, which would support the idea that today's US calm is well-founded. It breaks if the Houthi strikes escalate further and reverse crude's move, or if Nikkei and Shanghai weakness spreads into a broader Asia selloff that eventually forces a repricing of the VIX's current 17.31 level.

Sources: Reuters · Reuters · Proactive financial news

A pickup in that kind of sovereign-backed financing activity would reinforce the sector's current policy tailwind without needing confirmation from credit spreads, which are already undemanding.

The drag traces to office credit stress in secondary markets, with Cleveland receivers racing to process a growing foreclosure docket and Colorado Springs postponing a foreclosure sale on its tallest towers rather than resolving it. Pending home sales fell 7.3% in September per NAR, adding a residential soft spot. Read more

The dominant thread is a bifurcated office market. In Cleveland, Crain's reports receivers are scrambling to keep pace as more downtown office buildings head into foreclosure, a sign the workout pipeline is backing up faster than special servicers can clear it. In Colorado Springs, the foreclosure sale on the city's tallest buildings was postponed rather than completed, which typically means the parties are still negotiating terms rather than resolving the default. At the same time, national data from the office pipeline report shows construction starts shrinking to 32.2 million square feet and vacancy ticking down 80 basis points, and a separate Business Record piece describes stabilization with little new supply and active leasing. The sector is not uniformly distressed, it is bifurcated between legacy secondary-market towers facing lender action and newer or better-located stock that is actually tightening.

Dispersion inside the sector is doing the work today. Office is the visible stress point, concentrated in non-gateway metros like Cleveland and Colorado Springs where older vintage buildings face refinancing walls that top-tier assets are not seeing. Retail is the relative bright spot, with Crain's Chicago Business and The Real Deal both flagging State Street vacancy improving as retailers circle back, a narrow but genuine green shoot. Residential is soft on the transaction side, with NAR's pending home sales index down 7.3% in September, though that data point affects brokers and homebuilders more than the REIT complex directly. XLRE's decline on a day when every major equity index rose suggests investors are pricing the office overhang and the housing transaction slowdown ahead of the retail and office-stabilization offsets.

The financing angle is the headline itself. Foreclosures and postponed foreclosure sales are credit events, and postponement usually signals a lender and borrower still working out extension or discount-payoff terms rather than an orderly resolution. That workout process is happening against a 10-year at 5.12%, down just 0.58% today but still high enough to keep cap rates elevated and refinancing math difficult for maturing office loans. The 2-year ticked up to 4.79%, which raises the cost of shorter-duration bridge and construction financing even as the long end eased modestly. High yield spreads widened slightly to 3.33 from a tighter level, a small move but one that matters for REIT issuers with sub-investment-grade debt tied to office and retail collateral, since any directional widening raises the cost of refinancing exactly the assets showing up in these foreclosure headlines.

From here the setup is a race between secondary-market office distress working through the system and national vacancy and construction data continuing to improve. The Cleveland and Colorado Springs situations are worth tracking for whether more postponements convert into completed foreclosure sales, which would confirm lenders are done extending and ready to take properties back. The next pending home sales print and any follow-through in the State Street retail vacancy trend are the other data points that will tell you whether today's dispersion between distressed office and stabilizing retail persists or converges.
