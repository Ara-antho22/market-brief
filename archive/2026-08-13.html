Macro Update

July CPI at 3.4% Locks the Fed Into a Hold, Not a Cut

July CPI came in at 3.4% yoy, matching expectations and reinforcing consensus that the Fed leaves rates unchanged next month. UST10 fell to 4.70% and UST2 to 4.22% as the print removed tail risk of a hawkish surprise, but it did not open the door to a near-term cut either. The move is a rates story more than a growth story: inflation cooled enough to freeze the Fed, not enough to force its hand.

Read more

July CPI rose 3.4% year over year, matching expectations, with gas price declines doing most of the work while food and shelter costs stayed elevated. Reuters and CFO Dive both frame this as sufficient to keep the Fed on hold next month rather than triggering a rate cut discussion, and Treasury markets priced accordingly, with the 10-year falling 0.42% to 4.70% and the 2-year down 0.71% to 4.22%.

The transmission here runs through the front end. A held-not-cut Fed means the SOFR curve (3.62% spot) stays anchored near current levels for at least one more meeting, which keeps carry costs on floating-rate leveraged loans and revolvers unchanged rather than declining. Equities read the absence of a hawkish surprise as green light for risk, hence the S&P and Nasdaq both advancing, but the rates move is really about removing a left-tail scenario, not adding a right-tail one.

For leveraged issuers, a Fed on hold with inflation still above target means no imminent relief on the cost of new floating-rate debt, and it explains why IG OAS widened 1.28% and HY OAS 0.74% even as equities rallied. If the market believed a cut was coming, spreads would likely be tightening alongside the equity move. Instead the spread widening suggests credit investors are pricing a longer hold than equity investors are, a divergence that tends to resolve through credit repricing rather than equities giving back gains.

Watch the next CPI print and any Fed commentary on the labor data for confirmation. If wage growth continues to slow (Business Insider flags four straight months of wages trailing inflation) alongside a soft jobs number, that combination would pull the Fed toward a cut and should show up first as HY OAS compression rather than further equity upside.

Sources: Reuters · CFO Dive

Payrolls Shed 23,000 as Wage Growth Stalls Four Straight Months

The July jobs report showed the economy shed 23,000 jobs, and wage growth has now trailed inflation for four consecutive months. That combination weakens the case for the Fed to worry about wage-driven inflation, which is part of why the market is comfortable with a hold rather than pricing in a hike. It also directly threatens consumer credit performance heading into the back half of the year.

Read more

July payrolls fell by 23,000, a headline that eciks.org frames as confirmation of a cooling labor market, while Business Insider and Investopedia both document that wage growth has lagged inflation for four straight months. NBC News ties this directly to the CPI release, noting inflation stayed stubborn even as wage gains slowed for workers, a stagflationary mix at the household level even if the aggregate CPI print looks tame.

The mechanism for markets is that a weakening labor print without a corresponding wage acceleration removes the Fed's main inflation-persistence argument, reinforcing the hold call and keeping the front end of the curve anchored. But job losses of this magnitude also flow directly into consumer discretionary spending and, eventually, into consumer ABS and card-backed credit performance, since real wage erosion compounds the effect of a shrinking payroll base.

For her seat, the read-through is on consumer-facing leveraged credits and any issuer with variable-rate consumer exposure. Weak wage growth squeezing spending power, as Investopedia lays out, is exactly the kind of slow-bleed macro condition that shows up first in same-store sales softness and later in covenant headroom for retail and consumer-products credits, well before it shows up in the unemployment rate itself, which actually fell 2.38% in this data set to 4.10%, a divergence worth flagging since the payroll and unemployment-rate signals are pulling in different directions this cycle.

Watch the next payrolls print for whether the 23,000 decline was noise or trend, and watch whether wage growth reaccelerates. If both jobs and wages weaken together, that is a demand-destruction signal that should widen HY spreads further regardless of what the Fed does with rates.

Credit Spreads Widen Into the Rally as Issuance Pipeline Builds

HY OAS widened 0.74% to 2.72 and IG OAS widened 1.28% to 0.79 even as the S&P and Nasdaq advanced, a divergence that typically precedes either a valuation reset in credit or a rotation of flows toward equity beta. Meanwhile QTS is out scheduling investor calls for a likely debt offering and SK hynix halted bond buybacks ahead of its own issuance, both signs the primary market is gearing up to test this spread level. The setup matters because any softening in the hold narrative could catch new issuance at wider levels than issuers priced for at the start of the week.

Read more

Both investment-grade and high-yield spreads widened today, IG OAS by 1.28% to 0.79 and HY OAS by 0.74% to 2.72, even as equity indices posted gains and the VIX fell to 15.28. Separately, QTS is scheduling investor calls ahead of a likely debt offering, and SK hynix halted its own bond buybacks specifically to clear the runway for new issuance, both signals that the primary credit market is active into this print rather than waiting on the sidelines.

The mechanism is that spread and equity markets do not have to move together, and when they diverge it usually reflects different investor bases digesting the same data differently. Equity buyers are pricing the CPI print as risk-on because it validates a Fed hold without a hike; credit buyers are pricing the same print more cautiously, likely because inflation at 3.4% with a still-elevated cost base means real yields on new paper need to stay wide enough to compensate for a longer hold rather than an imminent cut.

For a leveraged finance desk this is the more actionable signal than the index-level equity move. Wider OAS at the point issuers are actively marketing new deals (QTS, SK hynix) means new-issue concessions get bigger, all-in coupons stay elevated even if benchmark rates ease, and any issuer without a near-term maturity wall has more reason to wait rather than print into spread widening. It also means secondary paper issued at tighter spreads earlier this year is now underwater relative to today's curve, a mark-to-market drag for credit funds holding it.

The next confirming or disconfirming data point is whether the QTS and SK hynix deals price inside or outside current indicative spread levels. A deal pricing tighter than the OAS move would suggest today's widening was technical and reflects a temporary rotation into equities rather than a genuine repricing of credit risk. A deal pricing wide, or a pulled deal, would confirm that credit investors are demanding more compensation than the equity tape currently reflects.

With UST10 at 4.70% and SOFR at 3.62%, absolute financing costs for energy borrowers are still elevated relative to a low-teens spread environment, so any further OAS widening compounds rather than offsets the base-rate burden.

The setup from here hinges on whether the Hormuz risk premium proves transient or gets validated by an actual supply disruption. If the strait headlines fade without incident, WTI likely reverts toward the level implied by the Reuters demand-and-inventory narrative, dragging XLE down with it and easing the modest credit-spread pressure seen today. The specific catalyst to watch is any EIA inventory print or IEA balance revision that either confirms the higher US stock build cited by Reuters or validates a supply-side tightening from the Strait situation, since that data point will determine whether energy credit spreads continue to widen into a genuine repricing or retrace as geopolitical noise clears.
