Macro Update

Waller Backs December Hike Against Hot Jobs and CPI

Fed's Waller said he will support at least a quarter-point hike in December, landing alongside an October payrolls report of 165,000 jobs added, unemployment falling to 4.10%, and CPI running 3.72% year over year. The 2-year yield outran the 10-year, up 1.12% to 4.51% versus a 0.41% rise to 4.86%, a front-end-led move consistent with markets pricing a hawkish December outcome. It matters because the front end, not the long end, is what resets the cost of floating-rate leveraged debt.

Read more

Fed's Waller said he plans to support at least a quarter-point hike in December, arriving alongside an October payrolls report showing 165,000 jobs added, unemployment falling to 4.10% (down 2.38% on the month), and CPI running 3.72% year over year with the CPI index itself up 0.65% in the latest read. The 2-year yield rose 1.12% to 4.51% while the 10-year rose a smaller 0.41% to 4.86%, a front-end-led move consistent with markets pricing a hawkish December outcome rather than a growth shock.

Front-end yields are the direct transmission channel into financing costs: SOFR, at 3.88%, is the base rate off which floating-rate leveraged loans and revolvers price, so a December hike raises the coupon on every unhedged floating-rate liability on day one, before any change in credit spread. That is distinct from a 10-year move, which affects duration-sensitive fixed income and equity discount rates but does not touch a borrower's cash interest expense the way a front-end hike does.

HY OAS tightened to 3.42 even as the front end sold off, which suggests the market is treating this as a rate story, not a credit story, for now. That matters for the leveraged finance calendar because all-in yields for new issuance are rising on the risk-free leg while the risk premium leg compresses, a combination that still lets issuers come to market. The risk is that spread compression has less room to offset further hikes if the Fed moves again, narrowing the cushion for marginal credits.

Watch today's CPI print and the Fed's own rate decision for whether the hawkish read holds. Barron's and NBC both frame the October CPI release as a major input into the Fed's next move, and a cooler-than-expected reading would undercut Waller's case and could reverse the front-end selloff quickly given how thin the move already is.

Sources: Reuters · Scripps News · CNBC · 24/7 Wall St.

The S&P 500 fell 4.36% to 7,353.73 in the same session the Nasdaq gained 2.74% to 25,253.14, the Dow gained 2.15% to 52,389.75, and the Russell 2000 gained 3.23% to 282.51. That is a wide dispersion for one trading day, and it happened alongside a 9.01% drop in the VIX to 25.64, a combination that does not fit a textbook broad-based equity selloff.

A large-cap-weighted index can fall sharply on the strength of a handful of heavy constituents even as the median stock and small caps rally, and the session's Nvidia headline, shares sinking after a report of an investigation into its CEO, is the kind of idiosyncratic, single-name shock that can move the S&P's level without moving the VIX, since options pricing reflects aggregate dispersion rather than one stock's news. International markets moved with the rally cohort rather than the S&P: the Nikkei gained 2.87%, the Eurostoxx gained 3.63%, the FTSE gained 2.33% and Shanghai gained 1.64%.

Credit did not follow the S&P lower. HY OAS tightened 1.72% to 3.42 and IG OAS was unchanged at 0.93, both consistent with the rest of the risk complex, Nasdaq, Dow, Russell, global indices, rather than with the S&P's headline number. Fortune's framing of a market bloodbath exposing debt fears is harder to square with spreads this tight; if credit investors shared that fear, OAS would be widening, not compressing.

The read to watch is whether the S&P's weakness stays contained to a narrow set of AI and chip-adjacent names or broadens into the cash credit market. If chipmaker CDS keeps widening while HY cash spreads stay tight, that confirms a composition story. A move where HY OAS starts widening alongside the S&P would be the signal that this has become a genuine risk-off event rather than an index-level quirk.

Sources: Investment Week · Fortune · Reuters

HY Spreads Tighten to 3.42 as AI Credit Risk Shifts to Chipmakers

The cash bond market and the CDS market are pricing the AI buildout's risk very differently. That split matters for where financing stress would show up first.

HY OAS tightened 1.72% to 3.42 and HYG was roughly flat, up 0.36% to 77.51, even as Peloton tapped the junk bond market for the first time in a refinancing deal and Goldman flagged that chipmakers' CDS spreads have surpassed hyperscale cloud providers' for the first time, with AI-linked credit default swaps at record highs.

Tight HY OAS alongside record AI CDS spreads reflects two different parts of the same capital structure repricing differently. Cash high yield bonds, which fund operating companies across sectors, are trading on broad technical demand for yield, tight enough that a first-time issuer like Peloton can access the market. CDS on chip names, a purer read on AI capex and customer concentration risk, is the instrument actually pricing the AI buildout's credit risk, and that risk is now seen as sitting more with the chipmakers supplying capacity than with the hyperscalers buying it.

For financing desks the split matters because it changes where to look for stress first. A widening in chip CDS without a corresponding move in HY cash spreads or IG OAS, flat at 0.93, suggests the AI capex risk is still contained to a specific credit silo rather than bleeding into the broader leveraged loan and bond market that funds unrelated sectors. That containment is what allows deals like Peloton's refinancing to price through a week when equity headlines look volatile.

Watch whether the Peloton deal prices in line with recent HY issuance or needs a concession, which would be the first tell that technical demand is softening. Also watch whether chip CDS widening starts to show up in investment-grade spreads for hyperscaler-adjacent issuers, which would mean the AI credit risk is migrating up the capital structure rather than staying isolated.

Credit conditions remain a tailwind regardless of the crude wobble. The EnergyNow piece on depleted global oil inventories is the one headline worth flagging for asymmetry: it argues the next price spike could roil markets precisely because stocks are already thin, meaning any reversal of the Iran de-escalation narrative could move crude faster than the orderly slide seen today. The near-term catalyst is explicitly geopolitical, any concrete development on Iran, alongside whatever inventory data confirms or complicates the depletion thesis.

The standout approval is Bayer's asundexian, now cleared by the FDA as a first-in-class factor XIa inhibitor for secondary stroke prevention, per Fierce Pharma and NeurologyLive. That is a new mechanism entering a market historically dominated by existing anticoagulants, and the approval alone is a meaningful pipeline validation for Bayer regardless of how quickly it scales commercially. Separately, ABC News reported an FDA approval for a breakthrough drug targeting advanced pancreatic cancer, one of oncology's harder-to-treat indications. The dispersion within the tape is straightforward: approvals helped, a rejection hurt. Bayer and Merck are the clearer beneficiaries today, each advancing a specific franchise rather than relying on a single catalyst. The lenacapavir generic tentative approval is a slower-burn story for Gilead, since tentative approval signals a future competitive threat to Sunlenca's exclusivity without creating immediate market access, but it is a data point worth tracking for patent-cliff timing.

There is nothing in today's headlines pointing to a financing or balance sheet story specific to health care, so the more relevant read is positioning. Health care's 1.91% gain against a 4.36% drop in the S&P is the kind of divergence that shows up when investors rotate toward sectors with steadier cash flows during a broader equity drawdown, and the VIX falling 9% to 25.64 alongside HY OAS tightening 1.72% suggests credit markets are not pricing distress even as the S&P sells off. That combination, calm credit plus a defensive equity rotation, is consistent with investors treating today's weakness as idiosyncratic to the broader index rather than a systemic risk-off event.

The next catalyst is less about the sector tape and more about follow-through on today's approvals: how quickly asundexian gets payer coverage and formulary placement will determine whether Bayer's first-in-class advantage translates into share gains against incumbent anticoagulants, and the lenacapavir generic's tentative status means the real Gilead exclusivity question gets resolved later, likely through litigation or patent expiry timing rather than this headline alone. Watch for commentary from Gilead on the generic threat and from Novocure on any path to resubmission for the rejected device.

PepsiCo's decision to shed about 1,000 North America jobs is a self-help move against a sales slump that RSM's broader framing suggests is sector-wide: food producers are running out of room to recover cost inflation through price before volume elasticity bites back. That is the opposite of the 2022-2023 playbook, when staples companies pushed through repeated price increases with little pushback. Now the labor cuts at Pepsi read as an admission that further pricing is off the table and cost structure has to absorb the gap instead.

Dispersion within the sector is showing up in category and geography specifics rather than broad multiples. Separately, Britannia, PepsiCo and Hindustan Unilever are reworking recipes ahead of new food labelling rules in India, which adds near-term reformulation and compliance cost on top of the margin squeeze RSM is describing domestically. None of this is dramatic on its own, but it stacks the deck against companies that were already leaning on price.

The more relevant angle is capital discipline: PepsiCo cutting headcount rather than cutting debt or issuing to fund buybacks suggests management teams in the group are prioritizing opex reduction over balance sheet actions, which is consistent with a sector trying to protect margins organically rather than through financial engineering.

The setup from here hinges on whether the RSM-flagged pricing window actually closes.
