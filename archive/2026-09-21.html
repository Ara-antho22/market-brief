Macro Update

Yields Fall as Fed 'Regains Trust,' Spreads Tighten Further

The 10-year is down 1.40% to 4.94% and the 2-year down 1.48% to 4.67% even after last week's Fed hike, with WSJ attributing the rally to the Fed "regaining trust" on inflation. VIX fell 4.08% to 14.81 and both HY OAS (2.68, -0.74%) and IG OAS (0.77, -1.28%) tightened in tandem. The single reason this matters: credibility, not just rate levels, is what's compressing the term premium and risk premia simultaneously.

Read more

The Fed hiked and the BOE held rates unchanged, per WSJ, yet the long end of the Treasury curve rallied rather than sold off, with the 10-year falling to 4.94% and the 2-year to 4.67% while SOFR held flat at 3.85%. VIX dropped 4.08% to 14.81 in the same window, and both investment grade and high yield spreads tightened, IG OAS to 0.77 and HY OAS to 2.68.

This is a credibility repricing rather than a rate-level repricing. A hike that the market reads as decisive on inflation lowers the inflation risk premium embedded in long-dated yields, which is why the curve can rally even as the policy rate itself moves higher or holds. Lower realized and implied volatility then does double duty, compressing both duration risk premia in rates and default/liquidity risk premia in credit spreads at the same time.

For leveraged finance, tighter OAS on both sides of the credit spectrum plus a rallying long end is the best-case financing backdrop: cost of capital falls for new issuance and existing floating-rate borrowers see relief if SOFR eventually follows the curve down. HYG's 0.25% gain is a small but real signal of retail and ETF demand supporting the tighter-spread move, which typically precedes a reopening of primary calendars for refinancing and opportunistic issuance.

The read breaks if either the next jobs print or the next CPI release surprises hot, especially given Kashkari's comment that the inflation fight remains "painful" and that inflation is still too high in "all aspects" of the economy. Watch the NFP release referenced in the stonex.com preview and whether diesel-driven cost pressure shows up in the next CPI index reading above the current 334.13 level.

Oil Spikes 4.5% on Yemen Escalation, Reviving Inflation Pass-Through Risk

WTI jumped 4.49% to $107.02 as Houthis push for control of Yemen highlands, with Reuters reporting Trump did not pledge military support in a call with Yemen's president after being said to have called off strikes. This matters because it lands directly on top of already-record diesel prices, threatening the disinflation narrative just as credit spreads had been tightening on Fed credibility.

Read more

WTI rose 4.49% to $107.02 in the same session that Reuters reported Houthi forces pushing for control of Yemen highlands and separately reported that Trump, in a call with Yemen's president, did not pledge US military support against the Houthis after earlier being said to have called off strikes. Diesel prices are already at record highs, per WMAR 2 News and qz.com, which flags the threat to transport stocks and broader inflation.

The transmission mechanism runs through freight and fuel costs rather than headline crude alone. The Atlanta Fed's work on shipping cost shocks and producer price pass-through shows that these input costs move into PPI quickly during crisis-type shocks, and diesel is a disproportionately large input for trucking and logistics margins, so a Yemen-driven shipping risk premium reaches CPI (currently 3.35% year over year, index at 334.13) faster than a generic crude move would.

For the credit book, this is a direct stress test of the spread tightening described above. Transport and consumer-facing high yield issuers with fuel-cost exposure face margin compression exactly as HY OAS sits at 2.68, testing whether that tightening holds if energy costs keep climbing. It also complicates the Fed's "regained trust" story, since Kashkari's framing that the inflation fight will stay "painful" gets more credible if an energy shock reasserts itself on top of already-record diesel.

Watch whether WTI holds above the $107 level into the next session or reverts as a single-day geopolitical spike, and watch the energy and transport components of the next CPI print. Any explicit escalation toward direct US military engagement in Yemen, beyond the current no-pledge stance Reuters reported, would be the next catalyst to track.

Sources: Reuters · qz.com · Atlanta Fed

AI Credit Stress Diverges From Broad Spread Tightening

SoftBank is seeking more than $11 billion in junk bond financing collateralized by OpenAI and Arm share pledges, while Oracle's Project Jupiter loans have fallen below 90 cents on the dollar, even as aggregate HY OAS (2.68, -0.74%) and IG OAS (0.77, -1.28%) tighten. This matters because it shows idiosyncratic leverage risk building inside AI-linked capital structures underneath an otherwise benign spread tape.

Read more

SoftBank is marketing over $11 billion in junk bond financing with OpenAI and Arm share pledges as collateral, a structure the reporting flags as raising leverage risk. Separately, Oracle's Project Jupiter loans have fallen below 90 cents on the dollar. Invesco's Baskind, per International Financing Review, sees a broader dealflow pickup but remains explicitly cautious on AI.

Share-pledge collateral is mark-to-market sensitive in a way traditional covenant-based leveraged loans are not: if OpenAI or Arm equity values swing, margin or collateral calls can force deleveraging independent of the borrower's own operating cash flow. Project Jupiter loans trading below 90 cents indicate the market is already pricing execution and recovery risk on AI capex-linked debt ahead of any formal distress event, which is a leading indicator rather than a lagging one.

This divergence is happening while aggregate high yield and investment grade spreads are tightening 74bps and 128bps respectively, meaning the market is currently treating AI-linked credit risk as idiosyncratic rather than systemic. If Project Jupiter-style repricing continues, it becomes a read-through for the broader wave of AI-linked debt, including data center and hyperscaler capex financing, that leveraged finance desks have been underwriting, and the SoftBank deal size is itself a live test of junk bond demand for concentrated single-name equity collateral risk.

Watch pricing and demand on the SoftBank $11 billion-plus deal, further marks on Project Jupiter paper, and whether Invesco's cautious framing spreads to other credit managers. A widening gap between AI-linked HY paper and the broader HY OAS of 2.68 would be the tell that this stops being contained.

MarketWatch flagged that company insiders are buying energy names into that exact risk, a signal that management sees value even if a ceasefire caps the crude bid. Broader risk assets rallied (S&P +0.17%, VIX -4.08%, HY OAS -0.74% to 2.68) as oil and yields gave back part of last week's spike, leaving energy as the session's clear laggard against a otherwise risk-on tape.

The dominant story is a repricing of geopolitical premium rather than a straightforward crude move. WTI rose 4.49% to 107.02 on the session, but that print sits against an AP News narrative describing oil and bond yields giving back some of last week's jump, and a MarketWatch piece explicitly framing the trade around what happens to energy stocks if the Iran war ends suddenly. XLE fell 2.63%, which is the equity market pricing forward risk to the crude curve rather than reacting to today's spot level. That gap between a firmer crude print and a sharply lower energy tape is the session's central tension.

Insider buying is the notable signal inside that tension. MarketWatch's reporting that company insiders are buying even as a ceasefire would strike a blow against oil prices and energy equities suggests management teams are treating current levels as attractive regardless of near-term geopolitical resolution, a classic value signal when the market is discounting tail risk. Refining subsector strength stands out against that backdrop, with Rigzone describing an AI-like mania in refining names, pointing to crack spread dynamics or capacity tightness decoupling refiners from the upstream and integrated names dragging the index lower. TSX energy shares were also described as muted on the same oil-slide narrative, reinforcing that the pressure is broad across producer-heavy indices rather than idiosyncratic.

On the financing side, credit conditions remain unusually supportive for a sector this volatile. That divergence matters for capital structure decisions: producers and refiners can still term out debt or fund capex at tight spreads even as equity multiples compress on ceasefire risk, which historically has supported buybacks and bolt-on M&A rather than defensive deleveraging. Insider buying into equity weakness while credit stays tight is consistent with management viewing the selloff as a multiple problem, not a balance sheet problem.

The setup from here hinges entirely on the Iran war headline flow. A durable ceasefire would validate the equity selloff and pressure crude further, testing whether refiners can hold their premium once crack spreads normalize and whether insider buyers were early or simply wrong. Continued escalation, or a stalled resolution, would likely see energy equities catch back up to the firmer WTI print, especially if credit spreads stay tight and give producers cover to keep leverage steady. Watch WTI's reaction to any concrete de-escalation headline and whether refining names give back the gains Rigzone flagged, since that will show whether the mania is fundamentally grounded or purely positioning.
