Macro Update

Fed Hike Now the Base Case, First Under Warsh

A Reuters poll of economists has shifted to expecting a rate hike at Wednesday's FOMC meeting, with at least one more to follow, which would be the first hike since 2023 and the first policy move of the Warsh era. It follows a 9-3 July vote with Logan dissenting. The move matters because it flips the market's operating assumption from cuts to hikes right as inflation data and oil prices are both moving the wrong way.

Read more

Reuters' poll of economists now points to a Wednesday hike as the likely outcome, with more tightening penciled in after that, a reversal from the cutting-cycle narrative that dominated most of this year. This would be the first hike since 2023 and the first rate decision under new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, following a July vote that split 9-3 with Logan dissenting. CPI is running at 3.35% year over year with the index up 0.40% on the month, giving the hawks cover.

The mechanism here is straightforward but consequential for anyone pricing floating-rate paper: a hike moves the policy rate that SOFR is anchored to, and SOFR currently sits at 3.62% unchanged, so any move Wednesday resets the reference rate under every leveraged loan and revolver in the market immediately. Inflation running above target with oil now spiking gives the committee justification to act even with growth data that isn't screaming for tightening.

For leveraged finance specifically, a higher SOFR floor raises debt service on floating-rate capital structures the same week HY OAS compressed 1.85% to 2.65, so spread compression is partially offsetting base-rate increases rather than fully absorbing them. That combination, tighter spreads against a rising base rate, keeps all-in yields for new issuance elevated even as the credit market signals it isn't worried about default risk. Watch whether primary HY and loan pricing this week reflects the SOFR reset or whether spread tightening keeps all-in cost of debt roughly flat.

The confirming signal is Wednesday itself: the decision, the dot plot showing how many hikes follow the first, and Warsh's press conference tone. A dissent count wider than July's 9-3 split, or language suggesting this is a one-and-done move, would break the market's current second-hike pricing and should reprice front-end rates fast.

Sources: Reuters · Dallas Express · Reuters

10-Year Touches 5% First Time Since 2023

The 10-year yield hit 5% intraday, the highest since October 2023, according to CNBC, NYT and Firstpost, arriving right before Wednesday's Fed decision. The 2-year is up 2.93% to 4.56% on the same repricing. The long end selling off into a hiking decision is the opposite of what happened during the last tightening cycle's early stages, when curves flattened on growth fears instead.

Read more

Multiple outlets confirm the 10-year touched 5% for the first time since October 2023, moving in tandem with the 2-year note's 2.93% jump to 4.56%, both ahead of Wednesday's FOMC decision. This is a broad curve repricing, not an isolated front-end move tied to Wednesday's meeting alone.

The mechanism is a market demanding more term compensation for a Fed that a Reuters poll now says will hike and then hike again, meaning the terminal rate embedded in longer-dated paper has to rise to match. One piece in the coverage frames it bluntly: every hike now lands directly on the Treasury's own funding tab, so a higher policy path also raises the government's marginal cost of issuance, which itself pressures long-end yields further through supply expectations.

For capital structure work, the read-through is a higher discount rate on any financing priced off the 10-year, meaning LBO debt packages, opco-propco structures, and refinancings anchored to Treasuries all get more expensive at the same moment SOFR itself may be reset higher on Wednesday. What's notable is that this yield move has not shown up in the VIX, which fell 11.21% to 15.84, or in HY OAS, which tightened to 2.65, so equity and credit vol are not yet pricing the same stress the rates market is.

The thing to watch is whether the 10-year holds above 5% after Wednesday's decision and dot plot, versus reversing if the hike is delivered but framed as a close call or a peak. A break back below 5% on a dovish press conference would suggest the long end had already priced the worst case; a push higher would confirm the market believes the second hike flagged by the Reuters poll is coming soon.

Sources: CNBC · The New York Times · Firstpost

Oil Jumps 3.2%, Feeding the Same Inflation Case as the Hike

WTI rose 3.24% to 97.26 the same session CPI year-over-year sits at 3.35%, and U.S. News flagged the Fed meeting as happening amid uncertainty over oil alongside AI and inflation. Reuters separately noted Canada's August inflation held at 3% specifically because crude stayed firm. Energy is now doing double duty as both a market mover and a direct input into the inflation print the Fed is reacting to this week.

Read more

WTI climbed 3.24% to 97.26 in the same session that CPI year-over-year is running 3.35% with the index up 0.40% month over month, and U.S. News framed Wednesday's Fed meeting explicitly as happening amid uncertainty over oil, AI, and inflation together. Reuters' Canada coverage makes the same link directly, attributing that country's steady 3% inflation reading to crude staying firm even as food prices eased.

The mechanism is direct pass-through: energy costs feed straight into headline CPI with a short lag, and a 3%-plus move in crude in a single session reinforces exactly the inflation narrative that pushed the Reuters poll of economists toward expecting Wednesday's hike. Because this is a supply-side price move rather than a demand-driven one, it complicates the Fed's job, since a hike aimed at demand doesn't directly address an oil-driven CPI print, but the committee has to react to the number regardless of its source.

On the credit side, firmer crude is a tailwind for energy-sector HY and leveraged loan issuers, whose cash flow and coverage ratios improve directly with the commodity, while it's a headwind for everyone else facing a stickier inflation backdrop that keeps SOFR-linked coupons higher for longer. That HY OAS still tightened to 2.65 despite this suggests the credit market is reading the oil move as more supportive to energy credits than threatening to broad default risk.

Confirmation to watch is whether crude keeps climbing into Wednesday's decision, since a continued spike would harden the case for the second hike the Reuters poll already flags, versus a pullback that would let the Fed treat this print as transitory and soften the path guidance in the dot plot.

Sources: U.S. News & World Report · Reuters

Energy XLE -0.72%

Crude spikes, sector equities lag

WTI jumped 3.24% to $97.26 on reports that attacks are choking off Saudi supply, but XLE still fell 0.72% even as the S&P, Nasdaq and Dow all rallied on the session. The gap reflects a market pricing a geopolitical supply shock into the barrel while punishing energy equities alongside a reported run toward a 5% ten-year yield. Credit isn't panicking: HY OAS tightened another 1.85% to 2.65, treating the shock as a pass-through rather than a systemic risk. Within the tape, refiners and select upstream names are the exception to an otherwise soft sector print.

Read more

The dominant story is supply, not demand. Reports from the New York Times, the Guardian and the Hartford Courant all point to an intensifying Houthi campaign against Saudi energy infrastructure, and the market response was immediate: WTI up 3.24% to 97.26 on the day, with the BBC noting petrol and diesel prices have hit their highest level since 2022. That combination, a supply-side shock landing on top of already-elevated pump prices, is the kind of setup that typically forces a repricing of both the crude curve and downstream margins simultaneously.

The dispersion inside the sector is doing the real work. Valero has rallied 148% over the past year and IBD flags Vista, the Mexico-based E&P, as approaching a buy point specifically because the oil surge drops straight to upstream cash flow. But XLE's 0.72% decline shows the index-level story is not uniformly bullish. TradeStation's characterization of stocks teetering as oil and rates surge together, and the Stamford Advocate's link between the oil spike and a reported run at the 10-year toward 5%, suggests capital-intensive energy names are getting caught in the same discount-rate compression hitting the rest of the tape, even as their commodity input just repriced higher.

This is a genuinely useful environment for energy balance sheets if the price holds. HY OAS at 2.65, down another 1.85% on the day, and IG OAS flat at 0.80 show credit is not demanding a risk premium for the disruption, which matters because higher realized crude prices accelerate free cash flow generation and debt paydown for E&P and refining issuers alike, improving the refinancing math for BB and single-B names precisely when spreads are already tight. The tension is that UST2 rose 2.93% to 4.56% and one headline puts the 10-year near 5%, so while the debt market is rewarding energy credits with better coverage ratios, the equity market is applying a higher discount rate to the same cash flows, which helps explain why bonds and equities in this sector are not moving together today.

The setup from here hinges on duration, not direction. If the Houthi campaign against Saudi infrastructure proves sustained rather than episodic, the supply premium in crude stays embedded and upstream free cash flow keeps improving, which is constructive for leverage tolerance and M&A appetite across E&Ps. If it fades quickly, XLE's underperformance today looks like the correct read and the equity market's rate-driven caution was the signal worth following. The specific catalyst to watch is confirmation of the scale and duration of the Saudi supply disruption, alongside any Fed or rates commentary responding to the reported move toward 5% on the ten-year.

Nvidia sold off while Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft gained, and data-center power and construction names got hit on renewed doubt about the AI buildout pace. Oracle fell 4.72% even as its $95 billion capex plan lifted server suppliers Dell and HPE, underscoring a widening split between who funds the AI buildout and who benefits from it.

Read more

The tape's dominant story is a safety-driven repricing of the AI trade rather than a macro rotation. Three AI chiefs calling for frontier model development to slow down triggered broad selling in AI-exposed names, described across coverage as AI stocks stumbling amid growing safety fears and a slowdown that hammered data-center power and construction stocks tied to the buildout, including GEV. This is happening against a backdrop where the broader market had every reason to rally: crude up 3.24%, the VIX down over 11%, and high yield spreads tightening 1.85% to 2.65, all consistent with risk-on positioning everywhere except inside the AI complex itself.

Dispersion within the sector is the real story. Nvidia fell on the safety headlines while Meta, Alphabet and Microsoft actually rose, a signal that capital is rotating toward diversified hyperscalers with cash-generative core businesses and away from pure-play chip and infrastructure exposure most levered to continued frontier-model scaling. Oracle dropped 4.72% despite its own $95 billion capex commitment being the headline that lifted Dell and HPE as suppliers, meaning the market is rewarding the beneficiaries of AI infrastructure spend while penalizing the entity taking on the balance sheet risk to fund it. Separately, coverage highlighting software names with recurring revenue as AI spending gets rethought suggests investors are also seeking shelter in subscription-model businesses less exposed to a capex-cycle air pocket.

The financing angle is where this gets interesting for credit. Oracle committing $95 billion to capex is a leverage story as much as a growth story, and the stock's decline even as the capex plan is framed as a positive for the broader AI hardware chain suggests the market is starting to discount Oracle's own credit metrics and free cash flow trajectory rather than simply cheering the spend. That this is happening while HY OAS is tightening broadly (2.65, -1.85% on the day) and IG OAS is flat at 0.80 tells you the credit market isn't yet demanding a risk premium for AI capex leverage sector-wide, but single-name dispersion in equities is already flagging where that scrutiny will land first if capex-funded balance sheets can't show a near-term payback.

The setup from here hinges on whether the safety pushback from AI leadership translates into anything policy-relevant or is a sentiment shock that fades. If frontier model development actually slows, capex-heavy names like Oracle face a double hit of higher leverage and a longer payback runway, while hyperscalers with diversified revenue bases have more room to absorb it. Watch Oracle's own guidance and any commentary on capex financing, along with whether data-center power and construction names stabilize, as the tell for whether this is a rotation within AI or the start of broader repricing of AI-linked leverage.

The dispersion here favors smaller-cap, single-asset biotech and specialty diagnostic names over diversified large-cap pharma, since approval events are binary re-rating catalysts for names like Scholar Rock and Telix in a way they are not for a diversified major. Isemblyd's approval validates a novel muscle-targeting mechanism in SMA, a category where efficacy data can translate directly into a re-rated equity story. Telix's back-to-back imaging approvals in glioma and brain cancer give it two new revenue lines in oncology diagnostics, a subsector that has been quietly building out ahead of broader radiopharmaceutical adoption. For a name like Scholar Rock, an approval event is often the trigger to access capital markets on improved terms, since de-risked, revenue-stage biotechs command materially tighter spreads and higher equity valuations than pre-approval peers. The move in UST2 to 4.56%, up nearly 3% on the session, is a modest headwind on the discount-rate side, but the scale of the HY spread compression suggests credit conditions are still net accommodative for this cohort. Larger diversified pharma and device names, by contrast, are less sensitive to today's spread move since their capital structures already sit comfortably in investment-grade territory near an IG OAS of 0.80%.

From here the setup is about follow-through: whether Scholar Rock and Telix use this window to raise capital while spreads are tight and volatility is low, and whether the pancreatic cancer approval translates into a visible commercial ramp for its still-unnamed sponsor. The catalyst that would change the picture is a reversal in the VIX or a re-widening in HY OAS, which would close the low-cost financing window these small-cap biotechs need to fund commercialization off the back of today's approvals.

From here, the setup is a slow sorting process rather than a sector-wide re-rating. The catalyst that would change the picture is a sustained move down in the 2-year and SOFR, which sits flat at 3.62%, since that would ease refinancing pressure on the distressed cohort faster than any further HY spread tightening would.
