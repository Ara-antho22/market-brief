Morning Edition

Friday, September 11, 2026

Newsletter Summary

Equities sold off across the board, with the S&P 500 down 0.58% to 7,591.70 and the Nasdaq off 0.65%, as the VIX spiked 8.38% to 17.84 and the curve repriced hawkish after August CPI printed 3.4% year over year. The move was compounded by WTI jumping 3.24% to 97.26 on Middle East escalation, a combination that puts the Fed in a genuine bind heading into its decision. UST10 rose to 4.83% and UST2 to 4.43% as markets priced roughly 90% odds of a hike rather than a cut, an unusual repricing given the growth backdrop. Curiously, credit did not follow equity vol higher: HY OAS tightened 0.37% to 2.70 and IG OAS tightened 1.23% to 0.80, even as the Nikkei fell 1.93% overnight. Gold added 0.95% to 400.12, a modest bid for havens that sits oddly alongside tightening spreads. For a leveraged finance seat, the story this morning is less about the equity drawdown and more about the base rate resetting higher while spread compensation stays cheap, a combination that raises all-in cost of capital for new issuance even as investors show no incremental credit concern yet.

Macro Update

August CPI at 3.4% Pushes Fed Hike Odds to 90%

August CPI came in at 3.4% year over year with the index up 0.40% month over month, hotter than the disinflation path the Fed needs to justify easing. Yahoo Finance and Reuters both flagged that futures now price roughly 90% odds of a hike rather than a cut at the upcoming decision, and CNBC framed this as a direct test of Kevin Warsh's credibility. The front end moved immediately, with UST2 up 0.91% to 4.43%. This is the single data point driving today's cross-asset repricing.

Read more

The August CPI report showed the index at 334.13, up 0.40% on the month, translating to a 3.35% to 3.4% annual rate depending on the source, above the pace needed to validate rate cuts. Fed funds futures responded by pricing close to 90% odds of a hike, a sharp shift documented by Yahoo Finance and Reuters, with CNBC noting the print arrives as a direct test of new Fed leadership credibility under Kevin Warsh. UST2 rose 0.91% to 4.43% and UST10 rose 0.63% to 4.83%, both consistent with a hawkish repricing rather than a recession-driven bid for duration.

The mechanism here is straightforward front-end repricing: when core inflation surprises to the upside, the market pulls forward the expected path of policy rates, which lifts short-dated yields disproportionately and compresses the term premium the Fed would otherwise need to pay down. That is why UST2 moved more than UST10 in percentage terms, and why equity multiples compressed across the S&P, Nasdaq, and Dow simultaneously, since a higher discount rate hits duration-sensitive growth names hardest.

For financing markets, the immediate read is that floating rate paper, including leveraged loans and CLO tranches, reprices higher in carry terms as SOFR expectations shift, even though spot SOFR printed 3.62% this morning. Notably, HY OAS tightened to 2.70 and IG OAS tightened to 0.80 despite the rate move, meaning the all-in cost of new issuance is rising through the base rate channel rather than through spread widening, a distinction that matters for anyone modeling new LBO paper or refinancing walls over the next two quarters.

The next confirming data point is the Fed's actual decision and accompanying statement language, since a hike delivered against a 90% priced market would be unsurprising, but any dovish hedge in the statement despite the hot print would signal the committee is more worried about growth than the CPI number suggests. Watch Warsh's press conference tone specifically, since CNBC's framing suggests his institutional credibility is now tied to how convincingly he explains any divergence between the data and the committee's action.

Sources: Yahoo Finance · CNBC · Reuters

Middle East Escalation Sends Oil and Tanker Rates Higher, Feeds the CPI Print

WTI jumped 3.24% to 97.26 as the Houthis reached a strategic island at the mouth of a vital shipping lane, per Reuters, and oil tanker rates hit record highs following Iran and US shipping attacks. AP and the Guardian both tied elevated August fuel prices directly to the CPI surprise, meaning the energy shock and the inflation shock are the same story. This matters because it links a geopolitical event directly to the Fed's rate path.

Read more

WTI rose 3.24% to 97.26 overnight, and Reuters reported that Yemen's Houthis reached a strategic island position at the mouth of a vital shipping lane, while tanker rates hit record highs following what Reuters described as Iran and US shipping attacks. AP News and the Guardian both connected this directly to the August CPI print, noting that Middle East clashes lifted fuel prices to painful levels for US consumers and that the Iran conflict specifically raised energy costs embedded in the inflation data.

The transmission mechanism is direct rather than abstract: energy costs flow into CPI with a short lag through gasoline and transport components, and the Boston Herald and NBC10 coverage both flagged gas price spikes as the proximate driver of the August acceleration. That is why the oil move and the CPI surprise are not two separate stories this morning but one, and why a hawkish Fed repricing is occurring alongside a supply-side shock rather than a demand-side one, a distinction that matters for how durable the inflation is likely to be.

For credit markets, energy-linked HY issuers, particularly E&P and oilfield services names, get a near-term tailwind from higher realized prices, while transport, airline, and consumer discretionary credits absorb margin pressure from higher input costs. QatarEnergy's move to lock in US LNG deals through 2031 signals that even non-US buyers are underwriting a multi-year higher energy cost regime rather than treating this as transitory, which is relevant context for anyone pricing long-dated energy infrastructure paper or midstream credit.

The next things to watch are whether the Houthi position at the shipping lane escalates into an actual disruption of flows, which would be a second, larger leg up in oil rather than a plateau, and whether tanker rate spikes persist into the next EIA weekly data. A stabilization in tanker rates and no further shipping lane incidents would support a read that this is a supply-shock spike that fades, while continued escalation would argue the energy component of CPI stays elevated into the next print.

Sources: Reuters · AP News · The Guardian

Yields Rise, Vol Spikes, But Spreads Refuse to Widen

UST10 rose to 4.83% and UST2 to 4.43% as a global bond selloff extended, with the Nikkei down 1.93% overnight, while Goldman Sachs and TD Economics both argued yields stay elevated for structural reasons tied to global debt supply. Yet HY OAS tightened to 2.70 and IG OAS tightened to 0.80 even as the VIX jumped 8.38% to 17.84, a divergence between rates vol and credit spreads that leveraged finance desks should not ignore. JPMorgan's research flagged AI bond issuance at $266 billion, suggesting a specific source of demand absorbing supply.

Read more

UST10 climbed to 4.83%, up 0.63%, and UST2 rose to 4.43%, up 0.91%, part of what commentary from Goldman Sachs, TD Economics, and Fidelity all characterized as a global bond selloff with structural rather than cyclical roots, including the Robin Brooks Substack piece describing a broader global debt shock and the El País piece on the $8 trillion in US debt held abroad. The Nikkei fell 1.93% overnight, the sharpest single-market move in the dataset, while the VIX rose 8.38% to 17.84, both consistent with a genuine repricing rather than a one-day wobble.

What makes this notable for a credit seat is that HY OAS tightened 0.37% to 2.70 and IG OAS tightened 1.23% to 0.80 in the same session, meaning spread compensation compressed even as the risk-free rate and equity volatility both rose. Mechanically, that suggests the selloff is being read by credit investors as a rates story rather than a corporate fundamentals story, so far, with demand for HY and IG paper staying intact even as duration cost climbs, a pattern consistent with JPMorgan's research flagging $266 billion of AI-related bond issuance being absorbed without spread concession.

The second-order effect is that all-in yields for new leveraged issuance are rising through the base rate even without spread widening, which raises the cost of capital for LBO financing, refinancings, and dividend recaps regardless of how credit-friendly the market otherwise feels. CreditSights' coverage of hyperscaler capex and AI infrastructure credit, along with commentary on active CLO strategies from Reckoner Capital, both point to a bifurcated market where large, well-sponsored tech-adjacent credits absorb enormous issuance at tight spreads while smaller, weaker credits face a higher all-in hurdle purely from the rates side.

The key thing to watch is whether spreads hold if the rates selloff extends further, since a genuine credit repricing would show up as HY OAS and IG OAS widening in tandem with rising yields rather than tightening against them. Also watch primary market results for AI-linked and hyperscaler paper specifically, since a weak print there would be the first sign that the demand absorbing the $266 billion in issuance JPMorgan flagged is not unlimited.

Sector Update

Energy XLE +0.34%

Crude spikes as equities wobble

WTI jumped 3.24% to 97.26 on Mideast supply fears, with wire reports pointing to an 8% weekly gain and one outlet flagging a $108 print on the broader benchmark. XLE managed only a 0.34% gain to 65.15, badly lagging the move in crude as the S&P, Nasdaq and Dow all fell and the VIX jumped 8.38%. Treasury yields rose alongside oil, with the 10-year at 4.83% and the 2-year at 4.43%, a combination the NYT tied directly to the day's cross-asset wobble. Credit spreads did not follow equities into stress, with HY OAS tightening to 2.70 and IG OAS to 0.80.

Read more

The dominant story is a geopolitical supply shock rather than a demand story. Reuters-style wires via aa.com.tr describe Mideast attacks driving oil toward an 8% weekly gain, and NBC's report of a run to $108 signals the move has legs beyond a single session's WTI print. The NYT frames this explicitly as a cross-asset event, oil surging while stocks and bonds wobble together, which is unusual positioning since oil spikes tied to supply fear typically coincide with bond strength, not weakness. Instead the 10-year rose to 4.83% and the 2-year to 4.43%, suggesting the market is pricing inflation risk from higher energy costs alongside the supply disruption itself.

Dispersion inside the sector is doing a lot of work. Yahoo Finance highlights refiners as the standout, benefiting from wide crack spreads even as crude input costs rise, while Barron's points to dividend-paying majors as the preferred vehicle for investors chasing the move without taking commodity-price risk directly. Simplywall.st's list of names tracking oil and yields higher, plus the broader claim from Opening Bell Daily and inc.com that energy stocks are beating the S&P 500 and still look cheap, suggests the bull case is now consensus among sector commentators. Yet XLE's 0.34% gain against a 3.24% move in WTI shows the ETF, weighted toward majors and diversified operators, is not fully capturing the upside that refiners and pure-play E&Ps are seeing.

The financing picture is the most interesting divergence in the data. HY OAS tightened 0.37% to 2.70 and IG OAS tightened 1.23% to 0.80 even as the VIX jumped 8.38%, meaning credit investors are not yet treating this as a risk-off event for energy issuers. Higher realized crude prices directly improve free cash flow coverage and reserve-based lending redetermination math for E&P and oilfield services credits, which supports refinancing at current spreads rather than forcing wider new-issue concessions. The offset is on the rates side: a 10-year at 4.83% and 2-year at 4.43% raises the all-in cost of any new long-dated debt or project financing for capex-heavy midstream buildouts, even as SOFR ticked down to 3.62%, which eases the floating-rate burden on existing leveraged loans.

The setup from here hinges on whether the Mideast supply disruption extends or resolves. An 8% weekly gain driven by attacks rather than OPEC+ policy is inherently reversible, and a de-escalation would unwind both the crude premium and the yield backup that accompanied it, pressuring the refiners and dividend names that have led the sector higher. Conversely, continued escalation would keep credit spreads a key tell, since HY and IG OAS holding near 2.70 and 0.80 respectively despite an 8.38% VIX spike is not typical late-cycle behavior, and a reversal in that tightening would be the first sign that energy credit is starting to price the same risk equities are.

Technology XLK +1.45%

AI capex diverges from the tape

XLK rose 1.45% while the S&P and Nasdaq both fell more than half a percent, with Oracle's earnings the catalyst. Oracle's AI cloud revenue surged 121% and shares rebounded after hours even as capex hit $28.5 billion, because customers are increasingly paying up front. MRVL, MSFT, and HPE headlines all center on the same question of how long the AI infrastructure buildout can run and who ends up financing it. Tech is trading as its own asset class again, decoupled from a broader session where oil jumped 3.24% and the VIX spiked 8.38%.

Read more

Oracle is the story. AI cloud revenue growth of 121% and an after-hours rebound despite $28.5 billion in capex tell you the market is now pricing the revenue conversion, not just the spend. The more consequential detail is that Oracle is keeping a lid on its own capex because customers are paying up front, which shifts working capital risk off Oracle's balance sheet and onto the buyers of committed capacity. MRVL, MSFT, and HPE headlines are all variations on the same theme: is the capex cycle still accretive at these levels, or is the market starting to demand proof of payback before it extends further credit to the buildout.

The dispersion inside tech is about who is financing the capacity. Oracle's prepaid-contract model looks structurally different from Microsoft and HPE, where the capex is still being funded on the vendor's own balance sheet ahead of revenue recognition, which is exactly why simplywall.st is asking whether MSFT is fully valued against continued AI investment. Marvell rides the wave as a supplier rather than an operator, meaning its economics depend on custom silicon demand holding up regardless of who ultimately carries the capacity risk. That distinction, upfront-paid infrastructure versus self-funded infrastructure, is becoming the line investors use to separate durable AI capex exposure from capex that still needs to prove out.

Credit conditions are unusually accommodating for this to keep going. HY OAS tightened to 2.70 and IG OAS to 0.80, both moving tighter on the day, even as the 10-year rose to 4.83% and the 2-year to 4.43%. That combination, wider risk-free rates but tighter spreads, means the market is pricing tech and capex-linked credit as lower risk in relative terms, which keeps the cost of funding data center buildouts contained despite the back-up in rates. SOFR easing to 3.62 also helps anyone financing capex with floating-rate facilities. Oracle's shift toward customer prepayment is itself a capital structure decision, since it reduces the incremental debt needed to fund the next leg of buildout and preserves balance sheet capacity for M&A or buybacks rather than pure capex.

The setup from here hinges on whether prepayment economics like Oracle's become the template or the exception. If MSFT and HPE commentary in the coming weeks shows capex still outrunning monetized backlog, the market will start discounting self-funded AI infrastructure spend the way it is currently rewarding Oracle's prepaid model. The VIX rising 8.38% to 17.84 alongside a 3.24% jump in WTI is a reminder that the macro backdrop is getting less forgiving, so tech's ability to keep trading apart from the broader tape depends on capex-to-revenue conversion staying visible in the next round of guidance.

XLV's flat print masks a session driven entirely by idiosyncratic FDA news rather than sector-wide repricing. The FDA expanded approval of Bayer's Hyrnuo in HER2-mutated lung cancer, a first-line label win reported separately by STAT and roi-nj.com. Working against that, the agency delayed its approval decision on Exelixis' tyrosine kinase inhibitor in colorectal cancer, pushing out a catalyst the stock had been trading toward. Separately, FDA advisers narrowly voted to add six peptides to the drug compounding list, a regulatory move with direct implications for outsourcing facilities and specialty pharmacy supply chains tied to peptide-based therapies.

The dispersion runs along catalyst lines rather than subsector lines. Exelixis loses time value on its colorectal cancer opportunity, extending the window before that revenue can offset R&D spend. Ionis was flagged by simplywall.st as fully priced following its rare disease approval, a reminder that single-approval biotech re-ratings get absorbed by the market almost immediately once the news is out, leaving limited follow-through unless pipeline optionality gets repriced separately.

The offset is the rate backdrop: UST10 at 4.83% and UST2 at 4.43% keep all-in yields elevated even as spreads compress, so the benefit to total coupon cost is partial. SOFR easing to 3.62% is a modest tailwind for floating-rate leveraged loan borrowers in the sector, including hospital operators and pharmacy roll-ups carrying term loan B structures.

The near-term setup stays defensive as long as broader risk-off persists, with oil up 3.24% and gold up 0.95% both pointing to a safety and inflation bid that typically keeps money in health care as a low-beta parking spot. The catalysts to watch are name-specific: a rescheduled PDUFA-equivalent timeline for Exelixis, finalization of the compounding peptide list and its effect on 503B facility economics, and whether Ionis holds its post-approval level or fades on the fully-priced thesis. None of these are sector-moving on their own, but they will keep dispersion inside XLV wider than the index-level flat print implies.

The dominant thread in the property press is the uneven office recovery. Phoenix is being cited as an outperformer within the West region, with Colliers' September data and AZ Big Media both pointing to falling vacancy there, part of a broader 'office comeback' framing echoed in trade coverage. That stands in contrast to a vacant, landmarked office building in Manhattan's Financial District trading for just $13.5 million, a Crain's New York item that reads as a data point on how far basis has reset for older, unleased CBD office stock in the country's largest gateway market. The dispersion is geographic and asset-quality driven rather than sector-wide. That combination, tighter spreads alongside a steepening curve, is constructive for REIT refinancing and for selective new development like the Winter Park groundbreak, even as it does nothing to fix the underwriting problem for older CBD office, where deals are still clearing at prices like the $13.5 million FiDi sale rather than being refinanced at prior valuations.

The setup from here is whether the rate move higher persists and starts to bite through the cap rate channel despite tight credit spreads, or whether today's XLRE outperformance was simply a rotation into perceived safety amid the VIX spike. The clearer near-term catalyst is regional vacancy data: continued improvement in West Coast and Sunbelt office markets like Phoenix would validate the recovery thesis embedded in today's move, while another distressed gateway office sale comp at a level near or below the FiDi print would reinforce that CBD office financing and valuation resets are still incomplete.
