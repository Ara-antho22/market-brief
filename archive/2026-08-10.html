Macro Update

Warsh's Fed and the credibility question rattling rates

Kevin Warsh's handling of the latest rate decision is being described as a credibility shock on Wall Street, compounding a fight over Fed independence as Trump renews his push to oust governor Lisa Cook. The 10-year rose 1.30% to 4.69% and the 2-year rose 1.67% to 4.25% in a steepening move that reads as a term-premium reaction rather than a growth signal. This matters because a Fed whose reaction function is in question gets priced with a wider band of outcomes, and that band shows up directly in duration and credit spread volatility.

Read more

Axios and the Motley Fool both flagged the same dynamic from different angles this week: Warsh is being characterized as rewiring the Fed's approach, and his latest rate decision sparked what one outlet called a credibility shock among investors. Simultaneously, Trump is renewing pressure to remove Fed governor Lisa Cook, adding a second and separate challenge to the institution's independence narrative at the same time the market is trying to price where policy goes next.

The transmission mechanism here runs through term premium, not the policy rate itself. When investors are uncertain about who is setting rates and why, they demand more compensation for holding longer-dated paper, which is consistent with the 2s10s move this week even as SOFR eased 0.82% to 3.62%, a sign the front end still expects easing. That combination, front-end cuts priced alongside back-end steepening, is a classic credibility discount showing up in the curve rather than in the fed funds path.

For leveraged finance specifically, this cuts two ways. Wider term premium raises the all-in cost of fixed-rate new issue even if base rates fall, and it can widen the gap between where borrowers want to price deals and where real money accounts want to buy them. Investment grade spreads are flat at 0.78 and HY OAS actually tightened to 2.71, so credit has not yet repriced the Fed uncertainty, but that gap between rates volatility and spread complacency is exactly the kind of dislocation that widens fast if it turns.

Watch whether the Cook ouster push escalates into an actual legal or personnel change, and watch whether Warsh's next public rate commentary reinforces or walks back the credibility concern. A quiet next two weeks from the Fed would let the curve normalize; a second surprise would likely show up first in swap spreads and IG new issue concessions before it hits HY.

Bad jobs, good tape: payrolls fall 23,000 and spreads tighten anyway

July payrolls fell by 23,000 and the S&P still hit a record, with Fed rate hike odds falling to 33% on the print. Unemployment dropped 2.38% to 4.10%, an odd combination that markets are treating as confirmation the Fed has room to cut rather than evidence of a deteriorating labor market. HY OAS tightened 1.45% to 2.71 and HYG added 0.19%, showing credit is fully on board with the bad-news-is-good-news read.

Read more

The July report showed weak job growth and slowing wages according to CounterPunch's breakdown, and Kalshi's market-implied probability of a Fed rate hike fell to 33% in direct response. The apparent contradiction, a 23,000 payroll decline alongside a falling unemployment rate, points to labor force dynamics rather than a straightforward hiring collapse, which is why equities read it as a green light instead of a warning.

The mechanism is a rate-cut repricing that lowers the discount rate applied to future cash flows, which lifts multiples across risk assets simultaneously. That is why the Nasdaq outperformed at 1.30% versus the Dow's 0.28%, since longer-duration growth names benefit more from a lower discount rate than value-heavy industrials. The same discounting logic explains why HY spreads tightened rather than widened on a weak jobs print, since lower base rates reduce refinancing risk for floating-rate and near-term maturity issuers even if revenue growth is softer.

For leveraged finance the second-order effect is on primary issuance. A market pricing cuts and tightening HY OAS to 2.71 is a market where sponsors want to bring deals now rather than wait, and where floating-rate direct lending vehicles like the ones reporting Q2 commentary this week face compressing spread income as base rates roll over. The private credit stress test narrative sits uneasily next to a 33% hike probability and a tightening OAS, since easier policy helps borrower coverage ratios but also compresses the yield premium that private credit funds have been marketing to LPs.

The next print that either confirms or breaks this is CPI, due this week per Yardeni's economic calendar. A soft CPI number alongside the weak payrolls would cement a September cut narrative and likely push HY OAS tighter still; a hot CPI would force the market to reconcile a falling hike probability with sticky inflation, and that reconciliation is where spread volatility would show up first.

Oil craters on Hormuz reopening bets while gold buys the hedge

WTI dropped 4.87% to 81.96 on chatter that Hormuz shipping lanes could reopen tied to US concessions to Iran, while gold rose 2.26% to 398.47 in an inverse move that signals investors are not fully trusting the de-escalation. Reuters reported Iran is tying Hormuz reopening to several specific US demands, meaning the supply relief priced into crude is conditional rather than confirmed. This matters because energy disinflation is now a live input into the CPI print investors are waiting on this week.

Read more

Reuters reported that Iran has tied any Hormuz reopening to US concessions on multiple demands, while separately noting Iran sees no reason to fear the Pakistan-Turkey-Saudi security pact, both suggesting the geopolitical premium in oil is being negotiated down rather than eliminated. WTI's 4.87% drop to 81.96 reflects the market pricing a real probability of restored flow through the strait, but the conditionality in Iran's position means the move could reverse quickly if talks stall.

The mechanism connecting this to broader markets is straightforward: oil is a direct input to headline CPI, and a sustained drop in crude lowers near-term inflation prints even if core services inflation stays sticky. That is precisely why this matters ahead of this week's CPI release, since a lower energy component gives the Fed more room to validate the rate-cut path that Fed hike odds falling to 33% already reflects. Gold's rise to 398.47 despite the same news flow suggests some allocators are hedging the tail risk that Hormuz talks fail and oil snaps back, which would also be inflationary and complicate the cut narrative.

The financing angle sits in energy credit and in inflation-linked issuance. Falling oil prices ease cost pressure for high-yield issuers in transportation and industrials, which helps explain part of the tightening in HY OAS to 2.71 this week, but any reversal in crude would hit energy-sector HY paper first and could reintroduce basis risk for issuers who hedged production at higher price assumptions. Sovereign and consumer inflation-sensitive credits benefit most directly from sustained energy disinflation.

The variable to watch is whether Iran's specific demands get met or rejected in the coming days, since Reuters coverage suggests this is an active negotiation rather than a settled outcome. A confirmed reopening would likely extend oil's decline and reinforce the disinflation trade into CPI; a breakdown in talks would spike oil back up and could force gold's current premium to look prescient rather than excessive.

That reversal decoupled energy from the risk-on mood elsewhere, where the VIX fell to 15.15 and credit spreads tightened. Meanwhile Exxon's record profits and BP's reported $5.7 billion windfall kept a bid under select major-cap names.

Read more

The session's defining move was a full round-trip in crude. Headlines out of the NYT and wire services flagged oil climbing on the Strait of Hormuz stalemate, a geopolitical risk premium tied to the chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of global seaborne oil. But the settlement data tells a different story: WTI closed at 81.96, down 4.87% on the day. That gap between the narrative and the print suggests the market priced in disruption risk early, then faded it as no actual flow interruption materialized, a classic risk-premium unwind rather than a demand-side repricing.

Dispersion inside the sector is doing the real work. Exxon's record quarterly profits and BP's disclosed $5.7 billion windfall are pulling retail and long-only interest toward the integrated majors, whose downstream and trading arms cushion them against a single-session crude drawdown. Pure-play E&Ps with direct spot exposure have no such offset, and that is almost certainly what dragged XLE down 1.13% while the majors' equity stories stayed intact. The Venezuela headline on international oil companies' operating status adds a second, unrelated risk factor for any names with legacy exposure there, layering political risk on top of the commodity swing.

Credit is not confirming the crude move, which matters more than the equity tape here. HY OAS tightened 1.45% to 2.71 and HYG ticked up 0.19%, while IG OAS held flat at 0.78. If the market believed this WTI drop reflected a genuine demand or oversupply shock, energy-heavy high yield paper would be repricing wider alongside it. Instead spreads are behaving as if this was a one-day unwind of a geopolitical premium, which keeps refinancing windows open for leveraged E&P issuers and preserves the majors' cost-of-capital advantage for buybacks and bolt-on M&A, the kind of capacity BP's windfall and Exxon's profit base are built to fund.

The next move hinges on whether the Hormuz stalemate stays a stalemate. A confirmed easing would let WTI retest lower without a credit response, favoring the majors' relative outperformance to continue. Any actual disruption to transit, or a hard turn in the Venezuela IOC situation, would force both the crude curve and E&P credit spreads to reprice in the same direction for the first time this cycle, which is the signal to watch rather than the daily XLE print.

Watch whether the vacancy tax concept spreads beyond Evanston to other municipalities grappling with the same downtown occupancy problem, since that would directly raise the cost of holding empty office space and could accelerate distressed sales or conversions. Also watch the German commercial data for whether the dip reported by Reuters extends into a longer stretch, which would confirm the recovery there was more sentiment than fundamentals.
