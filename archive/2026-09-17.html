Macro Update

Warsh's First Hike Steepens the Curve, Doesn't Tighten Funding

The Fed raised rates for the first time in three years under Warsh and signaled one more hike this year, defying Trump's calls to cut. UST10 rose to 5.00% and the dollar index ticked up to 118.21, but SOFR actually eased to 3.62%, a steepening move rather than a broad tightening. That split matters for anyone pricing floating versus fixed exposure this week.

Read more

The Fed's hike, described as Warsh's first major step to contain inflation, came with guidance for at least one more increase this year. The reaction across the curve was uneven: UST10 climbed to 5.00% (+0.60%) and UST2 to 4.67% (+0.43%), while SOFR fell 0.55% to 3.62% and the dollar index rose modestly to 118.21.

The mechanism here is a term-premium story, not a policy-rate story. A hike that comes with explicit inflation-fighting credibility from a new chair repriced long-duration paper more than it repriced near-term funding costs, because the market is treating this as a front-loaded move toward a terminal rate rather than a sustained hiking cycle. That is why SOFR eased even as the ten-year sold off.

For leveraged issuers, the split cuts both ways. Floating-rate liabilities benefit from the softer SOFR print, but any new fixed-rate issuance now prices off a 5% ten-year, raising all-in coupons for term-out deals even as spreads compress. The CLO market's continued formation, including MetLife's 45th vintage priced at $450 million, suggests demand is still absorbing the higher base rate without demanding materially wider spread compensation.

Watch whether the next inflation print validates the one-more-hike signal or whether Warsh softens language at the next opportunity. A further UST10 move toward the levels flagged in the 26-year comparison would confirm the steepening is durable rather than a single-session repricing, and that would start to bite on new-issue economics for BB and single-B credits specifically.

Hormuz Transit Collapse Sends Crude Through $107

WTI jumped 4.49% to $107.02 as ship transits through the Strait of Hormuz fell to just three on Wednesday, a sign the Iran conflict is now constraining physical flow rather than just headline risk. Gold added 2.09% as a hedge. The timing is unhelpful for a Fed that just hiked on inflation grounds.

Read more

Reuters data showed vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz dropping to three on Wednesday, coinciding with a 4.49% jump in WTI to $107.02 and a wave of new supertanker orders tied directly to the conflict. Gold rose 2.09% to 399.93 alongside the move, consistent with a safe-haven bid rather than a growth story.

The transmission here runs straight through headline inflation. CPI is already running at 3.35% year over year with the index up 0.40% month over month, and a sustained energy shock feeds directly into both the next CPI print and freight and insurance costs across shipping-dependent supply chains, exactly the kind of input-cost pressure that undercuts a central bank trying to declare victory on inflation the same week it hikes.

For credit, the split is sectoral. Energy and shipping-adjacent issuers see a tailwind to cash flow and coverage ratios, while transportation, chemicals, and consumer-facing high yield names face margin compression if the move holds. A durable oil shock also reinforces the curve steepening already underway, since it raises the odds the Fed leans hawkish again rather than pausing, which keeps long-end financing costs elevated for anyone terming out debt.

The number to track daily is Hormuz transit volume itself, alongside any diplomatic movement following reports that China is pressing Iran to rein in the Houthis. A rebound in transits and a fade in the tanker order wave would suggest this is a risk-premium spike rather than a supply event; a further drop toward zero transits would move this from a market story to a shortage story.

Sources: Reuters · Reuters

Spreads Tighten Into the Hike, Not With It

HY OAS tightened 2.17% to 2.70 and IG OAS fell 2.50% to 0.78 the same session equities sold off and the Fed hiked, with HYG up 0.37%. CLO issuance kept moving, including MetLife's 45th vintage at $450 million. Credit is decoupling from the equity vol print, which is the signal worth watching from a leveraged finance seat.

Read more

Spreads compressed on the day the VIX rose 2.97% to 17.71 and the Dow fell 1.21%, an unusual split. HY OAS closed at 2.70, down 2.17%, and IG OAS at 0.78, down 2.50%, while HYG gained 0.37%. Separately, MetLife closed its 45th CLO vintage, Galaxy 38, at $450 million, and coverage of European leveraged loans flagged early green shoots in that market.

The mechanism is a technical one: strong structural demand from CLO formation and retail credit fund inflows is absorbing the back-up in risk-free rates faster than equity investors are absorbing the hawkish hike and oil shock. That decouples credit spread direction from equity volatility in the near term, because the marginal buyer of a leveraged loan or high yield bond is pricing base-rate risk differently than an equity investor pricing discount-rate risk.

The practical read for the desk is that primary windows remain open even after a surprise hike and a 5% ten-year, which is constructive for refinancing calendars. But a commentary flagging 'weakness at the edges' alongside broad spread tightening implies the compression is concentrated in higher-quality collateral; the forty-fifth CLO from a single manager also signals collateral scarcity and continued reach for yield rather than broad-based credit improvement across the quality spectrum.

Watch whether spread tightening holds if the oil shock persists into next week's CPI print, and whether single-B and CCC tiers participate in the rally or lag it. A widening in the lower-quality cohort while headline OAS stays tight would be the first sign that the compression is a technical distortion rather than a genuine credit-quality signal.

Diesel prices hitting $6.31 a gallon has Senate Majority Leader John Thune floating an export ban, a policy lever that would theoretically ease domestic pump prices by keeping supply onshore, but the Trump team is reportedly not convinced it would work given how integrated U.S. refiners are into export markets. Layered on top, Europe's gas storage levels are drawing scrutiny for both economic and political reasons heading into winter, adding a second demand-side pressure point on distillates and keeping the crude complex bid even as broader equities sold off, with the S&P down 0.45% and the Dow down 1.21% on the session.

The dispersion inside energy is the story: WTI up over 4% should be unambiguously good for E&P cash flow, and headline flow around oil-linked names, including cash-flow-focused U.S. producers and a 10%-yielding play in Vitesse Energy, reflects that upstream optimism. But XLE's flat print suggests the tape is discounting the diesel export ban as a real tail risk for refiners, whose crack-spread economics would compress if forced to redirect exported barrels domestically. That policy overhang is likely offsetting the upstream tailwind at the index level, even before Thune's proposal has any legislative traction.

The financing backdrop is unambiguously supportive regardless of the equity chop. HY OAS tightening 2.17% to 2.70 and IG OAS tightening 2.50% to 0.78 both signal credit investors are not pricing incremental sector stress, and HYG ticking up 0.37% confirms the bid in high yield paper broadly. For energy issuers, that means refinancing windows stay open and cost of capital stays low even with commodity volatility elevated, which matters most for the smaller-cap E&Ps and yield-focused names like Vitesse that depend on capital markets access to fund distributions. Higher realized WTI also mechanically improves free cash flow and deleveraging trajectories across upstream balance sheets, which should support continued buyback and dividend capacity into year-end.

The catalyst to watch is whether the diesel export ban moves from floated proposal to actual policy, since that would be the trigger repricing refiners independently of crude direction. European gas storage data into the winter heating season is the second variable, as continued drawdowns would keep distillate demand elevated globally and reinforce the bid under WTI regardless of what Washington does on exports. Until one of those resolves, expect the E&P versus refiner dispersion inside XLE to persist even if the index-level print looks unremarkable.
