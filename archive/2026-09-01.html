Macro Update

Warsh's hawkish debut repriced the front end, not just Fed-dated futures

Barclays now sees two more hikes this year after Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks, and the 2-year yield moved 3.33% to 4.34% on the read. The 10-year followed to 4.73%, its highest since January 2025. Trump's comment that Warsh will 'do what he has to do' added political noise without changing the market's own math. This is a genuine hawkish repricing, not a one-headline overreaction, because it shows up across the curve and in SOFR.

Read more

Warsh's speech at Jackson Hole was read by markets and by Barclays specifically as an endorsement of a September hike, with commentary describing him sounding hawkish even if he stopped short of pre-committing. The 2-year yield's 3.33% move to 4.34% is the tell: front-end paper is the most sensitive instrument to near-term Fed path changes, and a move of that magnitude in a single session reflects real repositioning, not noise. SOFR at 3.68, up 0.82%, confirms the same repricing is showing up in the reference rate that prices floating-rate leveraged loans directly.

The mechanism here is straightforward but consequential for anything priced off short rates. A hawkish Fed repricing raises the risk-free component of every discount rate simultaneously, compresses the present value of duration-sensitive assets, and for floating-rate credit it raises debt service costs in real time without any change to the underlying credit story. That is why the 2-year moved harder than the 10-year on a percentage basis, a flattening impulse consistent with the market pricing near-term tightening more than long-run growth or inflation persistence.

For leveraged issuers, a higher SOFR path lifts the base rate leg of term loan coupons immediately, tightening interest coverage for names already living close to covenant thresholds. The fact that HY OAS still tightened 1.14% to 2.60 the same session tells you technical demand for paper is outrunning the base-rate story for now, but that gap between rising all-in cost of capital and compressing spread cushion is exactly the kind of divergence that reverses fast if hiring or inflation data surprises hawkish again.

Watch the actual September FOMC decision against what is now priced, and watch whether Friday's jobs report, expected around 55,000 per Staffing Industry Analysts, is read as validating Warsh's labor market view per Bloomberg's framing. A soft print that still gets read hawkish because of wage or participation details would confirm this is now a durable repricing rather than a single-speech overreaction.

Sources: Reuters · Bloomberg.com

Oil-driven bond selloff pushed mortgage rates to a 15-month high

Ten-year yields hit their highest since January 2025 as Middle East tensions reignited and pushed oil prices up, per CNBC, even though WTI itself is down 2.83% to 83.90 in today's session. Mortgage rates surged to the highest since June 2025 on the same dynamic. Euro zone inflation rose above 3% to 3.3%, adding a second front to the global bond selloff. The combination has global yields moving in the same direction for the same reason: energy-driven inflation fear repricing duration everywhere at once.

Read more

CNBC reported the 10-year hitting its highest level since January 2025 as renewed Middle East conflict lifted oil prices, with the WSJ and Yahoo Finance both framing this as a global bond selloff tied directly to energy. Mortgage rates followed, surging to their highest since June 2025, undoing the disinflationary path that had been expected for 2026. Separately, euro zone inflation printed 3.3% in August per Reuters, Morningstar, and euronews, cementing ECB rate hike expectations on top of the Fed story, so this is not a US-only phenomenon.

The transmission is direct and mechanical: energy costs feed into headline CPI with a short lag, and bond markets price the second-round effect by demanding higher term premium and higher policy-rate compensation before inflation even shows up in the official print. That is why yields moved on the oil-driven Middle East headlines before any CPI revision, and why WTI can fall 2.83% today, per today's data, while the yield move from the prior days' rally persists. Duration assets and long-dated credit are the most exposed because their valuation math discounts cash flows over a horizon where the higher-rate assumption compounds.

For financing markets, this raises the base case cost of any new fixed-rate issuance and makes rate locks on pending LBO or refinancing paper more expensive to arrange, since dealers hedge duration risk at prevailing swap rates that have moved against issuers over the past week. Mortgage-adjacent credit and any consumer-facing ABS paper tied to housing turnover activity should also be watched, since CNBC's own reporting flags 2027 homebuying affordability is now expected to stay difficult rather than improve.

The next confirming or disconfirming data point is whether oil's intraday reversal today, down 2.83%, holds or whether Middle East tensions reassert themselves and push crude back toward the highs that drove this move. A sustained pullback in crude without a corresponding give-back in yields would suggest the bond market has moved past oil into a broader term-premium repricing tied to Fed policy and fiscal supply, which is a structurally different and stickier story.

Sources: CNBC · CNBC · Reuters

Spreads tighten into a higher-rate world as AI-linked issuance piles up

HY OAS tightened 1.14% to 2.60 and IG OAS held at 0.79 even as Treasury yields ripped higher, a divergence that reflects strong technical demand absorbing a wave of AI-adjacent debt issuance. The Business Times flagged the tension between rising bond yields and the influx of AI debt directly. Separate coverage of Thoma Bravo-backed software credits and a 2027 maturity wall, plus a piece on CLOs facing structural issues into 2027, points to where the stress would first show up if the technical bid fades.

Read more

Spread compression during a rate selloff is a specific signal: HY OAS falling 1.14% to 2.60 while the 10-year rose to 4.73% means investors are being paid less credit-risk premium even as the risk-free component of their total yield rises, which only makes sense if demand for carry is strong enough to absorb both the rate move and ongoing new issuance. The Business Times piece on weighing higher bond yields against an influx of AI debt names this dynamic explicitly, describing an issuance wave that the market is currently digesting without spread widening.

The mechanism linking these threads is that private equity-backed software credits, per the Substack primer on Thoma Bravo-related names, are terming out and amending maturities to buy time against AI-driven revenue pressure rather than repairing balance sheets outright, which the Financial Times separately describes as software companies paying a steep price to buy time. That behavior is a symptom of a market willing to extend and pretend as long as base rates and spreads together keep refinancing arithmetic workable, which is precisely the condition current spread tightness is masking.

If SOFR and the front end keep rising the way this week's Warsh-driven repricing suggests, the arithmetic on amend-and-extend deals gets harder even without any spread widening, because floating coupons on term loans reset directly off SOFR. The CLO piece flagging structural issues into 2027 is the vehicle-level version of the same story: CLO liabilities and reinvestment tests are sensitive to both underlying loan spread compression and rising base rates simultaneously, and a maturity wall arriving into a higher-for-longer rate regime is a materially worse outcome than the same wall arriving into a cutting cycle.

Watch whether HY OAS keeps compressing through the September FOMC decision or whether a confirmed hike triggers spread widening that would signal the technical bid finally cracking. Also watch primary calendar behavior in AI-adjacent and software credits specifically, since a failed or repriced deal there would be the first concrete sign that the maturity wall concern is moving from thesis to price action.

Sources: The Business Times · Financial Times · Asset Securitization Report

Equities decouple from falling crude

XLE rallied 2.04% even as WTI fell 2.83% to 83.90, a sharp divergence from the headline narrative of oil spiking on Middle East violence and U.S. strikes on Iranian targets in the Strait of Hormuz. Exxon, Chevron and SLB led the sector higher per wire coverage, while the crude tape itself gave back its geopolitical premium intraday. The Reuters and ABC threads tie the same headlines to a broader bond selloff and inflation scare, which shows up in the tape as UST10 up 1.28% and UST2 up 3.33%. Credit stayed calm through the noise, with HY OAS tightening 1.14% to 2.60.

Read more

The dominant story is a geopolitical shock that is showing up differently in the commodity and the equities that supposedly track it. Multiple wires (ABC News, LA Times, Dayton Daily News) report U.S. strikes on Iranian targets in the Strait of Hormuz and broader Middle East violence pushing oil prices higher, with Fortune noting gasoline topped $4 a day in August for the first time as a result. Reuters frames the same event as an inflation-fear trigger that deepened a bond selloff. But the session's actual print on WTI was a decline of 2.83% to 83.90, meaning whatever premium got built into the crude curve overnight or intraday was already unwinding by the close even as the equity complex kept bidding.

The dispersion is concentrated in the majors and services names the Motley Fool and Globe and Mail both flagged: ExxonMobil, Chevron and SLB climbed on the session. That combination of integrateds and an oilfield-services name suggests the equity bid is less about spot crude economics and more about positioning for sustained volatility and potential supply disruption risk in the Strait of Hormuz corridor, which favors diversified majors with trading and refining optionality plus service names tied to activity levels rather than pure-play E&Ps with direct WTI realized-price exposure. The simplywall.st piece on European energy names being lifted by the same inflation and rate dynamic points to a read-through beyond just US large caps.

For the credit side of this sector, the setup is currently benign despite the macro noise. HY OAS tightened 1.14% to 2.60 on the day, so the energy-heavy high-yield complex is not pricing distress even as rates back up (SOFR at 3.68, UST10 at 4.73) and the DXY firms to 118.75. That combination, cheaper credit spreads alongside rising base rates, keeps all-in borrowing costs for E&P and midstream issuers roughly stable rather than punitive, which supports continued capital discipline, buybacks, and opportunistic M&A rather than defensive balance sheet moves. The risk is asymmetric: if the Strait of Hormuz situation escalates and crude reprices sharply higher on actual supply loss rather than headline fear, revolver borrowing bases and hedge books get repriced fast, and that is the scenario financing desks should be watching, not today's tape.

The catalyst that changes this is simple: does WTI confirm the headline narrative or keep fading it. A session where crude actually holds or extends gains on confirmed supply disruption would validate the equity move and likely pull credit spreads tighter still on stronger cash flow visibility for producers. A continued gap between geopolitical headlines and a softening crude tape, as happened today, is the more fragile setup, since it implies equity investors are pricing tail risk that the commodity market is not yet confirming, and that gap typically closes through an equity correction rather than a further oil rally.
