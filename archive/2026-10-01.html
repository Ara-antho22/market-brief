Macro Update

10-Year Hits 5.26% as Curve Steepens and Spreads Widen Together

UST10 climbed to 5.26%, a level Reuters frames as a 24-year high, while UST2 fell to 4.89%, steepening the curve on fiscal concern rather than growth optimism. UK 30-year gilts crossed 6% for the first time since 1998, confirming this is a global sovereign repricing, not a US-only story. HY OAS and IG OAS both widened on the same tape, meaning borrowers are facing a higher base rate and a wider spread simultaneously. That combination is the one that actually moves underwriting math on new leveraged paper.

Read more

UST10 rose to 5.26% (+0.38% on the day) in a move Reuters and WSJ both describe as taking long-dated government debt back toward levels last seen at the turn of the century. UST2 moved the opposite direction, down 0.61% to 4.89%, so the curve is steepening. The same dynamic showed up abroad: UK 30-year gilt yields topped 6% for the first time since 1998, per Reuters, confirming this is a global, fiscally-driven move rather than an idiosyncratic US story.

The mechanism here is term premium, not policy rate expectations. Short rates falling while long rates rise tells you the market is not demanding more Fed tightening, it is demanding more compensation for holding duration against a backdrop of sovereign issuance and deficit concern. For anyone pricing a term loan or bond off a Treasury or SOFR benchmark, that term premium flows straight into the base rate component of all-in yield, independent of what happens to credit spreads.

The problem for financing conditions is that spreads did not sit still to absorb the base-rate move. HY OAS widened 1.99% to 3.08 and IG OAS widened 1.20% to 0.84 on the same day, meaning the cost of debt for both investment-grade and sub-investment-grade issuers rose from two directions at once. SOFR itself ticked up 0.52% to 3.90. That is the setup that raises interest coverage pressure on any floating-rate capital structure and makes new-issue leveraged loan and HY pricing meaingfully more expensive even before accounting for deal-specific risk.

Confirmation of this read would be HY OAS and IG OAS continuing to widen from current levels alongside UST10 holding above 5.25%, and weak demand at the next UK long-gilt auction. A break would be a reversal on softer incoming data, specifically a weak September jobs report reviving rate-cut bets at the front end, which would flatten the curve again and likely pull spreads back in. Watch the full jobs report and any Treasury refunding commentary for the next data point.

Sources: Reuters · Reuters · WSJ

AI-Linked Credit Tests the Risky End of the Market

Low-rated issuers have raised $88 billion in AI-linked debt as investors demand higher returns for exposure to revenue streams still unproven, per reporting cited alongside Reuters' account of a tough sell in risky corners of the credit market. Paramount is paying up to fund its Warner Bros deal, an FT-reported read-through on acquirer financing costs even outside pure data-center paper. This matters because it is showing up as a bifurcation inside HY rather than a broad market move.

Read more

Reuters reports that AI borrowers are facing a tough sell in the riskier corners of the US credit market, and separate reporting puts total AI-linked debt raised by low-rated firms at $88 billion, with investors demanding higher returns to take the exposure. The Economic Times frames this as a test for lenders given that future revenues backing much of this debt remain uncertain. Paramount, per the Financial Times, is paying elevated borrowing costs to fund its Warner Bros acquisition, a separate but related signal that acquirers financing AI-adjacent or content assets are seeing their cost of capital move.

The mechanism is a compensation premium for revenue uncertainty rather than a leverage or coverage problem in the traditional sense. Much of this debt is tied to data-center buildouts, power contracts, or compute commitments whose cash flows are forward-looking and contractually thinner than a seasoned issuer's, so investors are pricing in a bigger spread cushion for the chance those revenue assumptions do not hold. That premium shows up as wider new-issue concessions and higher coupons specifically in the low-rated, AI-linked bucket, even while the broader HY index level, now 3.08 OAS, moves for separate base-rate reasons.

The second-order effect is a bifurcation within HY and leveraged loans: paper tied to AI capex is pricing wide of otherwise comparable credits, while the market absorbs rising net supply from this theme on top of already-elevated base rates. That combination raises underwriting risk for anyone structuring deals in media, hyperscaler-adjacent, or data-center capital stacks this quarter, since both the benchmark and the required spread cushion are moving against issuers at the same time. Paramount's experience financing Warner Bros is a tangible data point that even larger, rated acquirers are not immune.

The thing to watch is whether the next wave of AI-linked issuance clears inside initial price talk or requires further concession widening, and whether that spread differential between AI-linked and non-AI-linked HY paper keeps growing even if headline HY OAS stabilizes. A break in this narrative would be deals clearing smoothly despite the revenue-uncertainty framing; continued widening specific to this cohort would confirm investors are actively discriminating within the index rather than treating it as a uniform asset class.

Powell Survives the Audit, Payrolls Complicate the Cut Case

The Fed's inspector general found renovation mismanagement but no grounds for a criminal referral, effectively clearing Powell of the headline risk Trump had tried to weaponize into a resignation call. Separately, ADP showed private payrolls up 90,000 in September, better than expected, and Kashkari said inflation is still too high even after softer PCE data. Together these push against an aggressive near-term cutting path, which matters directly for floating-rate interest expense.

Read more

Multiple outlets, including CNBC, Reuters, The Guardian, and Al Jazeera, confirm the Fed's internal watchdog found mismanagement in the headquarters renovation but no criminal wrongdoing, and Politico separately reports Powell is likely to stick around. Trump nonetheless renewed his call for Powell's resignation despite the clearance, per Reuters. On the data side, ADP reported private payrolls rose 90,000 in September, beating expectations, while Minneapolis Fed President Kashkari said inflation remains 'still too high' even after a softer-than-expected PCE print, describing the labor market as 'pretty good.'

The mechanism is that removing leadership uncertainty at the Fed, combined with a labor market that is not yet cracking and a policymaker publicly resisting the inflation-is-solved narrative, reduces the probability markets assign to near-term aggressive rate cuts. That shows up directly in the front end: SOFR ticked up 0.52% to 3.90 and UST2 actually fell, reflecting a market still working out how much cutting is left rather than pricing in urgency.

For leveraged structures, this is a direct read-through to interest coverage. Most leveraged loans price off SOFR plus a spread, so a 'higher for longer' base case embedded in the front-end curve keeps debt service costs elevated for existing floating-rate borrowers even if credit spreads themselves are stable. The institutional continuity at the Fed also removes one source of volatility premium that had been showing up in rate-path pricing, though the political noise around Trump's continued resignation calls keeps a tail risk alive that is hard to fully price out.

The next confirming data point is the full September jobs report, previewed by CEPR, which will either validate the ADP strength or reverse it. A break in the 'Powell stays, cuts stay gradual' read would be a weak payrolls print reviving aggressive cut bets, or renewed political pressure translating into an actual leadership change rather than just rhetoric. Watch SOFR and UST2 for the market's real-time verdict once that report lands.

Crude craters, equity barely blinks

The gap says equity investors are looking through the spot move, reading Dallas Fed commentary that executives expect oil to soften further by year-end as already priced. Credit spreads moved the other direction, with HY OAS up 1.99% and IG OAS up 1.2% on the day, a reminder that financing costs for the sector are not following crude lower in lockstep.

Read more

The crude tape did the damage today. FXEmpire's data points to Gulf export flows recovering alongside a build in U.S. crude stocks, which is the textbook bearish combination: more barrels available and more getting out the door to compete in an already long market. Oilprice.com's reporting on U.S. production climbing despite extreme price volatility compounds that picture, since supply growth is continuing even as producers get mixed price signals, and the Dallas Fed's survey of energy executives forecasting lower oil by year-end gives that supply growth an institutional voice rather than just a spot-price data point.

Equity dispersion is the story underneath the flat XLE print. Moomoo's energy roundup framed shares as slipping even while oil remained high, which was the setup heading into today, so the commodity's 3% drop arriving after that framing suggests the equity complex had already partially priced downside. Simply Wall Street's piece on Murphy Oil tracking higher oil prices reads stale against today's tape, and that lag is exactly why single names like Murphy or Magnolia Oil & Gas, still digesting its own Q2 print per StockStory, will show more day-to-day volatility than the diversified XLE basket, which is buffered by larger integrateds with downstream and trading operations less sensitive to a single day's WTI move.

The spread widening matters more than the index print for anyone underwriting energy credit. HY OAS up nearly 2% and IG OAS up over 1% on a day when crude fell 3% is the kind of move that raises the cost of incremental debt for E&P issuers right when the Dallas Fed survey is telling them to expect softer realizations into year-end. Production continuing to climb despite the price volatility, per oilprice.com, is good for volume-driven cash flow but means capital intensity isn't easing, so any issuer with near-term maturities is now refinancing into a slightly wider spread environment than a week ago. That combination, falling commodity price plus widening credit spreads, is the one that tests leverage tolerance at the margin for lower-rated E&P names rather than the investment-grade majors.

Watch whether the crude stock build and Gulf export recovery repeat in next week's EIA data, since a second consecutive build would validate the Dallas Fed executives' year-end call and likely pressure WTI toward fresh lows, which would start to show up in XLE rather than staying contained to the commodity. The offsetting catalyst would be any sign that U.S. production growth decelerates in response to softer prices, which would tighten the market faster than the survey respondents expect and relieve pressure on both crude and the credit spreads that widened today.

Capex guidance, not cloud growth, is the trade

XLK added 0.64% while the broader tape was mixed, with Nasdaq up slightly and the Dow down 0.86%. The move is being driven by positioning ahead of Google's open to Big Tech earnings, where investors are telling anyone who'll listen that capex trajectory, not cloud revenue growth, will decide the next leg of the AI trade. Credit moved modestly against risk, with HY OAS up 1.99% and IG OAS up 1.20% on the day, a reminder that the AI buildout is increasingly a credit story as much as an equity one.

Read more

Google's print opens Big Tech earnings season, and the framing across desks has shifted from whether cloud growth justifies the multiple to whether capex guidance holds or breaks. Hyperscaler capex has reportedly topped $780 billion according to a16z's Anjney Midha commentary relayed via Wang, and Bain is now framing the AI buildout as a $6 trillion funding problem that requires new financing structures beyond straight balance-sheet capex. That framing matters because it shifts the debate from unit economics to funding mechanics, which is exactly the lens a credit investor would apply.

Dispersion inside the sector is showing up around capex sensitivity rather than product category. The Benzinga piece arguing Nvidia could feel a capex crack before Meta does is the cleanest articulation of the dispersion thesis: Meta can absorb a spending pause because its core ad business still throws off cash, while Nvidia's model depends on hyperscalers continuing to place forward orders. Anthropic's disclosed $42 billion loss and AMD's acquisition of World Labs are both symptoms of the same dynamic, with capital being deployed aggressively into AI capability even as the underlying labs burn cash at a pace that has no precedent in software.

The financing angle is now impossible to separate from the equity story. Bain's point that new funding innovation is required to sustain the buildout implies the sector is approaching the limits of what can be funded from free cash flow and traditional corporate debt alone, which is why vendor financing, off-balance-sheet vehicles, and structured data-center financing are becoming part of the conversation. That shows up in today's print as modestly wider credit spreads, with HY OAS up almost 2% and IG OAS up over 1%, even as UST10 ticked higher to 5.26% and SOFR sits at 3.90%, a funding backdrop that makes any acceleration in debt-financed capex more expensive at the margin. Anthropic's loss disclosure is the kind of data point that credit investors will use to question whether AI lab cash burn is being adequately priced into the financing stack underwriting hyperscaler capex commitments.

The setup from here hinges entirely on what Google says about capex cadence this week, since that print is being treated as the first real test of whether hyperscalers hold the line on spending guidance or start hedging it. A soft capex signal from Google would validate the Nvidia-before-Meta thesis and could reprice semiconductor and data-center-adjacent credit simultaneously, given how intertwined equity sentiment and financing appetite have become in this buildout.

The setup from here hinges on whether AI-driven leasing demand broadens beyond a handful of gateway markets or stays concentrated enough that it cannot offset the volume of empty secondary office coming due for refinancing. Watch for incremental UST10 moves and further spread widening in IG and HY, since both raise the bar for conversion and distressed-acquisition economics discussed in the Montgomery County and Pioneer Square stories. A stabilization in HY OAS back toward recent lows would ease refinancing pressure on the weaker end of the office complex faster than any leasing data point could.

Consumer staples sold off harder than the broader tape as the rate complex moved against bond-proxy equities. The 10-year yield rose 38bps to 5.26% and SOFR ticked up to 3.90%, both of which raise the hurdle rate for a sector whose investment case rests on dividend yield and defensive cash flow rather than growth. XLP's -1.53% session stands out against a S&P down only a quarter point and even the Dow's -0.86%, suggesting staples bore a disproportionate share of the rate repricing. The Yahoo Finance and Simply Wall Street pieces both framing staples as dividend plays into 'rate pressure' and a 'rate hike market' capture the sector's core vulnerability right now: as risk-free rates climb, the relative appeal of a 3-4% equity dividend yield erodes, and multiples compress accordingly.

The dispersion inside the sector tracks pricing power. McCormick posted better-than-expected Q3 2026 sales, a signal that branded spice and flavor demand is holding up and that the company can pass through input costs. Coca-Cola and Keurig Dr Pepper drew a bargain call from a Fidelity fund manager, implying the market has already priced in enough rate pain that select large-cap beverage names look oversold relative to their cash generation. Hormel sits on the other side of that dispersion: the Seeking Alpha piece on its recent purchase argues the deal doesn't move the appetizing needle enough, a reminder that M&A in this tape needs to clear a higher bar on accretion given where financing costs now sit. Conagra's Q1 call and the Japan Today report on over 3,000 food and beverage items rising in October both point to persistent input cost inflation that packaged food companies are still working through, which is the structural backdrop behind the pricing-power dispersion.

The financing read-through is direct. HY OAS widened nearly 2% to 3.08 and IG OAS widened 1.2% to 0.84 in the same session, meaning the cost of debt capital for staples issuers, many of which carry investment-grade but BBB-tilted balance sheets built on steady cash flow, ticked higher alongside the equity selloff. That matters for M&A appetite in a sector that has historically used leverage to fund bolt-on acquisitions, exactly the dynamic playing out in the skepticism around Hormel's latest deal. With SOFR at 3.90% and the 10-year above 5%, floating-rate refinancing and new deal financing both get more expensive, which raises the bar for any staples issuer contemplating leveraged M&A or a dividend-funded buyback in the near term. Higher-rated balance sheet names like Coca-Cola and McCormick have more room to absorb this; more leveraged or acquisitive names do not.

The setup from here hinges on whether the rate move extends or stabilizes. CPI came in at 3.35% year-over-year with the index up 0.40% on the month, keeping inflation in a range that doesn't obviously force the Fed's hand but doesn't let it ease either, so staples remain a rate-sensitive trade until that data clarifies. The catalyst to watch is whether credit spreads continue widening from here, since a further move in HY OAS and IG OAS would compound the multiple compression already visible in today's XLP print, while a stabilization or retracement in the 10-year would be the fastest route back to the bond-proxy bid the sector depends on.
