Macro Update

Fed Holds Again Under Warsh, Bond Market Disagrees

The Fed left rates unchanged for a second consecutive meeting under Chair Kevin Warsh, with three officials dissenting, even as CPI inflation sits at a three-year high. Treasuries sold off anyway, with the 10-year up 0.21% to 4.68% and the 2-year up 0.24% to 4.23%, and St. Louis Fed's Musalem told the FT the selloff signals markets want the Fed to bolster its inflation credibility. The dissent count matters more than the hold itself: a unanimous pause is a policy statement, a three-way split is a preview of the next fight.

Read more

The Fed held its policy rate steady for the second straight meeting, the first pause since the Iran war spiked oil prices earlier in the cycle, with three officials dissenting under new Chair Kevin Warsh. That dissent came against a backdrop of CPI at 3.46% year over year, a three-year high, and a CPI index level of 332.57 that is down 0.42% on the month even as the annual rate stays elevated. SOFR ticked up 0.27% to 3.66%, and the front end held near current levels while the back end moved: 10-year yields rose to 4.68% and 2-year yields to 4.23%.

The mechanism here is a term-premium repricing, not a funding shock. When a central bank holds with visible internal disagreement, long-end investors demand more compensation for the risk that policy is behind the curve, which is exactly what Musalem flagged to the FT: the selloff is the market pricing in a credibility gap, not a liquidity problem. That is why the curve twisted rather than parallel-shifted, and why front-end SOFR barely moved while 10s and 2s both backed up.

For credit, the tell is that spreads did not follow yields higher. IG OAS tightened 1.23% to 0.80 and HY OAS tightened 1.05% to 2.84, meaning the higher discount rate is being absorbed without any repricing of default or downgrade risk, at least at the index level. That is constructive for near-term primary issuance and refinancing windows, but it raises the all-in cost of any new floating-rate or SOFR-linked leveraged loan paper priced off a base rate that just moved up alongside term yields.

The next test is the dissent count itself. If three becomes four or five at the following meeting, or if Musalem's FT comments are echoed by other regional presidents, the credibility question becomes the dominant narrative and long-end yields likely keep grinding higher regardless of what oil or payrolls do. Watch for follow-through commentary from other Fed officials and whether the 10-year holds above 4.68% into the next data print.

Falling energy costs are a direct disinflationary input, which is why equities rallied on the same day CPI data confirmed a three-year-high annual rate. Crude dropped 8.16% to $84.25 on reports that US-Iran peace talks are back on and hopes for a broader Mideast deal, according to Reuters and Cramer's Monday notes on CNBC. Equities responded immediately: the S&P rose 0.70% to 7,489.72, the Nasdaq gained 1.00% to 25,373.85, and the small-cap Russell led at 1.45% to 295.43. The VIX fell 6.44% to 15.99, and AP and orlandosentinel both framed the move explicitly as falling oil calming Wall Street's inflation worries, even as chip stocks continued to sink within the same session.

The mechanism is direct rather than sentiment-driven: energy is a first-order input to headline CPI, so an 8% single-day drop in crude mechanically lowers near-term inflation prints and reduces the odds the Fed needs to react to the 3.46% year-over-year reading with anything hawkish. That is what allows equity multiples to expand even as the 10-year yield rises for reasons unrelated to growth, and it is why the VIX, which prices tail risk more than average volatility, fell harder than the index gains would otherwise suggest: an Iran-war energy shock was the tail risk being repriced out.

In credit, the read-through is that HY OAS tightened 1.05% to 2.84 and HYG was roughly flat at 79.30, down just 0.23%, consistent with spread compression tracking the equity rally rather than a fear-driven bid for safety. Energy-linked HY issuers face near-term margin compression from cheaper crude, but that is more than offset in index terms by the broader de-risking, and it matters for the pipeline: better tone and tighter spreads support windows for opportunistic refinancing exactly when the AI-linked loan stress described below is worrying private credit desks.

Confirmation would be oil holding below $85 alongside actual progress on a Mideast deal rather than just talk headlines, and a VIX that stays under 16 into payrolls week. The break case is any reversal in the US-Iran talks, which given the size of Monday's move would likely snap crude back up violently and revive the inflation-scare trade that has been suppressed since the hold. Watch Cramer's daily commentary and Reuters wire for deal-specific headlines, not just sentiment framing.

Index-level credit metrics moved firmly tighter to start the week: HY OAS fell 1.05% to 2.84 and IG OAS fell 1.23% to 0.80, both consistent with the broader equity rally and falling VIX. Against that backdrop, Tech Times reported that CoreWeave-linked loan spreads blew out 125 basis points, and The Star's coverage described loan investors pushing back and demanding tighter covenant protection as debt market concerns rise around AI infrastructure financing specifically.

The mechanism is a bifurcation between systemic and idiosyncratic risk pricing. Index OAS reflects the average investor's view of macro default risk across hundreds of issuers, and that average is being pulled tighter by the oil-driven risk-on move and the absence of a broad funding shock. But single-name AI infrastructure credits carry concentrated exposure to hyperscaler capex cycles, GPU depreciation assumptions, and customer concentration risk that a general market rally does not address, which is why lenders are repricing spreads and renegotiating covenant packages on a name-by-name basis rather than waiting for the index to move.

For a leveraged finance seat, this is the more important story than the headline spread print. CLOs and direct lenders with exposure to AI data center and GPU-cloud financings are the ones absorbing the CoreWeave-style spread widening even as the broader HYG tape sits roughly flat at 79.30, down just 0.23%. The FT's framing of a broader second act for high-yield bonds captures the aggregate mood, but the covenant renegotiation activity described by The Star is the leading indicator for how underwriting standards on new AI-linked leveraged loans will look in the next quarter, tighter covenants, wider spreads, and more selective single-name exposure regardless of where the index sits.

Watch whether the CoreWeave spread move is contained to that name or spreads to comparable AI-infrastructure credits in the leveraged loan and private credit space. The next confirming or breaking data point is whether new-issue covenant packages on similar AI capex financings tighten in step with the CoreWeave repricing, or whether this settles as an isolated single-name event that the broader tightening in HY and IG OAS ultimately overrides.

The setup from here hinges on whether the Iran de-escalation holds.
