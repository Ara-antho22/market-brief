Macro Update

Warsh's First Meeting: Steady Rate, Hawkish Signal

The Fed held rates steady in Kevin Warsh's first decision as chair, but his rhetoric on inflation risk was hawkish enough that Barclays now calls for two more hikes this year. Both the 2-year and 10-year moved up in tandem, and SOFR ticked to 3.65%. That combination matters because it reopens a tightening path leveraged borrowers had stopped pricing.

Read more

The Fed kept rates unchanged this week in what outlets flagged as Kevin Warsh's first rate decision as chair, but his accompanying commentary flagged inflation risk and signaled that hikes might still be needed. Barclays responded by shifting its call to two more hikes this year, and bond investors were described as turning wary in the aftermath.

The mechanism here is straightforward repricing of the forward path: hawkish rhetoric from a new Fed chair carries more weight than usual because markets are still calibrating his reaction function, so both the 2-year (4.20%, +0.24%) and 10-year (4.67%, +0.21%) moved higher together rather than just the front end. SOFR rising to 3.65% (+0.27%) transmits immediately into floating-rate cost of debt for leveraged loans and CLO tranches that reset off it.

Spread compression is partially absorbing the higher base rate, HY OAS tightened 1.5% to 2.63 and IG OAS tightened 1.25% to 0.79, so all-in yields for higher-quality issuers may still look manageable even as reference rates climb. But floating-rate borrowers in the leveraged loan and CLO universe feel the SOFR increase directly and immediately, tightening the refinancing math for anything rolling toward the 2027 maturity wall.

The confirming or breaking data point is the September jobs report and the Fed's Beige Book language this week. If payrolls come in soft, Warsh's hawkish framing loses its labor-market underpinning; if Barclays' two-hike call gets picked up by other desks, that is the signal the repricing is becoming consensus rather than a one-speech move.

Multiple outlets tied the release directly to the September decision odds. That framing matters for anything priced off the front end of the curve.

Bloomberg, Markets.com and kalkine.ca all frame this week's payrolls release as the data point that either keeps a September hike alive or takes it off the table. Unemployment printed 4.10%, down 2.38% from the prior reading, a tightening signal that lines up with Warsh's more hawkish characterization of labor market conditions.

A resilient payroll print alongside falling unemployment removes the labor-market-cracking argument that would otherwise support cuts, giving the Fed room to lean hawkish. That read hits the front end of the curve hardest, which is consistent with the 2-year already up 0.24% to 4.20% ahead of the release, and the reaction to the actual number will determine whether the curve continues to flatten or reverses.

The combination of a tight labor market and CPI running 3.3% year over year is exactly the setup that keeps SOFR elevated for longer, and SOFR at 3.65% feeds directly into floating-rate leveraged loan resets and private credit BDC base-rate calculations. It also matters for the CLO maturity wall story, since 2027 refinancings will be priced off whatever curve this data helps establish.

The specific things to watch are the headline payroll number, any downward revision to prior months, and the Fed Beige Book's labor market commentary due this week. A soft print or negative revisions would puncture the hawkish narrative Warsh set up at his first meeting.

Spreads Tighten Into a Rising-Rate, AI-Bond Wall

HY OAS tightened 1.5% to 2.63 and IG OAS tightened 1.25% to 0.79 even as the 10-year and SOFR both rose, a combination that traces to heavy hyperscaler bond issuance absorbing demand rather than easier financing conditions broadly. Fortune reports AI hyperscalers are issuing a flood of bonds that is reverse crowding out Treasuries. The stress this masks shows up instead in CLOs and sponsor-backed software credit.

Read more

Both credit indices tightened on the session, HY OAS to 2.63 and IG OAS to 0.79, even though the 10-year rose to 4.67% and SOFR to 3.65%. Fortune's reporting describes AI hyperscalers issuing a flood of investment-grade bonds that is reverse crowding out the Treasury, meaning corporate supply is now competing with, and in demand terms outcompeting, government issuance.

The mechanism is a demand-absorption story rather than an easing of financing conditions. Investors are rotating into high-quality corporate paper because AI-linked issuers screen as strong credits with a yield pickup over Treasuries, and that flow compresses spreads even as risk-free rates climb, keeping all-in yields for the top of the credit stack roughly stable while lower-quality floating-rate borrowers still absorb the SOFR increase directly.

The composite OAS tightening doesn't capture where the actual stress is building. Asset Securitization Report's piece on CLOs facing an array of issues heading into 2027, and the Substack primer on Thoma Bravo-backed software credits, both point to middle-market and sponsor-backed loan books maturing into a higher-for-longer SOFR environment, a mismatch invisible in indices dominated by large-cap issuers including the hyperscalers soaking up bond demand.

The tell to watch is whether HY OAS decouples from IG OAS if the Fed actually delivers a hike. ABF Journal's framing of September as a coin-flip hike is the right lens: a hike would hit floating-rate, sponsor-backed credit first, and if HY spreads widen faster than IG in response, that is confirmation the compression seen today was concentrated in large-cap and hyperscaler paper rather than credit broadly.
