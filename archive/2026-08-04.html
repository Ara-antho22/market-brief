Macro Update

Fed Holds at a Three-Year Inflation High, Curve Twists

The Fed left rates unchanged even as CPI printed 3.46% year over year, a three-year high, and Philadelphia Fed's Paulson said she is keeping an 'open mind' on the path from here. The 10-year rose to 4.75% and the 2-year to 4.28%, both up on the day, while SOFR eased to 3.65%. The move matters because it is repricing term premium at the long end while easing front-end funding costs, a genuine curve twist rather than a uniform shift.

Read more

The Fed's hold came against a CPI print of 3.46% year over year, described by ABC News as a three-year high, with unemployment ticking down to 4.20%, a 2.33% drop that keeps the labor market tight. Philadelphia Fed president Paulson framed the stance as keeping an 'open mind' on rates rather than committing to a path, language echoed across the Philly Fed's own commentary. The Treasury response was a genuine twist: the 10-year rose 1.50% to 4.75% while the 2-year rose 1.18% to 4.28%, and SOFR fell 0.27% to 3.65%.

The mechanism is that a hold paired with persistent inflation forces the market to reprice the term premium on long-duration paper upward, since investors demand more compensation for holding duration when the Fed signals it is not yet committed to cutting. At the same time, the front end eases because a near-term hold removes the risk of a hike, which is why SOFR ground lower even as the 10-year rose. That combination, long end up, front end down, is the twist the Economic Times flagged.

For leveraged finance, this cuts two ways inside the same capital structure. Floating-rate leveraged loans priced off SOFR see a modest reduction in coupon expense, a direct benefit to interest coverage for sponsor-backed borrowers. But fixed-rate high yield issuance and any refinancing of bullet maturities gets more expensive as the long end rises, and that tension shows up in the spread data: IG OAS tightened 1.25% to 0.79 as investors reward quality duration, while HY OAS widened 0.35% to 2.85 as the market demands more premium for lower-quality credit risk in a higher-for-longer long end.

The next confirming or breaking data points are the ADP employment report and nonfarm payrolls this week, both explicitly framed as inputs to the Fed's next move, along with commentary from the twelve Fed officials voting this cycle. If payrolls confirm the tight labor market implied by the drop in unemployment to 4.20%, the case for a prolonged hold strengthens and the curve twist likely persists.

Oil Craters 8% on Iran De-Escalation Hopes

WTI dropped 8.16% to $84.25 as Qatar said progress had been made toward US-Iran talks to end the war, easing fears of Strait of Hormuz disruption even as Iran continued to demand inbound control of the strait. The move freed up broad risk appetite, with the S&P up 1.48%, the Nasdaq up 2.13%, gold up 0.89%, and the VIX down to 15.86. It matters because it strips a tail-risk premium out of energy pricing in real time, even as core inflation and long yields remain elevated.

Read more

WTI fell 8.16% to $84.25 on reporting from Reuters that Qatar sees progress toward US-Iran talks aimed at ending the war, while a separate Reuters piece noted Iran is still demanding inbound control of the Hormuz strait and outbound oversight, an unresolved sticking point. Aramco separately said the war has already cost the global market 2.6 billion barrels of oil. Against that backdrop, equities rallied broadly, the VIX fell to 15.86, and gold rose 0.89%.

The mechanism is that oil markets carry a live supply-disruption premium tied to Hormuz transit risk, and any credible diplomatic signal gets priced out immediately because the market is forward-looking on geopolitical tail risk rather than current flows. That drop in the energy risk premium feeds directly into lower near-term headline CPI expectations and lower input costs for industrials and consumer names, which supports equity multiples even though the 10-year sits at 4.75% and core inflation is running at 3.46%. The VIX decline reflects reduced hedging demand now that the Hormuz closure scenario looks less imminent.

For credit, the split is real. Energy-heavy high yield issuers, particularly E&P and oilfield services names that trade inside an HY OAS of 2.85, face direct margin compression on lower crude realizations just as spreads are already drifting wider. Conversely, lower oil is a tailwind for consumer and industrial credits through lower input costs, and any resulting easing in headline CPI could reduce the urgency behind the Fed's inflation stance, a modest positive for duration-sensitive IG paper, where OAS already tightened to 0.79.

The next confirming data point is whether the US and Iran move from talks to a formal Hormuz resolution, since Iran's demand for inbound control is still unresolved per Reuters. Also watch the Reuters exclusive that the US has used 'virtually all' of its long-range precision missiles during the war, a constraint that could limit follow-through capacity if diplomacy stalls and tensions reignite.

The same coverage points to Amazon and Alphabet debt issuance as part of an 'AI debt tsunami' being blamed for pushing benchmark borrowing costs to 20-year highs. That matters because AI-infrastructure leverage is now a distinct, watchable credit segment inside HY rather than a diversified tailwind for the whole asset class.

Read more

The spread data shows IG tightening to an OAS of 0.79 while HY OAS widened to 2.85, and HYG, the high yield ETF proxy, still rose 0.34% on the session. Tech Times reported that CoreWeave's loan spread blew out 125 basis points, prompting lenders to demand tighter covenants, while a separate TradingView piece tied Amazon and Alphabet debt issuance to an 'AI debt tsunami' now being blamed for pushing borrowing costs to 20-year highs. The Financial Times separately framed this as a 'second act' for high yield bonds broadly.

The mechanism is credit discrimination inside a single sector. Hyperscaler balance sheets like Amazon and Alphabet can issue AI-capex debt without spread damage because they carry investment-grade support, which is consistent with IG OAS tightening even amid heavy issuance volume. But leveraged, single-purpose AI infrastructure borrowers like CoreWeave lack that cushion, so the same wave of capital demand that absorbs hyperscaler paper forces a real spread concession on weaker credits, which shows up as HY OAS drifting wider even while the broader HY ETF price holds up on technical demand.

For financing markets, the covenant demand after CoreWeave's blowout signals that loan documentation is re-tightening after a long stretch of covenant-lite terms, which raises the cost and reduces the structural flexibility of any capital structure with AI-datacenter exposure. CLOs and other leveraged loan holders of this paper face mark-to-market pressure now, even as HYG's 0.34% gain suggests net technical demand is still absorbing supply, a gap between price and spread that can close quickly if hyperscaler capex guidance disappoints.

The thing to watch is whether other AI-adjacent leveraged credits see the same treatment CoreWeave got, meaning wider spreads plus fresh covenant demands, and whether the broader 'AI debt tsunami' narrative around Amazon and Alphabet issuance keeps pushing benchmark borrowing costs higher. Any secondary trading levels in CoreWeave paper are the cleanest read on whether 125 basis points of widening becomes a template for the sector.

Read more

The dominant story is a repricing of capex itself. For much of this earnings cycle, investors punished hyperscalers for spending too aggressively on AI infrastructure without visible near-term returns. That flipped this week: The Register's tally of nearly $600B in combined cloud capex, Yahoo Finance's report of a $2.3 trillion cloud backlog, and Morgan Stanley's revised $1.4 trillion capex math after hyperscaler earnings all point to the same conclusion, that the backlog is now large and visible enough for the Street to treat the spending as revenue-generating rather than value-destructive. Cramer's characterization of a trillion-dollar boom captures the sentiment shift more than it adds new data, but it is consistent with the scale of what the other headlines describe.

Dispersion within the move is wide. CoreWeave's 20% surge is the clearest read-through, a name whose entire business model is renting out AI compute capacity, so it is the most direct beneficiary of the backlog-to-revenue thesis catching a bid. HY OAS ticked up slightly to 2.85, a reminder that the credit market is still discriminating between investment-grade balance sheets like Microsoft's and the more leveraged, project-finance-style structures used by AI infrastructure players like CoreWeave, where compute assets are financed against long-dated contracted revenue. As capex commitments scale into the hundreds of billions and backlog figures like $2.3 trillion get used to justify further spending, the sector's ability to keep terming out that debt cheaply is doing as much work as the earnings beats themselves.

From here the trade lives and dies on backlog conversion. The bull case needs the $2.3 trillion cloud backlog to keep showing up as recognized revenue on subsequent quarters rather than sitting as a headline figure, and needs Morgan Stanley's $1.4 trillion capex reset to hold rather than get revised up again in a way that reopens the margin-drag debate. The specific catalyst that would flip sentiment back is any hyperscaler guiding capex meaningfully above the new $1.4 trillion consensus without a corresponding lift in backlog conversion, or a widening in IG spreads that raises the cost of debt-funding the buildout just as the market has decided to reward it.
