Macro Update

Fed Set to Hike Into a Political Fight, Not a Cooling Economy

The Fed is expected to raise its benchmark rate this week over the administration's objections, and a CNBC survey now shows respondents expecting at least two more hikes over the next year rather than a single move. The timing coincides with a confirmation battle over Warsh, whose arrival had reportedly cooled the White House's war with the Fed. Roughly three quarters of survey respondents see the inflation problem as broader than just energy, which matters because it removes the easy excuse to look through the oil spike.

Read more

Multiple outlets, including AP and ABC11, report the Fed is expected to raise its benchmark rate this week despite explicit pressure from Trump to hold or cut. CNBC's survey adds texture: this is not read as a terminal move, with respondents expecting at least two more hikes over the coming year. The political backdrop is unusual, with Warsh's presence at the Fed previously credited with easing tension with the White House, and this hike now testing whether that truce holds.

The mechanism here is straightforward repricing of the front end ahead of realized policy action. The 2-year yield rose 1.54% to 4.63%, a larger proportional move than the 10-year's 0.20% rise to 4.96%, consistent with markets pulling forward expected hikes rather than revising long-run growth or inflation expectations. SOFR held flat at 3.62%, meaning the repricing is happening in expectations, not yet in realized funding costs, which is exactly where a hawkish surprise does the most damage to duration-sensitive positioning.

For leveraged finance, a front-end move that outpaces the long end is a curve-flattening signal that raises the cost of floating-rate liabilities faster than it raises the discount rate on cash flows. HY OAS widening 2.26% to 2.71 against a flat IG OAS of 0.80 shows the market discriminating by credit quality rather than repricing duration uniformly, which is the pattern you'd expect if the concern is refinancing capacity at higher-for-longer rates rather than a broad flight from risk. Floating-rate structures, the CLO and BDC commentary in today's set among them, become the more relevant read on how levered issuers are actually funding themselves under this path.

The confirming signal is the actual decision and the dot plot that accompanies it, plus how the Warsh confirmation vote count evolves this week. If the hike lands with a dovish dot plot or dissents, the front-end move partially unwinds. If the CNBC survey's two-hike view gets validated by the Fed's own projections, expect the 2s versus 10s move seen today to extend rather than fade.

Oil Drags the 10-Year Through 5%

WTI jumped 3.24% to $97.26 and the 10-year briefly breached 5% before settling at 4.96%, with Bessent explicitly attributing the yield move to rising oil prices. Gulf equities fell on Houthi attacks and a Hormuz shipping slump, the likely proximate source of the oil move. This matters because it converts an energy shock directly into a discount-rate problem for every duration-sensitive asset, not just an inflation nuisance.

Read more

WTI rose 3.24% to $97.26 on the session, and the 10-year Treasury yield touched 5% intraday, per CNBC, CNN, and Yahoo Finance Singapore, before reversing to close at 4.96%, up 20 basis points. Bessent tied the yield move directly to oil, and Reuters reports Gulf bourses fell on a Houthi attack and a slump in Hormuz shipping, which is the more likely trigger for the crude spike than any domestic demand story. CPI is already running at 3.35% year over year with the index up 0.40% month over month, and diesel-specific price pressure is called out separately as a leading indicator for broader inflation.

The transmission mechanism is inflation expectations embedded in the term premium. Oil is a direct input into headline CPI and a broad cost-push item across transportation and logistics, so a supply-side shock from a shipping chokepoint raises breakeven inflation and nominal yields simultaneously rather than working through growth expectations. That is why the 10-year moved on the oil headline rather than waiting for a data print, and why Bessent felt the need to publicly attribute the yield move to energy rather than fiscal or growth factors.

Higher UST10 raises the base rate for every fixed-rate credit instrument priced off it, and it does so at the same moment HY spreads are widening, meaning all-in coupons for new high-yield issuance are rising from two directions at once. A firm dollar, DXY up 0.11% to 118.21, adds a second tightening channel for dollar-denominated borrowers outside the US. For anyone underwriting a new leveraged loan or bond this week, the reference rate itself moved against them before the credit spread even got quoted.

The thing to watch is whether the Hormuz disruption and Houthi activity persist or de-escalate. A sustained supply shock keeps oil elevated into the Fed meeting and hardens the case for the second and third hikes the CNBC survey is pricing. A reversal in crude, which is what happened to the 10-year intraday, would pull yields back down with it and take pressure off both the inflation read and the term premium.

Sources: cnbc.com · bloomingbit · Reuters

Small Caps and Vol Are Repricing the Higher-for-Longer Path

The Russell fell 1.09% versus a 0.48% decline in the S&P, and VIX jumped 7.95% to 17.10, a divergence pattern consistent with rate-sensitive, floating-rate-heavy names taking the direct hit from the yield and oil moves. Global equities fell in sympathy, with the Nikkei down 0.81% and Eurostoxx down 0.52%. The relevant question for credit is whether HYG's near-flat 0.06% decline means the cash bond market is lagging the spread widening implied by the OAS index.

Read more

The Russell's 1.09% decline outpaced the S&P's 0.48% and the Nasdaq's 0.56% drop, and VIX rose 7.95% to 17.10 on the day. The move was global rather than US-specific, with the Nikkei down 0.81%, Eurostoxx down 0.52%, Shanghai down 0.51%, and the FTSE down 0.31%, indicating the oil and yield shock is being read as a global tightening event rather than a domestic Fed story alone.

Small caps carry disproportionate floating-rate and near-term refinancing exposure relative to large caps, so they are the most direct read on the higher-for-longer repricing happening in the front end. The VIX move reflects the same dynamic in options pricing, with implied volatility rising ahead of a Fed decision that is now expected to be the first of several hikes rather than an isolated move, per the CNBC survey.

The notable divergence is that HYG, the high-yield ETF proxy, was down only 0.06% even as the HY OAS index widened 2.26%. That gap suggests the cash high-yield market has not yet fully repriced to the spread widening implied by the index, which can happen when secondary trading is thin or when the OAS calculation is more sensitive to the Treasury base-rate move than actual credit-risk repricing. Either way, it is a signal to watch closely, since a catch-down in HYG would confirm the spread widening is real and not just a Treasury-driven artifact.

Watch whether Russell underperformance versus the S&P persists through the Fed decision, since a sustained gap is the cleaner signal that markets are pricing the second and third hikes into small-cap financing costs specifically. Also watch whether HYG converges toward the OAS-implied spread widening in the days following the decision, which would confirm credit is following equity vol rather than lagging it.

Saudi outage shock lifts crude

The New York Times confirms the strikes choked off Saudi supply, and AP notes crude gave back part of an even sharper intraday spike as AI-linked equities dropped elsewhere. Refiners are rallying on the fuel squeeze per OilPrice, and Stifel and TheStreet both flagged constructive calls on select E&P and integrated names into the print. Energy is the one sector trading with a bid while VIX jumps 7.95% to 17.10 and yields rise across the curve.

The dominant story is a supply-side shock, not a demand one. Reuters reports the Saudi pipeline outage threatens to remove 4% of global oil supply, and the New York Times frames it directly as attacks choking off Saudi energy output, which is why WTI jumped 3.24% intraday to 97.26 even as equity indices broadly fell. AP's framing matters for tape-reading: oil gave back some of an early, larger spike, and the rest of the market held up better once that reversal took hold, suggesting positioning was already leaning toward disruption before the number came in this hot. The immediate trade is a classic geopolitical risk premium repricing crude higher against a backdrop where nothing on the demand side has changed.

Inside the sector, the dispersion is straightforward. OilPrice reports refiners rallying on what it calls a global fuel squeeze, which tracks with tighter crack spreads whenever a supply outage hits crude and refined product markets don't clear symmetrically. Stifel's note naming four constructive oil and gas names and TheStreet's fresh price targets on three large energy names both point to sell-side repositioning around higher-for-longer realized crude prices, favoring upstream and integrated names with direct commodity leverage over anything services-adjacent or demand-sensitive. The XLE's 1.83% move is a beta reaction to the WTI spike, not a reflection of any company-specific catalyst.

The financing read here cuts two ways. HY OAS widened 2.26% to 2.71 and the VIX jumped 7.95% to 17.10, both signs of broad risk-off that would normally pressure high-yield energy issuers, but higher realized crude prices mechanically improve coverage ratios and free cash flow for E&P and integrated credits in the near term, which is why energy equity is decoupling from the spread widening elsewhere. The bigger problem is the rate side: UST10 at 4.96 and UST2 up 1.54% to 4.63 raise the discount rate on future cash flows and the cost of any new capex or refinancing, which matters more for leveraged E&P names that need to roll debt than for cash-generative majors. If the outage proves durable, expect capital discipline to hold and M&A appetite to stay muted, since managements will bank the windfall rather than lever up into a rate backdrop this uncertain.

CNBC's framing is the one to watch: oil and Treasury yields are moving in lockstep at a tightness not seen in seven years, which the piece calls bad news for markets because it removes the usual diversification benefit of holding both exposures. The setup from here hinges on how quickly Saudi authorities can restore the affected pipeline capacity. A fast repair timeline unwinds the risk premium and likely gives back today's XLE gain along with the crude spike, while any follow-on attack or extended outage would keep the correlation between oil and yields tight and keep pressuring risk assets broadly, not just equities outside energy.

The dominant thread in health care today is regulatory, not macro. Scholar Rock's muscle-targeting SMA therapy Isemblyd landed FDA approval, Curium got the nod for a generic version of Lutathera after a patent scuffle with Novartis, and Guardant Health moved higher as investors digested a recent approval alongside coverage expansion and supportive analyst commentary. Separately, the FDA opened its application window for a new program explicitly designed to shorten drug approval times, and a hearing on a closely watched psychedelic therapy candidate focused on access and safety standards ahead of a decision that FDA officials themselves are framing as culturally significant.

Dispersion is concentrated in single-asset and diagnostics names rather than large-cap pharma. Scholar Rock's approval de-risks a story that was previously binary, converting a clinical-stage credit profile into one with a commercial product and associated cash flow visibility. Guardant Health's move reflects the combination of approval plus payer coverage expansion, which matters more to a diagnostics name's revenue ramp than the approval alone. Curium's generic entry is a credit-relevant event for Novartis specifically, since it introduces direct competition against a branded radioligand therapy that has been a growth driver for that franchise. None of this shows up in the index-level print, which is why XLV can sit flat while the underlying tape is anything but quiet.

The financing backdrop is worth flagging even though today's health care headlines are approval-driven rather than capital-markets-driven. HY OAS widened 2.26% on the same day the VIX jumped nearly 8%, a combination that typically raises the cost of capital for smaller, milestone-dependent biotech issuers who rely on convertible debt or opportunistic equity to bridge to commercialization. Against that backdrop, an FDA approval is not just a clinical catalyst but a financing catalyst: it can be the difference between refinancing at a reasonable spread and being forced into more dilutive or covenant-heavy structures. The FDA's new expedited-review program, if it delivers on shorter timelines, would compress that de-risking window further and should tighten spreads for names with clear regulatory paths, while generic competition like Curium's works in the opposite direction for the branded franchise it targets.

The setup from here hinges on two things outside of today's approvals: whether the psychedelic therapy hearing produces a workable access and safety framework that unlocks a genuinely new commercial category, and whether the FDA's shortened-approval program attracts enough applicants to meaningfully compress review timelines industry-wide. Neither is a near-term event, but both would matter more to biotech financing conditions than any single approval. In the meantime, health care's flat performance against a risk-off tape, with oil up 3.24% and the VIX up nearly 8%, keeps the sector in its familiar defensive posture, and that posture will be tested if HY spreads continue to widen from here.
