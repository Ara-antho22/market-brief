Macro Update

Long End Breaks Higher Even as Auction Demand Holds

The 10-year yield sits at 5.28% and the 30-year hit a fresh 24-year high, yet Thursday's 10-year note auction drew the strongest demand in eight years with overseas takeup near a record. Pimco's CIO and Danske have both flagged 6% as a live risk for the 10-year. The split between rising yields and resilient auction demand is the story leveraged finance desks need to reconcile heading into bank earnings.

Read more

The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.28%, up 0.19% on the session, and the 30-year hit its highest level in 24 years as the long end of the curve sold off independent of the front end, where the 2-year fell 0.42% to 4.77% and SOFR eased to 3.87%. Despite the selloff, the 10-year note auction saw the strongest demand in eight years with overseas buyers taking up a near-record share, a combination that reads as a market willing to accept higher nominal yields rather than one in disorderly retreat from duration. Pimco's CIO and Danske have both put 6% on the table as a plausible near-term level for the 10-year if oil and fiscal pressure persist.

The mechanism here runs through the discount rate used to price every duration-sensitive asset, and leveraged credit is not exempt even though most loans float off SOFR. High yield new issue is still benchmarked against Treasury yields plus a spread, so a higher risk-free rate raises the all-in coupon demanded even if the spread component holds steady. That is showing up already: IG OAS actually tightened 1.20% to 0.82 as investors used the long-end selloff to lock in higher all-in yields on quality paper, while HY OAS widened 1.98% to 3.09, suggesting the duration bid is selective and does not extend to lower-quality risk.

Second order, this is exactly the dynamic that puts bank earnings this week under scrutiny, since surging yields raise questions about net interest margin durability on one side and loan demand and dealmaking appetite on the other. For leveraged issuers with maturities coming due, a higher benchmark rate compounds the refinancing wall problem even where SOFR itself has eased, because term financing gets priced off the belly and long end of the curve, not the overnight rate. Underwriting desks will be watching whether HY new issue can still clear at reasonable spreads to Treasuries with the benchmark this elevated.

The read breaks if auction demand fades at the next long-end sale or if the 10-year genuinely tests 6%, which would likely force HY OAS wider still and choke off new issue windows. It is confirmed if demand at upcoming auctions holds near this week's eight-year high even as yields stay elevated, which would support the idea that this is a rate repricing absorbed by real buyers rather than a liquidity event. Watch the next 10-year and 30-year auction results alongside how bank earnings commentary frames loan growth and net interest income this week.

Minutes Confirm Hawkish Unity in September, Leave October Open

FOMC minutes show all officials backed the September hike and most see another increase this year, but MarketWatch and WSJ both describe no urgency or appetite for a series of hikes, and a top Fed official has since cast doubt on an October move. That sits alongside jobless claims near a 57-year low for a fourth straight week even as unemployment climbed to 4.20%, a labor picture that gives the Fed room to wait. The front end is already pricing the hesitation, with the 2-year down 0.42% to 4.77% and SOFR at 3.87%.

Read more

The minutes released this week showed unanimous support among FOMC members for September's rate increase, with most officials still projecting at least one more hike this year, but language cited by MarketWatch and WSJ explicitly rules out appetite for a series of additional moves and leaves October's timing undecided. That hawkish-but-patient stance comes as CPI sits at 3.35% year over year, described elsewhere as a three-year high, while labor data pulls in the opposite direction: jobless claims have held near a 57-year low for four consecutive weeks even as unemployment rose to 4.20%, up 2.44% on the month, and job growth itself has been described as tepid.

This matters because the Fed is now navigating a labor market that is simultaneously tight on layoffs and soft on hiring, the so-called low-hire, low-fire phase, which complicates the usual read-through from claims to policy. The front end of the curve is already adjusting for that ambiguity, with the 2-year yield down 0.42% to 4.77% and SOFR easing to 3.87%, both consistent with markets pricing less near-term hiking urgency even as the long end sells off on inflation and supply concerns. That divergence between front-end and back-end pricing is the clearest market signal that the next move is genuinely data dependent rather than pre-committed.

For leveraged finance, a steadier to lower SOFR path is directly relevant to the cost of carry on floating-rate term loans and revolvers, offering some relief even as fixed-rate HY issuance gets more expensive on the Treasury benchmark side discussed above. The credit market is already differentiating by quality around this uncertainty: HY OAS widened 1.98% to 3.09 while IG OAS tightened 1.20% to 0.82, a bifurcation consistent with investors treating the labor softening signal as a quality and cyclicality risk rather than a broad rates risk. Lower-rated, more cyclical credits are the ones that would feel an unemployment uptick first, and spreads are starting to reflect that.

Confirmation would come from a clean September payrolls print that resolves the claims-versus-hiring tension, or from clearer Fed communication ahead of the October meeting about whether the hike bias is operative. A break in this read would be a sharp reacceleration in claims or a jump in unemployment that forces the Fed to abandon the hike bias altogether, which would likely pull HY spreads wider further and fast given current positioning. Watch the next payrolls release and any speeches from officials who have already cast doubt on an October move.

Quality Bifurcation Widens in Credit as HY Lags IG

HY OAS widened 1.98% to 3.09 while IG OAS tightened 1.20% to 0.82, and HYG slipped 0.05% to 77.14, a split that lines up with the Nasdaq's 1.25% decline and VIX ticking up to 15.08. Oaktree's latest Credit Quarterly is framed around new terms in the market and FTI Consulting is publishing on the state of restructuring, both signals that practitioners are already repricing the cycle. This is the clearest leveraged finance read of the session: risk premium is rebuilding at the bottom of the capital structure while quality continues to find a bid.

Read more

High yield OAS widened 1.98% to 3.09 on the session while investment grade OAS tightened 1.20% to 0.82, a divergence that also showed up in HYG slipping 0.05% to 77.14 even as the broader Treasury-driven selloff discussed above might have been expected to pressure both cohorts similarly. Instead, IG caught a duration and quality bid while HY underperformed alongside the Nasdaq's 1.25% drop and a modest uptick in the VIX to 15.08, consistent with investors discriminating by credit quality rather than reacting uniformly to the rate move.

Mechanically, this is a risk premium story layered on top of a rates story. When the risk-free rate rises, IG spreads can tighten if investors are willing to lock in a higher all-in yield on safer paper, which is what happened here. HY does not get that same offset because the marginal buyer of junk debt is pricing both the higher benchmark and the probability of credit deterioration, and with unemployment ticking up to 4.20% and jobless claims data showing a genuinely bifurcated labor market, that second input just got a little more uncertain.

The practitioner commentary reinforces the signal. Oaktree's Credit Quarterly is explicitly framed around new terms, language that in credit markets typically means looser covenants, more flexible documentation, or structural accommodations being negotiated as issuers and lenders adjust to a higher-for-longer rate regime, while FTI Consulting's piece on the state of the restructuring market suggests views on stress are diverging depending on who is asked. Put together with HY OAS widening, this points to a market where new issue and refinancing activity will increasingly be priced and structured around idiosyncratic credit quality rather than a uniform spread-to-Treasury assumption.

This read is confirmed if HY OAS continues to widen into the CPI print and bank earnings this week while IG holds its tightening bias, which would mark a genuine quality rotation rather than noise. It breaks if HY spreads snap back in alongside IG, suggesting the move was a one-day liquidity artifact tied to the yield spike rather than a repricing of credit risk. Watch the HY new issue calendar this week for whether deals clear inside or outside current indicated spreads, and watch bank earnings commentary on leveraged loan and high yield underwriting pipelines.

Energy was the one sector moving against a broader tape that saw the Nasdaq down 1.25% and credit spreads widen.

The headline tension here is that crude itself barely moved while the equity complex ripped. WTI at 96.24 is up just 0.11% on the session, yet XLE closed up nearly 3%. The explanation sits in the refining and supply-disruption headlines rather than the crude tape: the Dallas Fed's warning on refinery outages keeping fuel prices elevated, combined with Hurricane Isaias threatening Gulf supply chains, points to a crack-spread and product-margin story layered on top of a geopolitical floor from the unresolved Iran standoff. NBC's framing, that Trump's pledge not to strike before midterms hasn't soothed the market, suggests traders are still pricing tail risk into the forward curve even with spot prices quiet.

Dispersion is concentrated in refining and Gulf-exposed names given the outage and hurricane headlines, with retail flow (per the Yahoo Finance piece on stocks retail investors are watching) adding a bid to widely held large-cap energy names as both oil prices and bond yields rise. That retail interest is itself notable: it is being framed as a reaction to rising yields, not falling ones, which is unusual positioning for a rate-sensitive sector and suggests investors are treating energy equities as an inflation and geopolitical hedge rather than a pure commodity-price trade.

The financing backdrop cuts both ways for the sector's leveraged issuers. HY OAS widened 1.98% on the session while IG OAS tightened 1.20%, a divergence that matters because shale E&Ps and smaller refiners sit disproportionately in high yield. Wider HY spreads raise the marginal cost of capital for exactly the names best positioned to benefit from elevated crack spreads and refinery-outage-driven product pricing, a disconnect that could cap the move if spreads keep drifting wider. UST10 at 5.28% (+0.19%) also raises the discount rate on long-duration upstream capex, even as SOFR easing slightly to 3.87% offers some relief on floating-rate exposure. Net, balance sheets with refining exposure and near-term free cash flow from elevated fuel prices are better insulated than pure E&P credits still financing in a widening HY tape.

From here the setup hinges on two swing factors: whether the Iran standoff stays rhetorical through midterms or escalates, and how long the refinery outages and hurricane-related disruptions persist into product markets. A de-escalation headline would likely compress the geopolitical premium embedded in the equity move without crude having to confirm it, while a prolonged refinery outage or confirmed Isaias supply hit would validate the crack-spread trade and could pull HY spreads tighter for refiners specifically even if broader credit stays under pressure. Jobs data referenced in the Kitco wire is the next scheduled catalyst that could move the complex independent of the energy-specific headlines.

The setup from here is about whether today's HY spread widening is noise inside a broader equity risk-off (VIX up to 15.08) or the start of something that pressures levered pharma credit ahead of refinancing windows. On the regulatory side, watch how the FDA's compounding peptide decision actually lands, since it bears directly on GLP-1 compounder economics, and watch for any follow-through on the ProPublica prior authorization investigation if it generates payer-specific headlines. Absent either, this remains a quiet tape for the sector relative to the Nasdaq-led selloff elsewhere.

Office vacancy falls, foreclosures persist

Long Island and downtown Chicago both posted second-straight-quarter declines in office vacancy alongside strong industrial absorption, while a San Francisco Showplace Square office building taken back by its lender is reportedly filling up and a prime Fifth Avenue tower is headed to foreclosure auction after its planned sale collapsed. The sector is trading on a bifurcated narrative: stabilizing fundamentals in secondary office and industrial markets against unresolved legacy debt stress in trophy and suburban assets. Credit spreads diverged too, with IG OAS tightening 1.2% to 0.82 while HY OAS widened nearly 2% to 3.09, a split that maps directly onto which sponsors can refinance cleanly and which cannot.

Read more

The dominant story is a widening gap between operating fundamentals and capital structure outcomes in office. Long Island and downtown Chicago both reported vacancy rates falling for a second consecutive quarter, with industrial leasing described as strong in both markets, and Milwaukee's lakefront submarket drew a bullish Colliers readout on overall commercial activity. At the same time, a Showplace Square office building in San Francisco that has already passed to its lender is reportedly leasing up, which is a workout story, not a sale story, and a Norwalk, Connecticut office property with a high vacancy rate is listed at $9.9 million, a level that signals a discounted, basis-reset transaction rather than a stabilized asset trade. The clearest distress marker is the prime Fifth Avenue tower headed to foreclosure auction after its planned sale fell through, evidence that even trophy Manhattan office cannot always clear a negotiated exit at the lender's preferred basis.

The dispersion is geographic and vintage-driven rather than sector-wide. Markets with industrial and logistics exposure, Long Island, suburban Chicago along the I-88 corridor where an IT firm's relocation is enabling a new warehouse project, and Milwaukee's lakefront mix, are showing the kind of leasing velocity that supports rent growth and NOI stability. Office assets financed at pre-2022 cap rates and now facing refinancing are the ones surfacing in foreclosure dockets and discounted listings, with the Fifth Avenue and Norwalk situations both examples of basis that has not yet found a clearing buyer. Lender-owned assets like Showplace Square represent the intermediate case, properties where special servicers are stabilizing occupancy before attempting a disposition, which is consistent with a market still working through the office maturity wall rather than one that has already cleared it.

The financing read-through is that the cost of capital for office remains bifurcated along the same lines as the leasing data. SOFR ticking down to 3.87% eases floating-rate debt service modestly, but the 10-year at 5.28% keeps long-term fixed refinancing expensive and keeps cap rate expansion pressure on any office asset without strong in-place cash flow. The credit spread divergence matters directly here: IG OAS tightened to 0.82%, meaning well-capitalized REITs with investment-grade balance sheets can still term out debt efficiently, while HY OAS widened to 3.09%, a level that raises the cost of capital for the sponsors and special servicers who actually hold the distressed office paper now surfacing in foreclosure and discounted-sale headlines. That spread gap is why workouts like Showplace Square and the Fifth Avenue tower are happening through lender possession and auction rather than conventional refinancing, the private and non-investment-grade financing channel is pricier and less available than it was a year ago.

The setup from here depends on whether the industrial and secondary-office leasing momentum broadens enough to pull more distressed assets out of special servicing before loan extensions run out. A continuation of vacancy declines in markets like Chicago and Long Island would support the thesis that office is bottoming operationally, but the real catalyst to watch is whether foreclosure assets like the Fifth Avenue tower clear at prices that reset the comp set for other maturing office loans. A weak clearing price there would pressure valuations across comparable trophy office collateral, while a clean sale at a defensible basis would help re-open the refinancing channel for similarly situated borrowers.
