Thursday, October 15, 2026

Newsletter Summary

The selloff split cleanly along duration and quality lines Thursday. The S&P 500 fell 1.11% to 7,718.94 and the Nasdaq dropped a sharper 1.88% to 26,692.87, while the Dow barely moved, down 0.09% to 51,621.48, a gap that points to growth and rate-sensitive names taking the hit rather than a broad risk-off tape. The catalyst is the September CPI print, which came in at 3.72% year over year with the index up 0.65% month over month, hot enough that Reuters, WSJ and the Fed-watching press are now framing a rate hike, not a cut, as the live outcome at the next FOMC meeting. Overnight the pressure showed up loudest in Tokyo, where the Nikkei fell 4.90% to 64,425.95, with Shanghai down 2.34% and the Russell off 1.93% domestically. Credit is starting to register it too: the HY OAS widened 1.61% to 3.16 even as IG OAS held flat at 0.83, a split that says stress is concentrated in lower-quality paper rather than broad-based. The VIX barely moved, up to 15.27, which tells you the options market still sees this as a repricing, not a dislocation.

Macro Update

CPI at 3.72% Puts a Hike Back on the Table

September CPI rose 3.72% year over year with the index up 0.65% month over month, broad-based and energy-led, and Reuters, the Hill and MarketWatch all now describe Fed rate hike odds as rising into a meeting that falls on the eve of an election. The front end is where this shows up first: SOFR sits at 3.88% and UST2 at 4.79%, both already carrying the repricing even as they ticked down slightly today. For a desk focused on floating-rate exposure, a hike discussion rather than a cut discussion changes the base case for 2026 debt service.

Read more

September's CPI index level hit 336.30, up 0.65% on the month, pushing the year-over-year rate to 3.72%, described across Reuters, WSJ, AP and the Washington Post as broad-based and driven in large part by energy costs. The headlines are explicit that rate hike odds for the upcoming FOMC meeting are rising rather than falling, a reversal of the cutting-cycle narrative that has dominated most of this year, and the meeting itself falls on the eve of an election, which Yahoo Finance notes is the third consecutive cycle the Fed has had to navigate that timing.

The transmission mechanism here runs through the front end first. UST2 at 4.79% and SOFR at 3.88% already reflect a market that has partially repriced hike risk, and every basis point added to SOFR flows directly into the coupon on floating-rate leveraged loans and revolvers. UST10 at 5.21% sits at a level consistent with a higher-for-longer regime even though it eased 0.57% today, meaning long-duration credit and equity discount rates are being set against a materially higher risk-free rate than the market assumed earlier this year.

The second-order effect is visible in credit spreads. HY OAS widened 1.61% to 3.16 today while IG OAS held flat at 0.83, and the Penn Mutual note on widening CCC spreads flags stress concentrated at the bottom of the quality stack rather than investment grade. That is the pattern you would expect if the market is pricing higher-for-longer financing costs into the issuers with the least pricing power and the most floating-rate exposure, which is precisely the cohort most sensitive to a hike rather than a hold.

The next confirmation point is the FOMC decision itself and any accompanying dot plot language, since the headlines so far are describing odds and expectations rather than a committed path. Watch whether HY OAS continues to widen into the meeting or stabilizes once the decision is known, and watch SOFR for any further move that would reprice existing floating-rate loan books.

Sources: Reuters · WSJ · Penn Mutual Asset Management

CoreWeave Yields Near 15% Flag AI Credit Stress

CoreWeave bonds are trading near 15% yields according to CNBC, and Business Insider frames the move as a bond-market warning on the AI infrastructure financing model. That shows up today in the equity tape too, with the Nasdaq down 1.88% and HY OAS widening 1.61% to 3.16 while IG OAS stayed flat at 0.83, consistent with stress being concentrated in lower-rated, story-driven credits rather than the broad market. For lev fin, this is the clearest read yet that the capital markets window for AI capex-funded buildouts is getting more expensive at the margin.

Read more

CNBC reports CoreWeave shares falling as the company's bond yields approach 15%, with Business Insider separately describing the bond market as sending a warning about a company it calls a darling of the AI infrastructure boom. These are not small moves for a credit; yields near 15% imply the market is demanding a substantial risk premium relative to where AI infrastructure paper has priced earlier in the buildout cycle.

The mechanism is straightforward for a capital-intensive, debt-funded growth story. CoreWeave and similar AI infrastructure issuers depend on continued access to capital markets at reasonable spreads to fund data center and GPU capacity; when secondary yields blow out, it raises the marginal cost of the next financing round and signals to equity holders that leverage assumptions underwriting the growth case are being repriced in real time, which is consistent with the Nasdaq's 1.88% decline today outpacing the S&P's 1.11% drop.

The spread data supports a contained-but-real read. HY OAS widened 1.61% to 3.16 while IG OAS was unchanged at 0.83, meaning the stress is showing up in the lower-quality cohort rather than broadly across corporate credit. The Penn Mutual note on widening CCC spreads fits the same pattern. For leveraged finance desks, this matters less as a single-name story and more as an early read on whether the AI capex cycle's debt funding model can keep clearing at tight spreads once rate and credit conditions tighten simultaneously.

The next signal to watch is whether other AI infrastructure or hyperscaler-adjacent credits see similar spread widening, which would confirm this is sector-wide rather than idiosyncratic to CoreWeave, and whether HY OAS continues to drift wider from 3.16 or stabilizes. A move in IG OAS off its flat 0.83 print would be the signal that contagion is spreading up the quality stack.

Sources: CNBC · Business Insider · Penn Mutual Asset Management

The Nikkei fell 4.90% to 64,425.95 and Shanghai dropped 2.34%, a decline large enough by either index's own standards to stand out against the rest of the tape, where the Dow was flat and the FTSE actually gained 0.19%. Gold fell 1.49% to 375.99 at the same time the dollar firmed, with DXY up 0.13%, which argues this is a rates and dollar-driven repricing rather than a classic flight to safety. The combination of a stronger dollar, elevated UST10 at 5.21%, and a Nikkei decline this size points to tightening global financial conditions rather than an isolated regional event.

Read more

The Nikkei's 4.90% drop to 64,425.95 and Shanghai's 2.34% decline to 31,36 were the sharpest moves on the board overnight, well ahead of the Russell's 1.93% and Nasdaq's 1.88% declines domestically. The divergence from the Dow, down just 0.09%, and the FTSE, up 0.19%, shows this was not a uniform global risk-off session but one concentrated in Asia and in US growth and small-cap names.

The mechanism linking these moves is the dollar and rate complex rather than a credit event. DXY firmed 0.13% and UST10 held near 5.21%, a level that raises the discount rate applied to Asian equities and simultaneously makes dollar-denominated debt service more expensive for exporters and foreign borrowers. Gold's 1.49% decline to 375.99 is the tell that this is a real-rate and dollar story rather than a fear trade, since gold would typically catch a bid in a genuine flight to quality.

For credit, the read-through is that financial conditions are tightening on multiple fronts at once rather than just in one asset class. HY OAS widening 1.61% to 3.16 alongside a firmer dollar and still-elevated long-end yields compounds funding costs for any dollar-based borrower, including emerging-market and Asian issuers who roll dollar debt, even as the VIX's modest move to 15.27 suggests US volatility markets have not yet escalated this into a broader stress event.

Watch whether the Nikkei stabilizes or extends its decline in the next session, since a one-day 4.90% move needs follow-through to confirm it is a regime shift rather than a single-session air pocket. Also watch DXY and UST10 together: if the dollar keeps firming while yields stay elevated, the pressure on Asian equities and gold is likely to persist rather than mean-revert.

Sources: Reuters · Morningstar · Crux Investor

Reuters' companion piece on Saudi Arabia leading a September OPEC+ output increase, despite the Iran war disruption, is the more telling data point: supply grew even with conflict risk in the region, which is why WTI closed only 0.11% higher at 96.24 rather than showing a real risk premium.

That is a mild risk-off signal in high yield broadly, not an energy-specific one, but it matters for the sector's leveraged issuers because WTI holding near 96 keeps coverage ratios and free cash flow generation intact for E&P and midstream credits even as the rest of the HY complex cheapens slightly. Stable crude at this level is doing more for energy balance sheets right now than any single piece of news.

The next real test is whether the IEA report's supply assessment and the confirmed OPEC+ September output increase start to cap crude even if Mideast headlines stay tense. If chip-driven doubt about AI returns persists, the bond market's tolerance for that leverage is the next pressure point, not just the equity multiple.

From here the catalyst is whatever the next data point on hyperscaler capex intentions looks like, whether that is guidance language, a capex-linked bond deal, or another chip name reporting soft demand. The 10-year sitting at 5.21% and SOFR at 3.88% keep the cost-of-debt backdrop for that capex elevated, so any further wobble in the AI growth narrative would show up first in credit spreads on the names doing the heaviest borrowing before it shows up as a broader equity rout.

The dominant story in health care today is not a single corporate event but the scale of the sector's relative outperformance against a broadly weak tape. XLV's 2.53% gain against S&P, Nasdaq, and Russell declines of over 1% each marks a clear rotation day, the kind typically driven by investors favoring defensive, less rate-sensitive cash flows when growth indices sell off and overseas markets (Nikkei -4.90%, Shanghai -2.34%) signal broader risk aversion. The underlying news flow in health care itself was incremental: the FDA's planned new framework for rare disease drug reviews, reported by both The Pharma Letter and PharmTech.com, focuses on tightening chemistry, manufacturing and controls (CMC) review scope rather than loosening approval standards.

Dispersion within the sector is likely concentrated around pipeline and competitive-dynamics news rather than a single catalyst. Legend Biotech's IND approval for its dual-targeting CAR-T therapy LB2501 and continued coverage of GLP-1 drug benefits point to where speculative biotech and large-cap pharma pipeline interest is concentrated, even though none of today's headlines carry hard trial data or guidance that would explain a two-and-a-half point sector move on their own.

There is no financing or M&A story in today's health care headlines, so the more relevant angle is regulatory and competitive positioning. A clearer FDA pathway for rare disease reviews lowers development uncertainty for smaller biotech names reliant on orphan drug designations, while the Keytruda biosimilar filing is an early marker of the patent-cliff dynamic that eventually pressures large-cap pharma cash flow visibility, a factor credit analysts watch closely given how much of pharma's investment-grade rating support rests on durable blockbuster revenue. IG spreads were flat on the session, suggesting the market is not yet pricing incremental credit risk from this competitive threat.

From here, the setup is a sector benefiting from a risk-off rotation without a proportional increase in its own event risk. The next real catalysts are procedural: formal publication of the FDA's rare disease framework, movement in Zydus's biosimilar review timeline, and any trial updates tied to Legend Biotech's CAR-T program. Absent those, today's outperformance looks more like a function of where capital rotated on a weak broader tape than a repricing of health care fundamentals.
