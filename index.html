Monday, November 9, 2026

Newsletter Summary

The S&P 500 fell 4.36% to 7,353.73, which is the number that defines the session, while the Nasdaq rose 1.09% to 24,579.42 and the Russell 2000 added 0.99% to 273.67, a split that says this was a narrow, breadth-poor selloff rather than a uniform risk-off day. The VIX jumped 69.66% to 28.18, the sharpest one-day vol repricing of this stretch, and credit followed: HY OAS widened 4.82% to 3.48% and IG OAS rose 2.20% to 0.93%, both still low by historical standards but moving fast in percentage terms. Treasuries rallied even as equities accelerated lower, with the 10-year yield down 1.22% to 4.84% and the 2-year down 2.62% to 4.46%, a flight-to-quality bid that sits awkwardly against an October payrolls report that added 165,000 jobs and pushed unemployment down to 4.10%. For the desk, this reads as a vol and spread-widening event concentrated in broad equity and credit risk premia rather than a dollar, oil, or HY liquidity event, since DXY was flat and HYG held at just -0.06%.

Macro Update

S&P Drops 4.36%, VIX Up 70%, Spreads Widen Fast If Not Far

The S&P 500 fell 4.36% to 7,353.73 and the VIX spiked 69.66% to 28.18, the largest one-day volatility repricing of the cycle so far. HY OAS widened 4.82% to 3.48% and IG OAS rose 2.20% to 0.93%, both still tight in absolute terms but fast in velocity, which matters more for primary market pricing than the headline level does. HYG itself was flat at -0.06%, suggesting the move is a mark-to-market and vol event rather than a credit liquidity event yet.

Read more

The S&P 500 closed at 7,353.73, down 4.36%, against a Dow that fell a much more modest 0.46% to 51,285.73 and a Russell 2000 that actually gained 0.99% to 273.67, underscoring that this was not an across-the-board equity rout but a concentrated drawdown. The VIX jumped 69.66% to 28.18. HY OAS rose 4.82% to 3.48% and IG OAS rose 2.20% to 0.93%. Treasuries bid up in parallel, with the 10-year yield down 1.22% to 4.84% and the 2-year down 2.62% to 4.46%, while gold actually fell 0.79% to 372.52, which is not the pattern you would expect from a classic flight-to-safety session.

The transmission mechanism here runs through dealer vol books before it runs through fundamentals. As implied vol jumps this fast, market makers in HY and IG widen bid-ask and marks defensively, CLO equity tranches and leveraged loan funds get marked down on the move, and new-issue concession requirements widen even if the underlying credit has not deteriorated. The flight-to-quality bid in Treasuries, even alongside a hot jobs report, tells you the move is being driven by equity vol and position de-risking rather than a reassessment of the macro data itself.

For leveraged finance desks, the relevant second-order effect is in the primary calendar. A move in HY OAS of this percentage size in a single session, even from a low base, is the kind of print that causes issuers to either pull forward pricing to get ahead of further widening or pause deals altogether until spreads stabilize. The Yahoo Finance Canada and Fortune pieces both frame this session as exposing deeper concerns about U.S. debt sustainability, which the AEI piece ties to brewing bond-market stress in Europe as well, a read that if it holds would keep the bid for duration intact even as credit risk premia reprice wider.

What would confirm this is a vol event rather than something more structural is whether HY OAS stabilizes near 3.48% rather than continuing to climb, and whether the VIX mean-reverts back under 20 in the next session or two. What would break the benign read is further HYG weakness beyond the current -0.06%, or a widening in CDX HY/IG that outpaces the cash bond move, which would signal dealers are repricing tail risk rather than just marking positions.

Sources: Yahoo! Finance Canada · Fortune · American Enterprise Institute - AEI

Payrolls Beat, Yields Fall: A Growth-Scare Tape Overriding a Hawkish Print

October payrolls added 165,000 jobs, well above expectations, and unemployment fell to 4.10%, yet Treasury yields dropped across the curve with the 10-year down 1.22% to 4.84% and the 2-year down 2.62% to 4.46%. That combination is inconsistent with a market simply pricing a hot labor report, which argues the equity vol shock dominated the rates tape. The Fed held rates steady in Kevin Warsh's first meeting as chair, leaving the December path genuinely contested.

Read more

Payrolls rose 165,000 in October, described in coverage as far exceeding expectations, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.10%, a 2.38% decline from the prior reading. CPI remained elevated at 3.72% year over year with the index at 336.30, up 0.65%. Despite the stronger labor data, the 10-year yield fell 1.22% to 4.84% and the 2-year fell 2.62% to 4.46%, with SOFR down 0.77% to 3.89%. The Fed held rates steady in what is reported as Kevin Warsh's first meeting as chair.

A strong payrolls print alongside above-target CPI would normally argue for higher, not lower, yields, since it reduces the odds of near-term cuts. The fact that yields fell anyway signals that the equity vol spike, VIX up 69.66%, pulled enough flight-to-quality demand into Treasuries to override the growth-and-inflation signal from the jobs data. That is a classic duration bid driven by risk aversion rather than by a dovish reassessment of the Fed's reaction function.

For financing desks, the lower front end is a near-term tailwind for floating-rate borrowers, since SOFR resets lower feed directly into leveraged loan coupons. But if the move is a growth scare rather than a genuine dovish pivot, the benefit is partially offset by the same-day widening in HY OAS, up 4.82% to 3.48%, which raises the all-in cost of new leveraged issuance even as the base rate compresses. Reporting also flags midterm election results as adding uncertainty to the Fed's path into December, which keeps the policy outlook, and therefore the term premium, unsettled.

Watch the October CPI print flagged for release this week, previewed as possibly showing inflation growth slowing, since a soft print would validate the bond rally as a genuine disinflation trade rather than a risk-off flight. Also watch whether the 2-year stabilizes or keeps falling, since continued front-end declines would mean the market is now pricing cuts despite the payrolls beat, a materially different read than a one-day vol-driven dip.

Sources: The New York Times · ABC15 Arizona · National Association of Wholesaler-Distributors · bloomingbit

Nasdaq Up, S&P Down: Tech Credit Starts to Bifurcate

The Nasdaq rose 1.09% to 24,579.42 and the Russell 2000 gained 0.99% even as the S&P fell 4.36%, a split that traces to mega-cap tech holding up against broader selling. Meta's bond sale is being read by Bloomberg as a reset in pricing for tech debt, landing the same day Goldman warned the bond market is running out of room to absorb AI-related issuance. IG OAS widened 2.20% to 0.93% in the same session, a small but real signal that AI capex funding is starting to carry a credit premium.

Read more

The Nasdaq closed at 24,579.42, up 1.09%, and the Russell 2000 closed at 273.67, up 0.99%, both moving opposite the S&P 500's 4.36% decline and the Dow's 0.46% decline. The same session carried coverage of Meta's bond sale as marking a new pricing reality for tech debt, and a separate piece describing an AI credit clock running down as the bond market approaches capacity constraints for financing AI-related capital spending. IG OAS rose 2.20% to 0.93%, a modest absolute move but one landing directly on top of large tech primary issuance.

The divergence between the Nasdaq and the S&P points to a breadth problem rather than a uniform rally: mega-cap earnings strength is propping up the index-weighted names while the broader market, including Dow components, sells off on the macro and vol headlines. On the credit side, when IG spreads widen even modestly on the same day a large tech issuer like Meta is pricing new debt, it signals investors are beginning to differentiate AI-capex-funded credits from the broader IG universe rather than treating them as undifferentiated high-grade risk.

For a leveraged finance seat, the signal to watch is whether other large tech issuers follow Meta to the market to lock funding ahead of any further spread widening, and whether private credit and direct lending vehicles step in as a release valve if traditional IG and HY channels tighten up capacity for AI-related issuance, which is the exact constraint Goldman's note is flagging. HYG held essentially flat at -0.06%, which suggests this repricing is so far idiosyncratic to AI-exposed issuers and large-cap tech paper rather than spreading into broad high yield.

Confirmation to watch for is how Meta's new bonds trade in secondary versus where they priced, and whether the next large tech issuer coming to market prices at a wider spread to Treasuries than Meta did. If subsequent deals widen further or get pulled, that would validate Goldman's capacity-constraint thesis; if spreads hold or tighten back in, this session's IG move was likely a transitory mark tied to the broader equity vol spike rather than a durable repricing of AI credit risk.

Sources: Bloomberg.com · The Dark Side Of The Boom

Sector shrugs off broad market rout

WTI eased a modest 0.42% to $99.64, nowhere near the kind of move that headline risk-off action in equities would suggest. The divergence shows energy behaving as a relative safe harbor during a session when risk assets broadly sold off and credit spreads widened. The defining feature of today's tape was the gap between what happened to energy and what happened to everything else. The S&P 500 dropped 4.36%, the VIX jumped 69.66% to 28.18, and high yield spreads widened 4.82% to an OAS of 3.48 with investment grade OAS up 2.20% to 0.93. Against that backdrop, XLE's 0.02% decline and WTI's 0.42% slip to $99.64 look almost inert. Energy did not catch the bid that would typically accompany a flight to quality, but it also avoided the drawdown that hit broader equities, leaving the sector as one of the few pockets of relative stability.

Dispersion within the group is where the real signal sits. Analysts cited in Yahoo Finance Canada argued the midterm-driven selloff in Canadian energy stocks has overshot the fundamentals, implying a valuation gap worth watching. On the E&P side, Seeking Alpha's take on Diamondback Energy models the stock as pricing in $67 a barrel against spot crude near $100, a roughly 33-dollar cushion between the market's implied assumption and where oil actually trades, which the analysis frames as the source of 40%-plus upside. That gap, if the thesis holds, says more about how conservatively the market is discounting current energy cash flows than about any near-term catalyst.

The sector's resilience today is notable given that energy issuers are disproportionately exposed to the high yield market, and spreads widened nearly 5% in a single session. Wider HY OAS raises the marginal cost of capital for leveraged E&P and oilfield services names that rely on that market for refinancing, even if today's move in XLE itself shows no visible stress. The Diamondback framing, pricing shares off a $67 breakeven when spot sits near $100, effectively describes a balance sheet buffer: names underwriting production economics well below current prices have more room to absorb a spread-widening episode like today's without reaching for incremental leverage.

The next read comes from whether oil's recent slide, the subject of the Barron's piece questioning where prices go from here, extends or stabilizes, and whether today's spread widening in HY and IG persists into tomorrow's session or proves to be a one-day VIX-driven spike. If credit stays wider, the energy names most reliant on external financing will be the first to show it, while the low-breakeven producers highlighted by sell-side desks should keep trading on fundamentals rather than tape-driven fear.

The AI capex debate sharpened today rather than resolved. None of this is new as a thesis, but the timing, landing on a day when the VIX jumped 69.66% to 28.18 and the S&P 500 fell 4.36%, gave the bear case more attention than it has had in weeks.

The dominant fact in health care today is what did not happen to it. While the S&P dropped 4.36% and the VIX surged to 28.18, XLV barely budged, down 0.06%. That is the sector behaving exactly as a defensive allocation is supposed to behave on a day when equity vol exploded higher. There is no single health care headline big enough to explain sector-wide positioning either way, which is itself consistent with a group that trades on its own fundamentals rather than on the macro tape during risk-off sessions.

Underneath the flat index print, single names moved on FDA decisions. Novocure fell after the FDA declined to approve its lung cancer device, a regulatory setback for the company. Nothing in today's headlines points to a financing or balance sheet story specific to health care. The more relevant thread is regulatory and policy exposure. The FDA's device approval process remains a swing factor for medtech names like Novocure, and a separate headline noting RFK Jr.'s FDA will act on a petition to reexamine ultra-processed food ingredients signals continued policy attention at the agency that could eventually touch food-adjacent health and nutrition names. With IG and HY spreads widening only modestly today, 2.20% and 4.82% respectively off low absolute levels, there is no credit stress reaching into the sector's capital structure decisions yet.

The setup from here is more about continued single-name FDA catalysts than a sector-wide repricing. Watch for how Novocure responds to the device rejection, whether Vir's hepatitis delta data translates into follow-through versus Gilead-exposed names, and whether the broader equity volatility spike seen today (VIX up to 28.18) persists into tomorrow's session, which would reinforce the rotation into defensives that kept XLV flat even as the rest of the market fell sharply.

For owners with maturities coming due, that is a wash at best: a lower risk-free rate helps the refinancing math only if spreads don't erode the benefit, and today's spread widening suggests credit markets are still charging a premium for CRE-specific risk even as Treasuries rally.

The setup from here depends on whether today's Treasury rally holds or reverses once the VIX spike fades, since XLRE's relative resilience was built entirely on the rates move, not on sector-specific good news. If yields stay down while spreads normalize, the refinancing backdrop Puck describes starts to actually improve; if the equity vol spike instead bleeds further into HY and IG spreads, the industrial oversupply visible in New Jersey could spread to other distressed pockets. Watch the next session's move in HY OAS and the 10-year together, since that pairing is what decoupled XLRE from the broader tape today.
