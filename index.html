Morning Edition

Wednesday, November 18, 2026

Newsletter Summary

Equities sold off broadly but not dramatically: the S&P 500 fell 1.92% to 7,416.59, the Dow dropped 1.64% to 50,759.42, and the Russell 2000 lost 1.43%, while the Nasdaq held up better at -0.74%. The VIX jumped 18.70% to 22.91, a real one-day repricing of near-term risk though still far from stress-era levels. Credit moved with equities but unevenly: the high-yield OAS widened 11bp to 3.34% while investment-grade spreads were essentially flat at 0.90, pointing to selective de-risking in lower-quality paper rather than a systemic event. Treasuries caught a modest bid even as Fed officials talked tougher, with the 10-year yield easing a few basis points to 5.12% and the 2-year slipping to 4.83%, suggesting the equity selloff pulled in enough duration demand to offset hawkish Fedspeak from Musalem and Waller. UK inflation added to the higher-for-longer narrative, rising to a one-year high of 3.6% on record fuel prices, and European bourses fell in sympathy with London and New York (FTSE -1.35%, Eurostoxx -1.42%). None of today's moves are historically large on their own, but they are stacking in the same direction: rates staying restrictive longer than hoped, and credit starting to discriminate accordingly.

Macro Update

Hawkish Fedspeak Meets a Risk-Off Tape

St. Louis Fed's Musalem said several more rate hikes are likely needed before spring 2027, and CNBC reported Fed officials including Governor Waller are positioned to raise again in December with inflation still above target. Equities sold off in response, with the S&P down 1.92% and the VIX up 18.70% to 22.91. Credit widened too, but only in high yield, a sign the stress is concentrated in lower-quality balance sheets rather than systemic.

Read more

Musalem's comment that several hikes are likely needed before spring 2027 landed alongside CNBC reporting that Fed officials are set to raise again in December, with continued above-target inflation potentially pushing the committee toward a more aggressive stance. Powell separately confirmed he will stay on the Fed's board after his term expires next month, removing one source of personnel uncertainty but not changing the policy signal. The S&P 500 fell 1.92% to 7,416.59, the Dow fell 1.64%, the Russell 2000 fell 1.43%, and the VIX jumped 18.70% to 22.91.

The mechanism here is discount-rate driven. With SOFR at 3.92% and the 2-year at 4.83%, the policy backdrop is already restrictive, and Musalem's and Waller's comments remove the near-term prospect of relief. That keeps the discount rate applied to long-duration equity cash flows elevated, compressing multiples more than it changes earnings expectations, and the vol jump reflects markets repricing tail risk into the December FOMC meeting rather than a fundamental growth shock.

Credit told a more selective story: the HY OAS widened 11bp to 3.34% while IG OAS was essentially unchanged at 0.90%, up just 1bp. That divergence is consistent with the Fidelity note on the renewed appeal of floating-rate loans given rates are not falling as previously assumed, and it means the hit is landing on leveraged, floating-rate-exposed capital structures first. Issuers that were underwriting a 2027 cutting cycle into their refinancing plans now face a longer wait at a higher cost of capital.

Watch today's FOMC minutes release for the internal vote split behind the hawkish rhetoric, and watch whether the HY-IG spread gap keeps widening into the December meeting or stabilizes once the minutes are digested. A continued widening in HY alone, without IG following, would confirm this is a leverage-and-duration story rather than a broad credit event.

Sources: WSJ · CNBC · ABC News

UK Inflation Hits a One-Year High While the Labor Market Softens

UK CPI rose to 3.6%, a one-year high, driven by record petrol and diesel prices, even as separate data showed UK wage growth slowing and job vacancies at their lowest since 2021. StoneX framed the combination as pulling the Bank of England in opposite directions. This is a supply-side inflation print landing on top of a softening labor market, the exact mix central banks have no clean single tool to address.

Read more

UK CPI climbed to 3.6%, its highest in a year, with the Guardian, CNBC and WSJ all attributing the move to record petrol and diesel prices. That landed alongside jobs data described by Bloomberg as a mixed bag: wage growth slowing and payrolls cut, with Reuters adding that job vacancies fell to their lowest level since 2021. ABC News noted the inflation print keeps the possibility of a second straight BoE rate hike alive.

The transmission mechanism matters here because energy-driven, cost-push inflation does not respond to rate hikes the way demand-driven inflation does, yet the BoE has already signaled it may hike again regardless. That raises financing costs for UK corporates at the exact moment the labor data show wage growth, and by extension consumer demand, losing momentum, a margin squeeze from both directions rather than one the central bank can target cleanly.

The FTSE fell 1.35% today, among the weaker major indices, and Reuters Breakingviews described a sharp transatlantic divide in inflation data, meaning UK and US policy paths could diverge in restrictiveness even as both central banks face hawkish pressure. That divergence matters for GBP funding costs and for UK high-yield issuers, particularly consumer-facing names exposed simultaneously to higher input costs and softer wage-driven demand.

Watch the next UK CPI and labor prints and the BoE's next decision for whether policymakers weight the energy print over the labor softening. Gilt yields and GBP-denominated credit spreads are the cleanest read on whether markets are pricing that second straight hike StoneX flagged as live.

Sources: The Guardian · Reuters · StoneX

Nvidia Turmoil Starts to Show Up in the AI Credit Story

This matters because the AI capex cycle has been heavily debt-financed, and confidence in the equity story underwrites favorable terms for that debt. The Nasdaq's relatively smaller 0.74% decline today suggests the damage so far is contained rather than cascading.

Read more

Reuters framed the current Nvidia turmoil as marking the end of easy money for the AI trade. CNBC separately reported that a congressional commission found China's military is seeking Nvidia's advanced chips for AI capabilities, a finding issued as Nvidia has been rocked by its CEO's resignation after reports of an affair with a suspected Chinese spy. The Nasdaq fell 0.74% today, a smaller decline than the S&P's 1.92% or the Dow's 1.64%, suggesting the fallout has not broadened into a general tech selloff yet.

The mechanism runs through financing cost. AI infrastructure and data-center buildouts have been funded through a mix of leveraged loans and high-yield and investment-grade issuance, with favorable spread levels underwritten by confidence in the durability of the capex cycle and the equity premium on AI-exposed names. Headline risk around governance and a core supplier's exposure to geopolitical restriction erodes that confidence, and lenders start to reprice the risk that the buildout outlasts the demand narrative supporting it.

This lines up with the broader tape today: the Fidelity note on floating-rate loan appeal given rates are not falling, and the 11bp widening in the HY OAS to 3.34%, both point to credit becoming more discriminating about structurally leveraged, capex-heavy borrowers. IG spreads were nearly flat at 0.90% today, so the question is whether this stays contained to high-yield and leveraged loan exposure tied to AI infrastructure or starts to bleed into the investment-grade paper of the large-cap issuers funding the buildout.

Watch further Nvidia-specific news flow on succession and the China chip policy response, and watch spread performance of AI-linked high-yield and leveraged loan names relative to the broader HY index. A persistent widening gap versus the rest of high yield would confirm the market is pricing AI-specific credit risk rather than a macro rates story.

Sources: Reuters · CNBC · Fidelity Investments

That survey result lands alongside a sector tape that was actually relatively orderly: XLK's 0.91% decline was milder than the S&P 500's 1.92% drop and roughly in line with the Nasdaq's 0.74% slide, suggesting today's session was broad de-risking rather than a tech-specific flush.

The catalyst to watch is whether the BofA survey's de-grossing proves to be a durable reallocation or a short-lived flinch. Intuit and Elastic both report in the coming sessions and will offer a read on software demand that is separate from the AI capex debate, which could help the market decide whether this selloff is a capex-specific repricing or a broader tech derating. Until then, expect the sector to trade on positioning flows and survey data as much as on fundamentals.

XLV fell 1.41 percent, a smaller decline than the S&P 500's 1.92 percent or the Dow's 1.64 percent drop, giving the sector its usual defensive character on a day when the VIX jumped 18.7 percent to 22.91. The sector had no negative catalyst of its own. Instead the tape was crowded with routine FDA approval headlines spanning GSK's lung cancer drug, a Genentech combination for ESR1-mutant breast cancer, and a Bayer blood thinner. None of it moved the sector; the drawdown tracked the broader risk-off move in credit and equities.

Read more

There is no single dominant health care story today so much as a dense cluster of regulatory approvals landing on the same tape. GSK won FDA clearance for a new lung cancer drug, Genentech's giredestrant plus everolimus combination was approved for ESR1-mutant advanced breast cancer, and Bayer got the nod for a blood thinner. Separately the FDA approved a treatment for advanced pancreatic cancer and launched a new Global Regulatory Diplomacy Program aimed at speeding medicine access in lower-income markets. None of these are the kind of singular, market-moving approval that reprices a stock meaningfully on its own; collectively they read as a normal week of agency output rather than a catalyst for the sector's 1.41 percent decline.

The more interesting name-level item is Beren, whose rare pediatric disease drug won approval after passing through four different biotech owners and surviving a major trial failure. That kind of multi-owner, post-failure path to approval is the pattern lev fin desks watch closely because it shows how a single regulatory outcome can revalue a credit that previously looked impaired, independent of where the broader equity tape is trading. Outside of that, the approval slate is broad-based across large caps (GSK, Bayer) and specialty oncology, which is consistent with dispersion inside the sector even as the index-level move stayed moderate and defensive relative to the Russell's 1.43 percent and EuroStoxx's 1.42 percent declines.

Nothing in today's headlines points directly at health care issuer financing, but the macro backdrop is worth noting for positioning purposes. HY OAS widened 3.41 percent to 3.34 and IG OAS rose 1.12 percent to 0.90 on a day the VIX spiked nearly 19 percent, and health care's relative outperformance against the S&P and Dow is the classic defensive-sector response when risk appetite deteriorates broadly. For leveraged biotech and pharma services names, that combination of modestly wider spreads and a safety bid in the equity means the sector's relative cost of capital advantage over cyclicals held up even as absolute credit conditions tightened slightly across the board.

The setup from here is less about today's approvals and more about whether the broader risk-off move extends. If HY and IG spreads continue widening alongside further VIX escalation, health care's defensive characteristics should keep it a relative outperformer, but a genuine pickup in volatility would still pressure refinancing economics for leveraged health care credits even if the sector itself lags the selloff. The next real catalyst is whether any of today's approvals, particularly Beren's, gets priced into the underlying equity or credit tomorrow.
