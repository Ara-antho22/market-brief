Macro Update

CPI Day Lands on a Hawkish Fed

September CPI is due today with consensus pointing to a gasoline-driven pickup in headline inflation, while the core reading is described as the swing factor for the Fed's October decision. That data drops a day after Cleveland Fed's Hammack said higher rates could be needed to prevent an inflationary mindset from setting in. The front end is already moving in that direction, with the 2-year at 4.80% and SOFR at 3.89%.

Read more

September CPI is expected to show an acceleration tied to gasoline prices, with cpi_yoy already running at 3.35% and the index at 334.13, up 0.40% on the month. Core inflation is the number traders are watching because it is what determines whether the Fed can credibly frame any further hikes or holds as data-dependent rather than reactive. Hammack's comment that rates may need to stay higher to avoid an entrenched inflationary mindset is a direct signal that at least one voting-adjacent voice sees more work to do, not less.

This transmits through the front end first. Hawkish Fed commentary shifts the path priced into SOFR futures and short-dated Treasuries before it touches equities, which is why the 2-year (4.80%, +1.05%) and SOFR (3.89%, +0.52%) have moved more than the 10-year (5.24%, +0.38%) in relative terms. For leveraged balance sheets, that SOFR move is mechanical: floating-rate term loans and revolvers reprice off it directly, so debt service rises even without a single basis point of spread widening.

That is exactly what credit spreads are telling you right now. HY OAS is flat at 3.11 and IG OAS flat at 0.83, meaning the market is treating this as a rates story, not a credit story. The risk for leveraged issuers is that this split doesn't hold if core CPI surprises to the upside today. A hotter print would validate Hammack's framing, pull forward further front-end repricing, and start to pressure interest coverage ratios on floating-rate capital structures even as spreads stay calm.

Watch core CPI specifically, not headline. A core print in line with or below expectations would cap the front-end move and keep this a contained rates story. A beat would give the hawks more room to argue for holding rates higher into the Fed's October meeting, and that is the scenario that eventually tests whether HY OAS can stay at 3.11 if SOFR keeps climbing underneath it.

Sources: CNBC · InvestmentNews · Business Insider

China's Factory-Gate Prices Run Hottest Since 2022

China's producer price index rose at its fastest pace since June 2022 and beat expectations, while consumer prices lagged forecasts, a divergence driven by rising commodity costs rather than stronger domestic demand. That matters for US inflation watchers because producer price increases in China eventually show up in the price of imported goods. The dollar index at 121.60 and WTI at 96.24 are the two variables that determine how much of that cost gets passed through.

Read more

China's PPI rose at the fastest pace since June 2022, beating expectations, while CPI lagged forecasts according to both CNBC and Reuters reporting, with WSJ flagging the same divergence. The gap between hot producer prices and soft consumer prices points to rising input costs, commodity-driven, rather than a pickup in Chinese household demand. WTI itself was essentially flat at 96.24, up 0.11%, so the PPI beat is not being read as a crude-driven shock in the oil market itself.

The transmission channel to US assets runs through trade prices. When Chinese producer costs rise, that eventually feeds into the price of goods exported to the US, showing up in core goods CPI with a lag of a quarter or more. How much of that cost actually reaches US shelves depends on the dollar, sitting at 121.60 (+0.13%), since a stronger dollar offsets yuan-denominated cost increases when translated back into dollar terms.

For credit, this reinforces the case against a near-term dovish pivot. The IMF's warning about bond yields facing a disorderly spike tied to debt and AI-related borrowing sits alongside this story because persistent global producer-price pressure is one more reason the Fed and other central banks have less room to ease, keeping duration-heavy portfolios and long-dated credit under pressure from the rates side rather than the spread side.

The next confirmation point is whether China's CPI catches up to its PPI in coming months, which would signal the cost pressure is reaching consumers rather than staying contained in margins. Also watch whether the commodity inputs behind the PPI beat, industrial metals and energy, keep climbing, since that is the direct link between China's factory-gate数据 and US import prices.

Sources: CNBC · Reuters · WSJ

Aramco HQ Hit, But Oil Isn't Pricing Escalation Yet

Houthi forces struck Saudi Aramco's headquarters, causing what sources described to Reuters as extensive damage, and the group has vowed further attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities. Saudi stocks slipped on the news with Gulf markets mixed, but WTI barely moved, up 0.11% to 96.24. That gap between the headline and the oil tape is the thing to watch.

Read more

Reuters reported extensive damage to Aramco's headquarters from a Houthi attack, with the group vowing additional strikes on Saudi airports and oil facilities. Saudi stocks slipped in response and Gulf markets were mixed, a localized equity reaction to a headline that in isolation sounds like a major supply-side event for the world's largest oil exporter.

The oil market is drawing a distinction that matters. WTI moved just 0.11% to 96.24, which tells you traders are treating this as damage to corporate infrastructure rather than a hit on actual production or export capacity. Crude prices respond to barrels taken offline, not to headquarters buildings, so the muted reaction is the market pricing probability of escalation rather than confirmed supply loss.

The credit transmission channel here runs through energy-sector HY and Gulf sovereign risk rather than broad market spreads. HY OAS is flat at 3.11, meaning none of this has yet repriced risk premium in energy credit. If the Houthis follow through on strikes against actual export infrastructure, ports, pipelines, or loading terminals rather than offices, that is the scenario where oil-linked HY and Saudi sovereign spreads would move quickly, and WTI would stop sitting still at 96.24.

The specific thing to watch is the target list, not the rhetoric. A strike on production or export facilities would be the confirming event for a real oil risk premium; continued hits on corporate or administrative targets would support the current read that this stays a geopolitical headline rather than a supply shock priced into crude or credit.

Sources: Reuters · Reuters · Reuters

Equities outrun a flat crude tape

The gap is being driven by headline risk rather than the spot benchmark: reported attacks on ships in the Strait of Hormuz and the Saudi-Houthi conflict are keeping a geopolitical premium bid under energy equities. Reuters reported Saudi-led OPEC+ output actually rose in September despite Iran-war disruption, which helps explain why WTI itself isn't moving much. Occidental flagged higher energy prices benefiting its Q3 results, giving the equity move an earnings-linked underpinning as well.

Read more

The dominant story is a disconnect between the equity tape and the crude tape. Headlines cited reported attacks on ships in the Strait of Hormuz and an active Saudi-Houthi conflict, both framed as live supply-disruption risks, and WSJ attributed the energy stock rally directly to conflicts pushing up oil prices. Yet WTI closed at 96.24, up just 0.11%, a flat session by crude's own standards. Reuters' OPEC+ data point is the reconciling fact: Saudi Arabia led a jump in group output in September despite the Iran-war backdrop, suggesting the physical market has so far absorbed the disruption risk even as the headline flow intensifies.

Within the sector, the dispersion is best explained by earnings positioning rather than a uniform commodity repricing. Occidental said higher energy prices are set to help its Q3 results, a direct read-through for upstream-heavy names inside XLE. OilPrice.com's rundown of shale names to watch ahead of Q3 reporting points to the same dynamic, producers being bid on the assumption that realized prices track the forward curve (FXEmpire flagged Brent testing $107 and WTI eyeing 96.57) rather than today's flat spot print. That is consistent with equities moving roughly sixteen times the percentage gain of the underlying commodity.

Credit markets gave no signal that this is a stress event for the sector. HY OAS was unchanged at 3.11 and IG OAS unchanged at 0.83, flat spreads despite the conflict headlines and the size of the equity move. That argues the rally is sentiment and earnings-driven rather than a credit-market repricing of geopolitical risk, and it means E&P and oilfield-services issuers are not seeing any incremental cost-of-capital pressure from today's headlines, regardless of how the Strait of Hormuz story develops.

The catalyst to watch is whether the Strait of Hormuz reports and the Saudi-Houthi conflict escalate into an actual physical supply interruption, which would move WTI itself rather than just equity sentiment, or whether the Saudi-led September output increase is the more durable signal that OPEC+ supply is keeping pace with disruption risk. Q3 earnings from Occidental and the shale names flagged by OilPrice.com will be the near-term test of whether the realized-price assumptions behind today's equity move actually show up in results.

Real Estate XLRE -0.21%

Office market splits as rates bite

XLRE eased 0.21% to 41.83, a modest move against a 10-year yield that pushed up to 5.24% and a 2-year that rose to 4.80%. The real estate tape is less about the index print today and more about a widening gap in office fundamentals, with a $80 million San Jose campus default and a Naperville distressed auction sitting next to recovery headlines out of Cincinnati and Dublin. Credit spreads were flat, with HY OAS unchanged at 3.11 and IG OAS at 0.83, so today's office stress is reading as asset-specific rather than a systemic repricing.

Read more

The office subsector is telling two stories at once. A North San Jose office campus fell into default on an $80 million loan, and a distressed Naperville property hit the auction block as a potential redevelopment play, both pointing to continued stress in secondary and suburban office stock where occupiers have not come back. Against that, Cushman & Wakefield's national data shows sublease space shrinking and new construction at a multi-decade low, Downtown Cincinnati posted what local brokers called a 'blistering quarter,' and Dublin's office vacancy is falling on AI-driven leasing demand. Both halves of this story are true simultaneously: the supply-side backdrop is tightening nationally even as specific assets in weaker locations default.

The dispersion is geographic and vintage-driven rather than sector-wide. San Jose and Naperville are both secondary-market, pre-pandemic-vintage office assets, the kind most exposed to remote-work vacancy and the hardest to refinance at today's rates. Cincinnati, Louisville, and Dublin are showing up on the other side of the ledger, benefiting from either return-to-office momentum or AI-sector tenant demand that is absorbing space faster than new supply can come on. That split is consistent with the broader CRE narrative this cycle: it is not office as an asset class that is distressed, it is specific buildings in specific submarkets with specific tenant rosters.

The financing backdrop makes the weaker assets harder to rescue. Mortgage rates have moved back above 7%, UST2 rose 1.05% to 4.80%, and SOFR ticked up to 3.89%, all of which raise the bar for refinancing loans like the one behind the San Jose default. With construction starts already at multi-decade lows per Cushman & Wakefield, the supply side is self-correcting, but that does not help an owner facing a maturity wall today at a materially higher cost of capital than when the loan was underwritten. Flat HY and IG spreads suggest the broader credit market is not yet pricing contagion from these defaults, treating them as idiosyncratic rather than systemic.

The variable to watch is whether more special-servicer disclosures follow the San Jose and Naperville pattern as 2026 maturities come due, which would test whether the Cushman recovery narrative is broad enough to absorb distressed secondary-market supply or whether it is concentrated in a handful of stronger metros. A reversal in the 10-year back toward 5% or lower would meaningfully ease refinancing math across the board; absent that, expect the bifurcation between recovering urban cores and struggling suburban office to widen rather than close.

The setup from here hinges on whether the margin trap thesis shows up in actual Q4 prints or stays a commentary piece. If promotional intensity is as heavy as the Chain Drug Review piece suggests, watch for gross margin misses alongside in-line or better top-line numbers when holiday results land, which is exactly the pattern that would separate off-price winners from mid-tier discretionary names relying on discounting to move units. The Fed cash-use survey is worth tracking as a leading indicator into that print, since a consumer that says it would rather pay down debt than spend extra cash is not a consumer likely to bail out a soft holiday season.
