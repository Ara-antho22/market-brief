Morning Edition

Tuesday, October 13, 2026

Newsletter Summary

Equities closed another quiet session: the S&P 500 slipped 0.06% to 7,807.24, the Nasdaq fell 0.44% to 27,245.90, while the Dow added 0.38% to 51,850.55. The VIX dropped 3.7% to 14.84, a level that signals no systemic stress despite a backdrop of hawkish Fed rhetoric, 3.35% year-over-year CPI, and unemployment ticking up to 4.20% (+2.44%). The 10-year Treasury yield eased to 5.22% (-1.14%) even as Fed's Hammack argued rates may need to stay higher to prevent an inflationary mindset from taking hold. Credit spreads widened modestly, with HY OAS up 1.94% to 3.15 against a flat IG OAS of 0.82, a gap that still reflects orderly risk pricing rather than stress. Oil held near $96.24 (WTI, +0.11%) despite fresh reporting on Iran fortifying nuclear sites and a Houthi attack intercepted near Riyadh, as Russian diesel supply prospects capped the geopolitical premium. For the desk, the setup is a long bond still above 5%, a labor market showing cracks, and a Fed unwilling to declare victory on inflation, which argues for care on duration and new-issue timing rather than any dramatic repricing today.

Macro Update

Hammack Keeps the Door Open to More Hikes as Unemployment Ticks Higher

Fed's Hammack said higher rates could still be needed to avoid an inflationary mindset taking hold, favoring gradual tweaks over aggressive moves, even as unemployment rose to 4.20% (+2.44%) and CPI held at 3.35% year-over-year. The 2-year at 4.75% (-0.42%) shows the front end still isn't pricing imminent relief. This is the classic late-cycle tension for leveraged credit: policy staying restrictive while labor cracks start to show.

Read more

Hammack's comments reiterated a preference for gradual rate adjustments to keep inflation expectations anchored, landing alongside a CPI index reading of 334.13 (+0.40% month-over-month, 3.35% year-over-year) and an unemployment rate that rose 2.44% to 4.20%. The 2-year Treasury yield at 4.75% ticked down only 0.42%, a sign the market isn't yet pricing a near-term pivot despite the softer labor print.

The transmission here runs through the front end directly into leveraged balance sheets. SOFR sitting flat at 3.87% means floating-rate loan costs aren't falling even as the labor backdrop softens, so interest coverage ratios on floating-rate capital structures stay pinned at currently restrictive levels rather than getting relief from a Fed pivot.

HY OAS widened 1.94% to 3.15 against an unchanged IG OAS of 0.82, a modest but directional move that matters more for what it signals than its size. Brookfield Credit's positioning toward income and distressed opportunities on the view that rate cuts will keep failing to materialize is a direct read-through: private credit is pricing for a higher-for-longer front end extending the cost of capital for sponsor-backed issuers.

Watch the CPI print due this week, which Morningstar and Equiti Global coverage frames as the swing factor for keeping the Fed on hold versus validating Hammack's hawkish lean. A soft CPI surprise would ease pressure on the front end; a second consecutive month of rising unemployment without inflation cooling would harden the stagflationary read and keep HY spreads drifting wider.

Sources: CNBC · TradingView · Alternatives Watch

IMF Flags Disorderly Yield Risk as the 10-Year Sits Above 5%

The IMF warned that bond yields face risk of a disorderly spike tied to debt loads and AI-driven capital demand, even as the 10-year eased to 5.22% (-1.14%) today. A Council on Foreign Relations piece notes US borrowing costs keep climbing despite Treasury's efforts, and commentary is framing the roughly $40 trillion federal debt stock as both a known risk and a potential shock. Today's dip in yield is a pause, not a reversal of the underlying trend these pieces describe.

Read more

The IMF's warning centers on the combination of elevated government debt issuance and AI-related capital demand straining bond market absorption capacity, a theme echoed by CFR's framing of persistently rising borrowing costs despite Treasury debt-management efforts and commentary describing the roughly $40 trillion debt stock as a gray rhino now carrying black-swan tail risk. The 10-year yield's modest 1.14% decline to 5.22% today sits against that backdrop rather than reversing it, with the dollar index also softer at 121.38 (-0.33%).

The mechanism is a term-premium story: when issuance supply and demand for AI-linked capital compete with Treasury auctions, dealers require more compensation to hold duration, which shows up as a higher long-end yield independent of the Fed's policy rate. That higher base cost of long-dated capital flows straight into refinancing coupons for any issuer terming out debt at the long end of the curve, regardless of credit quality.

For credit, the bifurcation matters: IG OAS is flat at 0.82, showing high-grade issuance still clears without stress, while HY OAS has widened to 3.15. A sustained climb in the risk-free long end raises the all-in cost of refinancing leveraged structures even if spreads hold, which is why private credit platforms like Brookfield are positioning toward income and distressed strategies rather than assuming a rate-cut rescue. KKR's recent commentary on financing compute buildouts is a reminder that AI capex is becoming a direct, incremental draw on the same capital pool competing with Treasury supply.

Watch upcoming Treasury auction results and any follow-on IMF or Fed commentary on debt sustainability for confirmation. A reversal would show up as auctions clearing with shrinking tails and falling term premium; continued upward drift in the 10-year alongside widening HY OAS would confirm the disorderly-spike risk the IMF is flagging.

Sources: Wealth Professional · Council on Foreign Relations · China Daily Global Edition · KKR: A Leading Global Investment Firm

Saudi Airport Attacks and Iran Nuclear Buildup Fail to Move Oil

Iran is reportedly fortifying nuclear sites ahead of possible US strikes after the midterms, a Saudi-led coalition intercepted another Houthi attack on Riyadh, and Qatar Airways suspended Riyadh and Dammam flights. Despite that, WTI rose just 0.11% to $96.24 as Russian diesel supply prospects offset the Gulf risk premium. The muted price action is itself the signal: the market is treating this as a supply-substitution story, not a disruption story, for now.

Read more

CNBC reported Iran is fortifying nuclear sites in anticipation of possible US strikes after the midterms, while a Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted another Houthi attack targeting Riyadh and Qatar Airways suspended flights to Riyadh and Dammam as airport attacks disrupted regional air travel. Against that backdrop, WTI moved just 0.11% to $96.24, with Reuters attributing the restraint to Russian diesel supply prospects counterbalancing the Gulf supply risk.

Normally geopolitical escalation in the Gulf flows through oil into inflation expectations and breakevens, which then feeds the Fed's reaction function. The fact that WTI barely moved indicates traders see Russian diesel as a workable substitute for any Gulf-driven disruption, which is keeping the risk premium embedded in the price near zero despite the headlines.

A contained oil price matters directly to the inflation debate Hammack raised this week: with WTI roughly flat, the energy component of CPI isn't adding fresh upward pressure, which is one less variable pushing the Fed toward the higher-for-longer stance it's already signaling. Credit exposure is idiosyncratic rather than systemic for now, concentrated in travel-linked names like Qatar Airways facing route disruption rather than broad energy-sector spread widening.

The thing to watch is whether reporting on Iran's fortification efforts turns into an actual strike scenario after the midterms, or whether Houthi attack frequency on Saudi infrastructure escalates enough to overwhelm the Russian diesel offset. A sustained break of WTI above $100 would start to bite breakevens and complicate the Fed's inflation-mindset argument in a way today's flat tape does not.

Sources: CNBC · Reuters · Reuters

Rates ease, offices keep diverging

The office story remains two-tracked: Manhattan vacancy fell to 12.6%, its lowest since before 2020, and Cincinnati and Dublin both posted tightening leasing markets, while a distressed Naperville office asset is headed to auction and Seattle trails its gateway peers. Read more

The session's real estate trade was mostly a rates story. The 10-year yield dropped 1.14% to 5.22% and the 2-year eased to 4.75%, compressing the discount rate applied to REIT cash flows and giving the sector a 0.74% gain on a day when the S&P was flat and the Nasdaq fell 0.44%. Layered on top of that macro tailwind is a persistent bifurcation in office fundamentals: Manhattan vacancy hit 12.6%, the lowest print since before 2020 according to Yahoo Finance, Cincinnati's office market just posted what local brokers called a 'blistering quarter' with a literal 'we're back' quote, and Dublin's vacancy rate is falling as AI-related funding drives leasing demand. Against that, a distressed Naperville office property is heading to auction as a redevelopment play, and Seattle's office metrics continue to trail other gateway markets.

The dispersion is squarely a quality and location story rather than a sector-wide recovery. The financing read-through is that lower long rates are doing more work for REIT balance sheets than credit spreads are. That matters most for levered office owners: cheaper long-term financing eases refinancing math for well-located, high-occupancy assets like those in Manhattan or Cincinnati, but it does not resolve the underwriting problem for suburban offices financed at pre-2022 cap rates, which is exactly why an asset like Naperville is being pushed to auction rather than refinanced.

From here, the setup favors continued dispersion rather than a sector-wide re-rating. A further decline in the 10-year would extend the REIT bid, but the office story will keep splitting on occupancy data rather than rates alone. Watch for additional gateway leasing reports to confirm whether Manhattan and Cincinnati's tightening is broadening, and watch the pace of distressed suburban office sales for evidence of whether more owners follow Naperville's lead in capitulating to redevelopment rather than holding.
