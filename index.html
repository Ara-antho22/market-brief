Monday, November 2, 2026

Newsletter Summary

The S&P 500 closed essentially flat at 7,743.98 (+0.28%), but that number is hiding real damage underneath: the Nasdaq fell 1.38% to 26,184.72, the Russell 2000 dropped 1.65% to 279.33, and the Dow lost 1.09%. This sets up a loaded week: an FOMC decision, a Bank of England rate call, the October jobs report, US midterm elections, and Apple's earnings all land before Friday's close.

Macro Update

HY Spreads Widen Nearly 4% as Credit Reasserts Itself

The HY OAS jumped 3.82% to 3.26 and the IG OAS rose 3.49% to 0.89, the sharpest daily spread moves in the dataset, even as the HYG ETF slipped just 0.19%. FT's "bloodbath" framing and Man Group's note that credit's margin for error has narrowed point to the same thing: spread compensation is reasserting itself after a long grind tighter. This is the number that matters most for a leveraged finance desk because new issue pricing and refi math reprice directly off OAS, not off the index headline.

Read more

The HY OAS widened to 3.26, up 3.82% on the day, while the IG OAS rose 3.49% to 0.89. The move happened alongside a Treasury rally, with the 10-year yield down 1.39% to 4.98%, so spread widening occurred against a falling risk-free rate rather than alongside rising yields. The HYG ETF itself only dipped 0.19%, a gap between the cash spread index and the fund price that typically reflects the ETF lagging the move in less liquid underlying bonds.

The mechanism is straightforward: OAS measures compensation over Treasuries, and when it widens even as the risk-free rate falls, the signal is credit-specific deterioration in risk appetite, not a rates story. For leveraged finance desks this transmits directly into underwriting economics. Any LBO debt or refinancing priced off current spread levels now carries a higher coupon relative to the base rate than it would have a day earlier, which either compresses equity returns for sponsors or forces wider original issue discount on new paper.

Walker & Dunlop's framing of a refi wave as a "golden age for credit" depends on spreads staying contained, so a widening move like this is the kind of thing that could blunt that thesis if it persists. On the other side, Carlyle and KKR are reportedly positioning to buy loans at a discount, which suggests opportunistic capital views this as a buyable dislocation rather than the start of a systemic repricing. Hartnett's call to buy tech bonds despite relative returns hitting a 30-year low adds a further wrinkle, implying some allocators see this spread widening as indiscriminate rather than warranted by fundamentals.

Watch whether HY OAS keeps widening through the FOMC decision this week or stabilizes once rate uncertainty clears. A break lower in HYG alongside continued OAS widening would confirm this is becoming a genuine risk-off move in credit rather than a one-day spread adjustment. Carlyle and KKR actually deploying capital into the loan market in size would be the clearest signal that private credit views current levels as attractive rather than a warning sign.

Sources: Financial Times · Trustnet · PERE · Bloomberg · investingLive

Nasdaq and Russell Lead the Selloff While S&P Holds Flat

The Nasdaq fell 1.38% and the Russell 2000 dropped 1.65%, dragging the Dow down 1.09%, while the S&P 500 managed a 0.28% gain, a divergence that points to a rotation out of AI-linked growth names and small caps rather than a broad risk-off. The VIX rose a modest 1.71% to 17.20, consistent with position adjustment rather than panic. Coverage framing this as the AI trade being repriced around a harder question lines up with a week stacked with Apple earnings and AI bellwethers AMD and Palantir.

Read more

The index-level divergence is the headline fact: S&P 500 +0.28% to 7,743.98 against Nasdaq -1.38% to 26,184.72 and Russell 2000 -1.65% to 279.33. The Dow's 1.09% decline sits between the two, suggesting the damage is concentrated in higher-multiple growth and smaller, more financing-sensitive names rather than spread evenly across the market. The VIX's move to 17.20 is a small uptick by its own standards and does not support language stronger than a routine volatility adjustment.

The mechanism connecting this to the credit widening in the prior entry is valuation and financing sensitivity. The Nasdaq's AI-linked constituents carry elevated multiples that behave like long-duration assets, so when credit conditions tighten even modestly, as the HY OAS move suggests, those names reprice first and hardest. The Russell's small caps are more directly exposed to floating-rate financing costs, and with SOFR firming to 3.95%, their cost of capital is moving the wrong way even as Treasury yields fall.

The second-order read ties directly into the credit story: if wider spreads are hitting the balance sheets of smaller, more leveraged companies first, that is a plausible explanation for the Russell's underperformance relative to the S&P. Hartnett's observation that tech bond relative returns have hit a 30-year low frames the broader debate, whether AI-related debt-funded capex is now facing genuine scrutiny on return on investment rather than being underwritten on momentum alone.

The confirming or disconfirming data this week is earnings, not macro prints. Apple reports, along with AMD and Palantir as AI demand bellwethers, and strong results that justify current capex and multiples would argue this is a one or two day rotation. Continued underperformance in Nasdaq and Russell names through the jobs report and FOMC decision would suggest the market is genuinely repricing the AI trade rather than just digesting a busy calendar.

Yields Fall Into the Fed Decision as Warsh Reframes the Debt-Cost Problem

The 10-year yield fell 1.39% to 4.98% and the 2-year fell 1.28% to 4.61% heading into this week's FOMC decision, even as SOFR firmed to 3.95%. Kevin Warsh's Fortune piece argues the Fed is now managing debt-servicing costs rather than inflation, with interest rates at risk of topping GDP growth. CPI remains elevated at 3.72% year over year while unemployment rose 2.44% to 4.20%, a combination the Fed has to reconcile against a backdrop where the Bank of England is also expected to move on rates this week.

Read more

The Treasury curve moved lower across the board, 10-year down 1.39% to 4.98% and 2-year down 1.28% to 4.61%, while SOFR, the floating-rate benchmark, actually rose 0.25% to 3.95%. CPI sits at 3.72% year over year with the index itself up 0.65%, and unemployment rose 2.44% to 4.20%. The Futubull headline framing this week's event as "the Fed holds steady" suggests the FOMC decision itself may be a non-event on the policy rate, with the real signal coming from forward guidance language.

Warsh's argument matters mechanically because it reframes what moves long-end yields. If the market believes the Fed's reaction function is constrained by the cost of financing a growing deficit rather than purely by the inflation mandate, term premium behaves differently than a textbook Taylor Rule would suggest, yields can fall on growth concerns even while inflation stays sticky at 3.72%, because the perceived policy path is shaped as much by fiscal sustainability as by the dual mandate.

For the leveraged finance seat, the relevant transmission is that SOFR, not the 10-year, sets the floor for floating-rate leveraged loan coupons, so a SOFR move to 3.95% raises the cost of capital on new and existing floating-rate debt even as benchmark Treasury yields decline. Combined with the HY and IG spread widening covered above, that means all-in borrowing costs for leveraged issuers are moving higher on two fronts simultaneously, the spread and the base rate, even though the headline Treasury yield is falling. The Bank of England's expected rate move this week adds a cross-currency dimension for any issuer financing in sterling or relying on UK-based demand.

The thing to watch is the FOMC statement language on how it weighs the 3.72% CPI print against the rise in unemployment to 4.20%, since that tension is exactly what Warsh's debt-cost framing predicts will complicate the decision. A dovish tilt that acknowledges labor softening would confirm the market's lower-yield move as appropriate; a hawkish hold focused on sticky inflation would argue the Treasury rally into the decision got ahead of itself. The October jobs report later this week is the next hard data point that will either support or undercut whichever signal the Fed sends.

Sources: Fortune · Futubull Community · Reuters · The Real Economy Blog

Five separate FDA approvals hit the tape in a single session: Inovio's Voceryq, GSK's functional cure for hepatitis B, Takeda's orexin-receptor drug, a newly cleared treatment for advanced pancreatic cancer, and Sun Pharma's Ilumya, a clinic-administered IL-23 inhibitor for psoriatic arthritis. There is nothing in today's headlines pointing to a financing or capital structure story in health care specifically. The more relevant read for this seat today is regulatory and policy exposure: a dense approval calendar reduces single-name binary risk premium for the names that cleared, which is a valuation tailwind rather than a balance sheet one.

The setup from here depends on whether health care's relative strength is a genuine defensive rotation or simply a quieter sector on a day when growth names were sold hard. With the 10-year down to 4.98% and the VIX up modestly to 17.20, the move looks consistent with a classic flight to quality rather than anything health care-specific breaking down or improving. Watch whether XLV holds its bid if equities stabilize tomorrow, since a defensive rotation that persists even as the broader tape calms would say more about sustained sector preference than today's print alone can confirm.

Industrials underperformed the broader tape by a wide margin today, down 1.60% against a S&P 500 that actually closed higher, and the gap lines up with a session where every other growth-sensitive asset moved the same direction: gold down 3.47%, WTI down 1.79%, the Russell down 1.65%, and the Nasdaq down 1.38%. That pattern points to a cyclical-sector risk-off day rather than anything industrials-specific, with the sector's beta to growth expectations doing the work. If the Bain thesis holds, the constraint on defense capex is supply chain and execution capacity, not the cost or availability of debt, which is a more benign setup for leverage in the space than a capital-scarcity narrative would imply.

The near-term setup is a market-wide risk-off session landing disproportionately on a high-beta sector, with no industrials-specific news flow heavy enough to argue for a durable repricing. Watch whether the Pentagon memo produces concrete contract or budget detail, and whether the Air Force's propulsion industry day generates actual RFPs, since either would convert today's policy-themed headlines into something the sector can trade on. Absent that, follow-through depends on whether the broader risk-off (gold, oil, the Nasdaq) extends or fades, since industrials have been trading as a high-beta proxy for that move rather than on their own fundamentals today.
