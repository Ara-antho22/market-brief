Newsletter Summary

The Fed held its policy rate steady, and equities took it in stride, with the S&P up 0.28% to 7,743.98, the Nasdaq up 0.27%, and the Dow up 0.19%, while the Nikkei outran the rest of the complex with a 1.10% gain. The real action was underneath the index tape. Treasury yields split on the decision, with the 10-year easing slightly to 4.99% while the 2-year rose to 4.70%, a flattening move that WSJ framed as yields heading in different directions. Oil fell 1.79% to $103.37 on hopes for Middle East diplomatic de-escalation, and a Japan Times report that Asia-led buyers have cut Treasury purchases by 60% sits uneasily against a federal debt stock that ABC News pegs above $40 trillion. None of this is a dramatic session by its own standards, but the cross-currents, a hold with inflation still hot, credit spreads grinding tighter, and foreign demand for Treasuries fading, are the ones that matter for financing conditions into year end.

Macro Update

Fed Holds, Curve Splits, Warsh Briefs the Press

The Fed left its policy rate unchanged despite inflation still running at 3.72% year over year, and Chair Warsh held a press briefing afterward per PBS. The bond market did not read the hold as uniformly dovish or hawkish, the 10-year yield eased to 4.99% while the 2-year rose to 4.70%, a flattening move WSJ flagged as yields moving in different directions. Business Insider reported dissents at the meeting and a change to the meeting schedule, both signals that the committee is not unified on the path from here.

Read more

The Fed's decision to hold rates steady arrived alongside NYT and NBC headlines explicitly noting the central bank is doing so despite elevated and rising inflation, with CPI up 3.72% year over year and the CPI index itself up 0.65% on the month. Unemployment ticked up 2.44% to 4.20%, giving the Fed a labor-side argument for patience even as the inflation data argues the other way. Warsh briefed reporters after the decision, and Business Insider's post-mortem flagged dissents within the committee along with a change to the Fed's meeting schedule, both details that point to a less unanimous read on the forward path than the headline hold suggests.

The mechanism for the yield split is that the front end of the curve tracks the near-term rate path and the signal from an elevated-inflation hold is that cuts are not imminent, which is consistent with the 2-year rising to 4.70%. The long end instead reflects the market's read on where inflation and growth settle over a multi-year horizon, and a Fed that holds without panicking can lower the term premium embedded in 10-year paper, consistent with the modest drift down to 4.99%. SOFR easing 0.25% to 3.94% in the same session shows overnight funding costs loosening at the margin even as the 2-year, which prices the policy path more directly, moved the other way.

For leveraged finance, a flattening curve with funding costs easing at the short end is a mixed signal. Floating rate borrowers benefit directly from the SOFR move, which feeds into loan and private credit coupon resets, while fixed-rate HY issuers still have to clear deals against a 10-year anchored near 5%, keeping all-in coupons elevated even as spreads compress. The NYT's framing that gradualism risks a catch-up problem later is the read to keep in mind if the inflation data does not cooperate, since a Fed forced to tighten after the fact would hit both ends of the curve and credit spreads simultaneously.

The next tell is whether the dissents reported by Business Insider show up explicitly in the minutes, and whether the changed meeting schedule compresses or extends the runway before the next decision. Watch the next CPI print against the 3.72% year over year base and whether the 2s10s spread continues to flatten or reverses once the market has more data to work with.

Sources: The New York Times · WSJ · PBS · Business Insider

Spreads Tighten While AI Credit Warnings Pile Up

HY OAS tightened 3.64% to 318bps and IG OAS tightened 2.22% to 88bps, with the VIX down 4.73% to 17.52, a broadly risk-on credit session. That move came alongside Reuters commentary warning that private credit's AI footprint obscures growing risks and a Bloomberg piece titled Chickens Are Coming Home to Roost in Credit. Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly dismissed AI financing fears per Businesskorea, even as the warnings kept stacking up.

Read more

Both high yield and investment grade spreads compressed in the same session that the Fed held rates, with HY OAS down to 318bps from roughly 330bps and IG OAS down to 88bps, moves that are modest in absolute terms but directionally consistent with the VIX drop to 17.52. The spread tightening occurred against a backdrop of Reuters commentary specifically flagging that private credit's exposure to AI infrastructure financing is obscuring buildup risk, and a separate Bloomberg piece using the chickens-coming-home-to-roost framing to describe credit market conditions. Businesskorea reported that Bessent dismissed the AI financing concerns directly, even as the warning pieces ran in the same news cycle.

Spread compression alongside falling implied volatility is typically a search-for-yield signal, lower near-term macro uncertainty after the Fed removed one source of event risk by holding steady, combined with SOFR easing to 3.94%, which lowers the cost of carry for levered credit buyers. For new issue leveraged loans and HY bonds, tighter OAS translates directly into cheaper all-in coupons at a moment when private credit funds and BDCs are reportedly expanding exposure to AI-linked infrastructure financing, the exact exposure the Reuters piece flagged as under-scrutinized.

The tension for a lev fin seat is that underwriting conditions are easing, cheap spreads, low vol, compressed OAS, in the same window that multiple outlets are independently warning about concentration risk in AI-related private credit. That is a classic late-cycle divergence where price action and risk commentary move in opposite directions, and it matters because private credit documentation and marks are less transparent than syndicated loan or HY bond pricing, so spread tightening in public markets does not necessarily confirm underlying credit quality in the private vehicles funding the same capex cycle.

Watch new HY and leveraged loan issuance pricing this week for whether all-in yields are clearing tighter than the OAS move alone would suggest, and watch for any BDC or private credit fund commentary that either confirms or pushes back on the Reuters and Bloomberg warnings. A reversal in the VIX or a re-widening in HY OAS without a clear macro trigger would be the first sign the AI-financing concern is moving from commentary into price.

Sources: Reuters · Bloomberg · Businesskorea

Treasury Demand Shrinks While Oil Eases on Diplomacy Hopes

The Japan Times reported that U.S.-aligned countries, led by Asia, have slashed Treasury purchases by 60%, a demand-side question sitting over a federal debt stock that ABC News says has topped $40 trillion. WTI fell 1.79% to $103.37 on Reuters reporting that diplomacy hopes are outweighing Middle East escalation fears, even as South Korea separately said it is considering deployment options to the Strait of Hormuz. Both stories bear directly on how Treasury supply gets absorbed and what risk premium sits in the crude complex.

Read more

Japan Times reporting that Asia-led, U.S.-aligned buyers have cut Treasury purchases by 60% lands against the ABC News figure that federal debt has crossed $40 trillion, meaning a shrinking pool of traditional foreign official demand now has to be absorbed elsewhere even as issuance needs grow. Separately, WTI fell 1.79% to $103.37, with Reuters attributing the move to hopes for diplomatic de-escalation in the Middle East outweighing the risk of further conflict, a read complicated by South Korea's own report that it is weighing military deployment options to the Strait of Hormuz.

A shrinking foreign official bid for Treasuries matters because it shifts the marginal buyer toward domestic real money and primary dealer balance sheets, which tend to demand more term premium to hold duration, a dynamic consistent with the 10-year's stickiness near 5% even as the Fed held rates. Oil's pullback works through a more direct channel, lower crude feeds into headline CPI with a lag, a modest offset to the 3.72% year-over-year inflation print that the Fed is currently weighing, though a 1.79% single-day move is not large enough on its own to change the inflation trajectory.

For credit investors, weaker foreign Treasury demand raises the structural cost of financing the debt stock over time, since Treasury yields set the floor for both IG and HY coupon resets and for the repo and SOFR plumbing that leveraged credit relies on for short-term funding, which is part of why SOFR's small move to 3.94% is worth tracking alongside the 2s10s shift discussed above. A sustained decline in oil would be a mild tailwind for energy-intensive HY issuers in chemicals and industrials, but the Hormuz deployment headline is a reminder that the de-escalation read in crude is not yet a settled call.

Watch the results and indirect bidder participation at the next Treasury auction for confirmation of the Japan Times demand story, and watch whether the oil move holds or reverses if Hormuz-related tensions escalate further. A sustained widening in Treasury term premium alongside a reversal in crude would be the combination that most directly pressures HY and IG issuance costs into year end.

Sources: The Japan Times · ABC News · Reuters · Reuters

The headline tension in energy today is the gap between spot crude, which dropped 1.79% to $103.37, and the sector ETF, which still closed up 0.35%. That divergence is being driven by the refining side of the business rather than the upstream price itself. Sinopec's 57% year-over-year profit jump came specifically from higher oil prices and refining margins, and that read-through matters for how investors are pricing integrated and downstream names even as crude itself softened on the session. The EIA's report of falling crude inventories alongside rising gasoline stocks adds nuance: crude draws usually support price, but gasoline builds suggest refiners are running hard and supply is keeping pace with demand at the pump.

Capital discipline is the other thread tying the tape together. Noble's CEO made the point directly: higher oil prices have not translated into more drilling, which keeps the supply response muted and should, in theory, support realized prices and margins for existing production even as headline crude chops around. That discipline narrative is also why the equity reaction to a nearly 2% crude pullback was muted rather than sharp. Eni showing up as a sell-side 'Bull of the Day' pick is consistent with the same thesis playing out in the integrateds, where refining and chemicals offset upstream volatility.

Credit conditions are a tailwind the sector is not fighting today. High-yield spreads tightened 3.64% to an OAS of 3.18 and investment-grade spreads tightened 2.22% to 0.88, continuing a broad risk-on tone alongside the VIX's 4.73% drop to 17.52. Energy issuers, many of them high-yield rated in the E&P and oilfield-services space, benefit directly from tighter spreads when it comes to refinancing near-term maturities or funding incremental capex, even if that capex appetite remains deliberately restrained per the Noble commentary.

The setup from here hinges on whether crude's pullback is noise or the start of a trend, since XLE's resilience today assumes the refining-margin and capital-discipline story holds even if WTI keeps drifting lower. Watch the next EIA inventory print for confirmation on whether the gasoline build was a one-off or the start of a demand softening, and watch whether Sinopec's margin strength shows up in commentary from U.S. refiners as a read-across. A sustained break below $100 WTI would start to test the thesis that equity multiples can hold up independent of spot crude.

Microsoft's capex cut was the session's central data point, and the Financial Times framed it as a supply problem rather than a demand one: the company blamed data center delays, not a pullback in AI appetite, for the lower spending forecast. That distinction mattered to how the stock traded, because it arrived alongside Azure revenue up 48% to $33.2 billion, a growth rate that does not support a thesis of cooling enterprise cloud demand. The dispersion inside the AI trade is what stands out today. The next catalyst is whether Microsoft's delay language shows up as an isolated supply bottleneck or becomes a pattern across the other hyperscalers reporting capex plans. Watch whether Azure's growth rate holds even as the capacity constraint persists, since a deceleration there would validate the Seeking Alpha argument that the broader economy is not fully hostage to the AI capex cycle, versus a scenario where chip names sell off on confirmation that the inflection point NAI500 flagged is actually arriving.

HHS loosens the door on compounded peptides

XLV added 0.30%, basically tracking the broader tape rather than trading on its own catalyst. The one sector-specific headline flow of substance is HHS under RFK Jr. moving to let pharmacies compound and temporarily sell six previously unapproved peptides, a regulatory loosening covered across Reuters, NPR, CBS, The Washington Post, STAT and ABC. Separately, Michigan regulators approved 2027 rate hikes of up to 25% for health insurers, a data point on medical cost trend repricing in managed care. Neither story moved the sector materially today, but both carry multi-quarter read-through for pricing power in peptide therapeutics and insurer margins respectively.

Read more

HHS released a draft policy, confirmed across Reuters, NPR, CBS News, The Washington Post and STAT, that opens access to six unapproved peptides through pharmacy compounding, something the agency is framing as a wellness access expansion rather than a formal FDA approval pathway. The practical effect is that compounding pharmacies can now legally produce and sell versions of these peptides outside the standard FDA review and exclusivity framework, at least on a temporary basis while the policy is finalized.

This is a two-sided story for the peptide and metabolic-disease complex. Compounding pharmacies and the direct-to-consumer telehealth channels that distribute peptide products gain an explicit regulatory green light they did not have before, which is incrementally positive for volume in that channel. Branded manufacturers of FDA-approved peptide therapeutics face the opposite exposure, since cheaper compounded alternatives operating with lighter regulatory friction compete for the same patient dollar, even though today's headlines do not name specific companies or quantify any volume shift.

Nothing in today's flow points to a financing or credit story for health care, so the more relevant angle is regulatory exposure and pricing power. The Michigan rate approvals, up to 25% for 2027 plans per The Detroit News, show state regulators continuing to let insurers reprice for medical cost trend rather than cap increases, which is margin-supportive for carriers with exchange exposure in that market even as it raises affordability questions for enrollees. The peptide compounding shift is the mirror image: a regulatory decision that transfers some pricing power away from branded manufacturers toward lower-cost compounded supply.

The near-term catalyst is whether HHS finalizes this peptide compounding policy as drafted or narrows it under pressure from branded manufacturers, and whether FDA raises safety or manufacturing-quality objections given compounded products bypass standard approval review. On the insurer side, watch whether other states follow Michigan's lead on 2027 rate filings, since a pattern of approved double-digit increases would be the clearer signal that cost trend repricing is sector-wide rather than idiosyncratic to one market.

If it does, the office-conversion story shifts from necessity to opportunism. Near term, the more immediate swing factor for the whole sector is whether the drop in the 10-year to 4.99% holds, since that duration tailwind is the main offset to office-specific vacancy headwinds and today's price action suggests it wasn't enough on its own.
