Macro Update

Fed Minutes Turn Hawkish While Spreads Keep Grinding Tighter

Fed minutes showed 'many' officials see a need for further rate increases if inflation doesn't cool, a materially more hawkish tone than the market had been pricing. Powell separately confirmed he will remain on the Fed's board after his chair term expires next month, which keeps him inside the institution through the transition. The disconnect is that HY OAS and IG OAS both tightened on the same day the minutes hit, with credit pricing no incremental default risk from a higher-for-longer path.

Read more

Minutes from the latest Fed meeting showed broader internal support for rate increases than previously understood, with officials warning hikes may be necessary if inflation fails to cool from its current 3.30% year-over-year pace and a CPI index at 332.81. Separately, Powell said he intends to stay on the Fed's board of governors after his term as chair expires next month, meaning he retains a vote and institutional presence even after a new chair is named. The hawkish minutes landed alongside a UST2 that held flat at 4.19% and a UST10 that fell 1.27% to 4.65%, an unusual combination for a hawkish surprise.

The mechanism here is a split between rate-path repricing and duration demand. Hawkish minutes normally lift front-end yields on higher expected terminal rates, but the 2yr's refusal to move while the 10yr fell suggests the long end is being driven by a separate technical bid, most likely the Treasury buyback flow described elsewhere in today's headlines, rather than by a growth or inflation reassessment.

For credit, the read-through is that spread compression (HY OAS to 2.73, IG OAS to 0.81) is happening despite the hawkish shift, which means the market isn't yet treating a hike as a threat to corporate cash flows or refinancing capacity. That matters directly for the leveraged finance calendar: issuers term financing off spread levels, and if HY OAS keeps grinding tighter even as policy rates stay elevated or rise, all-in yields for new issuance stay manageable even without Fed cuts. The risk is a snapback if the hike actually materializes and spreads have to catch up to the front-end move rather than lead it.

Watch the next CPI print for whether inflation cools enough to take the hike off the table, and watch how Powell's continued board presence shapes the messaging from whoever is named the next chair. If HY OAS decouples and widens in response to any actual rate action, that is the signal the credit market was wrong to shrug off the minutes.

Bessent's Buyback Is the Long End's Real Bid

Treasury Secretary Bessent said the department's buyback operation could exceed $4 billion and could grow further, an unusual acknowledgment that debt management is now doing active work on yields. Fed officials are reportedly treading carefully in the wake of the intervention, wary of how it blurs debt management with monetary accommodation. This lines up with the UST10 falling 1.27% to 4.65% on the same day hawkish Fed minutes would normally have pushed yields higher.

Read more

Bessent told CNBC and Reuters that the current buyback operation could total more than $4 billion and signaled the size could increase from there. The Council on Foreign Relations piece framed the move as a surprise significant enough to change how the bond market interprets Treasury's role in yield formation, and Reuters separately reported that Fed officials are treading carefully around the intervention, a sign the central bank sees it as encroaching on territory that normally belongs to monetary policy.

The mechanism is straightforward: buybacks retire off-the-run and less liquid Treasury issues, concentrating market depth in benchmark securities and creating persistent demand for existing paper. That demand shows up as a price bid, meaning lower yields, independent of what the Fed does with the policy rate. It is a technical, not macro, driver of the 10yr's move, which explains why it happened on a day when the substantive policy news (hawkish minutes) argued the opposite direction.

For financing markets, this matters because a suppressed long end lowers the reference rate off which corporate bond and leveraged loan pricing is built, even as HY OAS (2.73) and IG OAS (0.81) are already historically tight. Cheaper Treasury duration plus tight spreads is a favorable combination for issuers running refinancing calendars into 2026, but some analysts flagged in the wire that buyback expansion could itself be inflationary by loosening effective financial conditions, which complicates the Fed's own hawkish signaling from the same day.

Watch the cadence and size of the next few buyback operations, and whether Bessent's comments about growing them further are followed through. If the Fed pushes back publicly on the buyback's effect on financial conditions, that tension between Treasury and the central bank becomes a story in itself, and worth tracking against any move in the UST10 that can't be explained by the data alone.

Sources: CNBC · Reuters · Council on Foreign Relations

Iran has vowed a 'devastating' response as Washington threatens its toughest-ever economic pressure. WTI rose 0.51% to $86.48 and is set for a second weekly gain, giving the physical squeeze a direct market print.

Read more

Reuters reported that ships passing through Hormuz have fallen to single-digit daily counts and that Iranian oil offers to Chinese buyers are declining as the US tightens its blockade. Iran's government said it must overcome what it called unjust sanctions, while separately vowing a devastating response to what the US is framing as its toughest-ever economic pressure campaign, all against the backdrop of what Reuters marked as six months of war. WTI closed the session at $86.48, up 0.51%, on track for its second consecutive weekly gain.

The mechanism connecting shipping data to price is that falling transit counts and buyer pullback are leading indicators of tightening physical supply, priced into the crude curve well before any formal disruption is confirmed. The market treats reduced throughput at a chokepoint carrying a large share of global crude flow as a probability-weighted supply shock, which is why WTI is bid even without a headline event forcing the move.

This complicates the same day's hawkish Fed minutes narrative. Energy-driven headline inflation, visible already in Japan's headline rate hitting its highest level this year on energy costs, gives the 'many officials' case for a hike a more concrete transmission channel into US CPI. For leveraged issuers in transport, chemicals, and other crude-linked sectors, a sustained oil bid is a direct margin pressure even as HY OAS sits at a tight 2.73, meaning spread compensation for that specific input-cost risk may be underpriced.

Watch for any confirmed disruption or escalation at Hormuz over the coming days, and whether Iranian rhetoric translates into an actual supply action rather than continued rhetorical escalation. A break above the recent WTI range on a weekly closing basis would confirm the physical read is becoming a durable repricing rather than a headline-driven spike.

Until then, watch whether crude actually clears toward the $93 to $95 area referenced in the softer outlets or converges back down toward the 86.48 print in this data set, since that gap needs to close one direction and will determine whether the E&P buyback and midstream yield trades have follow-through or were priced on a stale headline.

The dominant story in tech right now is capex intensity outrunning revenue conversion. Alibaba posted a 45% jump in AI cloud revenue but saw profit fall 75% as capex surged 75%, a combination two separate outlets framed as a margin squeeze rather than a growth story. Alphabet's reported $5.9 billion Q2 cash burn is being cast as a CFO-led fight over 2026 capex allocation, suggesting even the best-capitalized hyperscalers are facing internal pushback on the pace of infrastructure spend. Kingsoft Cloud traded lower on the same dynamic, AI-driven growth arriving alongside capex strain that is compressing near-term profitability.

The dispersion is concentrated in who spends versus who supplies. Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA are being framed as the direct beneficiaries of AI capex, with AMAT cited as up 98% in 2026, because equipment orders monetize the build-out immediately regardless of whether the hyperscaler or cloud operator ever turns a marginal AI dollar into a marginal profit dollar. Meanwhile a separate report notes semiconductor stocks broadly sliding even as rates fall, arguing money is rotating away from chips and asking where it goes next, a sign the market is starting to discriminate between capex beneficiaries and capex payers rather than buying the AI theme as a monolith. Microsoft continues to top screens as the preferred large-cap automation and AI name, benefiting from scale and diversified cash flow that smaller cloud operators lack.

This is a financing conditions story as much as an earnings story. UST10 fell to 4.65%, SOFR eased to 3.62%, and both IG OAS (0.81%, -1.22%) and HY OAS (2.73%, -0.73%) tightened on the day, meaning the cost of debt-funding AI infrastructure keeps improving even as the equity market debates whether that spend is being allocated well. For balance-sheet-rich hyperscalers, cheaper financing supports continued reliance on debt and off-balance-sheet structures like leases to fund capex without touching buyback capacity, which is why Alphabet's cash burn is a governance and capital-allocation debate rather than a credit event. For less-capitalized cloud operators like Alibaba and Kingsoft Cloud, the same capex ramp shows up faster in reported margins and profit, and a 75% capex surge against a 75% profit decline is the kind of print that draws scrutiny on returns on invested capital well before it becomes a leverage problem.

From here the setup is a widening gap between equipment suppliers riding order backlogs and cloud operators absorbing depreciation and power costs before revenue catches up. The next catalyst is hyperscaler guidance on 2026 capex budgets and whether CFOs signal any pullback in pace, which would hit equipment names first and hardest given how much of the AMAT-Lam-KLA move is backlog-dependent. Continued tightening in HY and IG spreads would keep the financing backdrop supportive for debt-funded infrastructure builds, but a reversal in that trend, or a rating agency comment on leverage tied to AI capex, would force the capex-payer names to defend the spending pace on the next call rather than the growth headline.

The next real catalyst is regulatory rather than macro.
