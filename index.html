Macro Update

10-Year at 5.15% as Fed Pause Consensus Hardens Into December Hike Bet

A survey of 100 economists unanimously expects the Fed to hold on October 28, but 94 of 100 now see a December hike, and the 10-year yield reflects that reassessment at 5.15%. Treasury yields have been described as steady into the decision, but the curve is pricing a central bank that isn't done tightening. For leveraged finance, a 10-year anchored above 5% keeps the cost of new paper elevated regardless of what happens at this specific meeting.

Read more

The 10-year yield sits at 5.15%, up modestly on the day, while the 2-year is at 4.77% and SOFR at 3.88%. Multiple wire pieces describe Treasury yields as holding steady ahead of the October 28 Fed decision, but the unanimous 100-economist survey expecting a pause is paired with 94 of 100 expecting a December hike, which is the more consequential data point embedded in the curve right now.

The transmission mechanism here runs through term premium rather than through the policy rate itself. If the market has already concluded the Fed pauses in October only to hike again in December, then the front end stays pinned near current levels while duration risk gets priced into everything from IG new issue to private credit vehicles marking against SOFR plus a spread. A 10-year that refuses to come down even as the Fed holds is a signal that inflation persistence, not just the policy path, is what's keeping long rates elevated.

For credit books, the practical effect shows up in refinancing math. Issuers terming out floating-rate exposure into fixed are locking in a materially higher all-in cost than a year of cuts would have implied, and that's consistent with the Radiology Partners story of pivoting to high yield bonds for an acquisition specifically because the lending market is described as weak. IG OAS ticked up 2.35% to 0.87% and HY OAS ticked down 0.60% to 3.29%, both small moves, but the backdrop of a 5%-plus 10-year is the reason absolute yields on new paper stay high even when spreads don't move much.

Watch the jobless claims and mortgage data due this week, which CNBC and other outlets flag as the next data checkpoints before October 28. A soft claims print would give the Fed more room to signal a genuine pause rather than a hawkish hold, and that's what would need to happen for the 10-year to retreat from current levels rather than grind toward the warnings from Citi's Nathan Sheets about fiscal and AI financing needs pushing yields higher still.

Sources: CNBC · CryptoRank · Radiology Business

Oil Jumps $2 on Iran Diplomacy Standstill, Pulling Gold Lower

WTI rose 1.04% to 101.91 after Reuters reported oil prices jumping roughly two dollars as Iran diplomacy failed to progress, with Gulf shares retreating and Indian Oil trimming Middle East imports under annual deals. Gold fell 0.54% to 377.65, with Reuters attributing the move directly to the elevated oil price rather than to a dollar or rates story. The combination keeps headline inflation sticky at a moment the Fed is trying to signal confidence in disinflation.

Read more

WTI closed at 101.91, up 1.04% on the session, consistent with the Reuters headline describing a roughly two-dollar jump tied to stalled Iran diplomacy. Gulf equities retreated on the same news, Indian Oil said it would trim Middle East crude imports under its annual contracts, and Iraq closed the Sheeb border crossing with Iran to review security, all pointing to a supply-side risk premium rather than a demand-driven move.

The mechanism that matters for rates is the inflation pass-through. CPI year-over-year already sits at 3.72% and the index level rose 0.65% in the latest read, so an oil price that's climbing on geopolitical risk rather than falling back adds to the case that disinflation is stalling out, which is precisely the dynamic underpinning the December hike bets embedded in the Treasury curve.

Gold's decline to 377.65 on the back of the oil move, as Reuters framed it, is a reminder that gold is not behaving as a pure geopolitical hedge here. If oil-driven inflation fears were the dominant force, gold would typically catch a bid alongside crude; instead it's down, suggesting the market is still treating this as a rates and dollar story more than a flight-to-safety one, with the DXY essentially flat at 121.40.

The next confirming data point is whatever comes out of Iran-US diplomatic channels this week, since a de-escalation would unwind the oil premium quickly given how headline-driven the move has been. Barring that, watch whether CPI prints continue drifting toward or away from the 3.72% trend, since that's the number the Fed will use to justify how hawkish December can credibly be.

Sources: Reuters · Reuters · Reuters

When specific credits move enough that holders feel compelled to publicly defend them, that's a signal worth more than the headline OAS print.

For leveraged finance desks, the second-order effect is in how new deals get priced and structured. If lenders are pulling back from certain sectors, as implied by Radiology Partners needing the high yield market rather than a loan, that pushes more deal flow toward bond markets and potentially into private credit vehicles at a moment when the 10-year anchor at 5.15% already makes all-in cost higher. The CoreWeave situation specifically is the kind of story that could widen spreads for an entire cohort of AI-infrastructure-linked credits if it isn't contained.

Watch whether the CoreWeave spread pressure stays contained to the name or starts showing up in comparable data center and AI infrastructure credits, and whether IG OAS, which ticked up 2.35% to 0.87%, continues drifting wider even from a historically tight level. A broadening of the pressure beyond single names would be the signal that this is more than an isolated overreaction.

The dominant story is a divergence inside the AI trade, not a sector-wide move. Chip stocks sold off on fears that hyperscaler AI capital spending is being trimmed or paced more slowly than the market had priced, a worry sharp enough to knock the Kospi down 3.5% as investors there read through to Korean memory and equipment suppliers. Deutsche Bank's note on 'different ways to skin capex' and a Yahoo Finance piece on a possible 20% cost reduction at Microsoft's Azure are the specific data points feeding that fear, raising the question of whether hyperscalers are disciplining spend or simply reallocating it toward different infrastructure.

Apple hit a new high the same day chips were sold, which is the clearest evidence this is dispersion, not a sector verdict. If capex spending were genuinely being pulled back in a way that threatened credit quality at the chip or cloud infrastructure names, HY spreads would be the first place it showed up, and they aren't.

The next real test is Big Tech earnings, specifically whether capex guidance confirms the Azure-style pullback speculation or shows spending merely shifting composition rather than shrinking. A confirmed guide-down from a major hyperscaler would validate the chip-stock selloff and likely pressure XLK's semi-heavy constituents further. A re-acceleration or reaffirmation of prior capex plans would flip the narrative back toward the Apple-style read that the AI trade's earnings power is intact and the chip selloff was overdone.

Health Care XLV +2.37%

FDA approval cluster drives outperformance

XLV rose 2.37% to 179.82, by far the best major sector tape of the session against a Dow up 1.02%, an S&P up 0.28% and a flat Nasdaq. The move coincided with an unusually dense run of FDA actions spanning oncology, autoimmune disease, medtech and generics. Risk appetite broadly was supportive, with the VIX down 7.75% to 18.44 and HY OAS tightening 6 basis points to 3.29%, but health care's outperformance was large enough relative to the index that the regulatory newsflow looks like the proximate driver rather than just beta to a calmer tape.

Read more

The session's defining feature was volume of regulatory output rather than any single blockbuster decision. The FDA granted accelerated approval to GSK's Jemperli, approved Cosette's Evoxac oral solution for Sjogren's disease, cleared the first generic for the Climara Pro hormone therapy patch, approved a once-weekly generic hormone patch, granted Roche's Enspryng a second indication for an eye disease, and cleared Haemonetics' vascular closure system, alongside a reported AstraZeneca-Oracle partnership on AI-powered clinical trials. Taken individually none of these is sector-moving, but taken together they amount to a cleared backlog that reduces near-term regulatory overhang across pharma, biotech and medtech simultaneously.

The dispersion within the move likely favors smaller, more event-driven names over the large-cap pharma complex. Credit conditions were a tailwind rather than a headline driver today. High yield spreads tightened 6 basis points to 3.29% and the VIX fell sharply, both consistent with an environment where royalty-backed and clinical-stage biotech names face a lower cost of capital for future financing or M&A, which matters given how directly approval-driven royalty structures like AnaptysBio's depend on capital markets access. Investment grade spreads ticked up slightly to 87 basis points, a small move that does not change the picture for the large-cap pharma issuers that dominate the sector's debt stack.

The question from here is whether this was a one-day clearing of an FDA docket or the start of a trend in approval cadence that the sector can keep trading on. Watch whether AnaptysBio's royalty economics get quantified in subsequent disclosures, since that is the one name in today's slate with a clear read-through to valuation, and watch whether the AstraZeneca-Oracle AI trials partnership generates follow-on commentary on trial cost or timeline compression, which would be a longer-duration efficiency story rather than a single-day catalyst.

Rally outruns the vacancy data

Read more

XLRE's 1.70% gain is a sizable outperformance against a flat-to-modestly-higher broader tape, but nothing in today's real estate headlines points to a sector-specific trigger. Instead the move lines up with the day's broader risk-on signature: VIX down 7.75%, Russell up 1.66%, Dow up 1.02%. That pattern, equities and high-beta cyclicals up together with credit spreads mixed, is more consistent with a market-wide repricing of risk appetite than with any new real estate catalyst. The disconnect is notable because the 10-year yield rose to 5.15%, up 1.18% on the session, which is the kind of move that typically weighs on REIT valuations given their sensitivity to discount rates on long-duration cash flows.

The property-level data in the headlines is genuinely mixed by subsector rather than uniformly improving, which makes the equity rally harder to tie to fundamentals. Office is split: Cleveland just posted a record-high vacancy rate while Scotsman Guide separately claims the office sector hit an 'inflection point' in its third-quarter recovery, and Grand Rapids is pointing to conversions as a way to work down obsolete stock. Industrial development is still happening despite rising vacancy, illustrated by a planned 40-acre industrial park near Research Triangle Park even as the region's vacancy climbs. The CommercialCafe piece on a shrinking development pipeline is the closest thing to a financing-conditions story here, and it cuts both ways. That same dynamic is the bull case the piece makes: fewer new deliveries gives over-supplied markets, like the life sciences hubs sitting near 27% vacancy or office markets with record vacancy in metros like Cleveland, more runway to absorb existing excess space without fresh competition. IG spreads ticked up slightly to 0.87 while HY tightened to 3.29, a split that doesn't show obvious stress building in real estate credit specifically, but a 10-year holding above 5% keeps refinancing costs elevated for any sponsor with near-term debt maturities.

The real test for this rally is whether it survives a rate backdrop that isn't actually easing. A single day of index-wide risk-on buying can lift REIT equities even as the 10-year rises, but a sustained move in XLRE on a higher-rate tape would be unusual and worth scrutinizing rather than extrapolating. The next real catalyst is whatever actual leasing and vacancy data follow: confirmation that the office 'inflection point' claim extends beyond isolated metros, or further evidence that life sciences oversupply near 27% is stabilizing rather than worsening, would matter more than another broad-market VIX-driven session.

Within the sector, the dispersion is coming from idiosyncratic stories rather than a sector-wide re-rating.
