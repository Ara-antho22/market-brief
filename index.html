Macro Update

10-Year Holds Near 24-Year Highs as Fed Minutes Keep December Hike Alive

The 10-year sits at 5.22%, a level Stocktwits and TradingView both frame as a 24-year high, even though it eased 1.14% on the day. Fed minutes showed most officials still expect another hike this year with timing left open, and Danske is reportedly flagging risk of 6% on the long end. Equities rallied anyway, with the S&P up 0.59%, suggesting the market is pricing resilient earnings against the higher-rate backdrop rather than fighting it.

Read more

The 10-year Treasury yield printed 5.22%, down 1.14% intraday but still framed by multiple outlets as a 24-year high, with a $39 billion 10-year note auction on deck this week and Danske reportedly flagging a path toward 6% if pressure persists. The 2-year sits at 4.75%, down a smaller 0.42%, keeping the 2s10s spread around 47 basis points. Fed minutes released this cycle showed most officials still see another hike coming this year, with the timing explicitly left open rather than calendared, a stance distinct from the prior decision to hold rates steady.

The mechanism here runs through the discount rate on every duration asset in the market, equity multiples and corporate debt alike. When the risk-free rate anchors near multi-decade highs, the present value of long-dated cash flows compresses, which is why a 10-year at 5.22% should in theory cap multiple expansion even as the S&P grinds higher on earnings. The fact that equities rallied 0.59% into this backdrop signals the market believes the hike risk is priced and that growth, not rates, is the swing factor for now.

For leveraged issuers, a 10-year anchored above 5% raises the all-in cost of any new term loan or bond priced off the curve, and it widens the gap between legacy fixed coupons and refinancing economics. That gap is visible in the HY OAS move to 3.15, a 1.94% widening on the day even as IG spreads held flat at 0.82, which tells you higher-beta credit is absorbing the rate repricing first. CLO managers and leveraged loan allocators, both referenced in today's flow, are the natural pressure point if base rates stay elevated into primary calendars.

The next real test is this week's CPI print, which bond traders are explicitly positioned around per Bloomberg's coverage, alongside any signal from Warsh on the Fed's rate path. A hotter-than-expected core number would validate the minutes' hawkish lean and likely push the 10-year back toward the cited 6% risk case; a softer print would let the curve re-steepen on hike-off bets. Watch the 10-year auction tail and bid-to-cover for a read on whether demand is keeping pace with supply at these yields.

Sources: Bloomberg.com · Stocktwits · ABC11 News

High Yield Spreads Widen 1.94% While IG Sits Flat, and Dimon Flags Private Credit

HY OAS widened to 3.15 from the prior level, a 1.94% move, while IG OAS was unchanged at 0.82, a split that puts the stress squarely in lower-rated and privately held credit rather than the broad market. JPMorgan's Dimon warned that private credit losses will exceed expectations in the next downturn, and separate coverage noted bond investors are starting to say no to the AI debt binge. HYG, the high yield ETF proxy, barely budged at +0.12%, so the spread move is a relative value story more than a liquidity event.

Read more

The spread data shows a clear bifurcation: HY OAS at 3.15 widened 1.94% on the session while IG OAS held flat at 0.82, and HYG itself was essentially unchanged at +0.12%. Layered on top, JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon issued a direct warning that private credit losses will exceed current expectations when the next downturn hits, while separate reporting flagged that bond investors are starting to push back on the pace of AI-related debt issuance.

The mechanism is a repricing of credit risk at the margin rather than a broad risk-off move. Public high yield reprices in real time against the Treasury curve, so as the 10-year sits near 24-year highs, HY spreads widen first to preserve the all-in yield demanded by buyers, while IG issuers with stronger balance sheets absorb the rate move without needing spread compensation. Private credit, by contrast, marks less frequently, which is precisely Dimon's point: losses accumulate off the public market's radar until a downturn forces repricing all at once.

For leveraged finance specifically, this is the signal to watch on new-issue concessions and OID in both the broadly syndicated loan and private credit markets. If bond investors are already resisting AI-linked debt issuance, that pushback will show up first in wider spreads or reduced size on data center and infrastructure financings tied to AI capex, the exact paper that has driven much of this year's leveraged supply. CLO managers, who buy the loan tranches behind much of this issuance, are a direct transmission point if demand for the underlying paper softens.

Confirmation would come from actual primary market pricing on the next wave of AI-adjacent leveraged loans or private credit facilities, specifically whether arrangers need to flex spread or OID wider to clear deals. Watch for any follow-through commentary from Dimon or other bank CEOs on private credit mark-to-market practices, and watch HY OAS for whether this week's widening extends or reverses once the CPI print clarifies the rate path.

Sticky Inflation Meets Resilient Claims But Softening Wages

CPI year-over-year sits at 3.35% with the index itself up 0.40% on the month, while unemployment ticked up 2.44% to 4.20%, a combination that keeps the Fed's hike bias intact per the minutes. Jobless claims remain near a 57-year low, so layoffs are not the transmission channel, but wage growth is slowing after an August bounce, which means real incomes are being squeezed from the inflation side rather than the employment side. That split is exactly why most Fed officials are reportedly still inclined toward another hike despite a labor market that looks resilient on the surface.

Read more

The CPI index stands at 334.13, up 0.40% on the month, with year-over-year inflation at 3.35%. Unemployment registered 4.20%, a 2.44% increase, even as initial jobless claims remain near a 57-year low according to separate coverage. Wage growth, meanwhile, slowed in September after an August bounce, and real wages are declining against the elevated CPI print per the Real Economy Blog's tracking.

The mechanism is that inflation and labor are sending different signals to the Fed's reaction function. A resilient claims number argues against the labor side forcing a dovish pivot, while sticky CPI and a slight uptick in the unemployment rate together argue the Fed cannot yet declare victory on prices without risking a wage-price dynamic even as paychecks fail to keep pace. That is precisely the combination the minutes point to when they describe most officials still leaning toward another hike this year with timing left open.

For credit markets, a Fed that stays open to hiking keeps the floating-rate cost of capital elevated for longer, which matters directly for interest coverage ratios on leveraged loans and private credit facilities priced off SOFR, currently at 3.87%. Borrowers who underwrote deals assuming rate cuts by now are instead facing another possible hike, which tightens the math on coverage covenants and refinancing capacity precisely as HY spreads are already widening.

The next CPI release, which multiple outlets note bond traders are explicitly positioned around, is the clearing event. A core print above expectations would reinforce the minutes' hawkish lean and likely extend the back-up in yields; a cooler print would give the Fed room to let the December decision slide. Watch wage data alongside it, since the Fed's own commentary ties persistent inflation risk to a tight labor market that current wage trends are only partially supporting.
