Morning Edition

Wednesday, November 4, 2026

Newsletter Summary

Equities split along a line that matters for positioning: the S&P 500 fell 0.51% to 7,541.40 and the Dow lost 0.38%, while the Nasdaq added 0.83%, with mega-cap growth holding up even as broader risk appetite softened. The VIX jumped 8.91% to 21.15, the clearest sign that hedging demand picked up overnight, though the level itself is still well short of anything resembling a stress regime. Treasuries diverged too: the 10-year yield fell to 4.91% while the 2-year rose to 4.59%, a flattening move that pairs long-end buying on growth concerns with front-end caution tied to inflation that is still running at 3.72% year over year and an unemployment rate that ticked up to 4.20%. Credit did not confirm the equity jitters, with high yield OAS actually tightening 0.29% to 3.43 and investment grade spreads unchanged at 0.94, so the AI-debt headlines circulating this morning have not yet shown up as a repricing in cash bonds. Oil slid 1.79% to $103.37 and gold eased 0.66% to $359.26 while the dollar index was essentially flat at 121.67. Overseas moves were larger than anything domestic: the Nikkei dropped 1.42% and the FTSE fell 1.52% ahead of the Bank of England's rate decision.

Macro Update

Midterm Sweep Splits Equities and the Curve

The Democratic sweep in the midterms produced genuinely divided headlines, with Reuters describing a stocks-and-bonds rally and a dollar slide to a one-month low while Daily Sabah called the move a blow to Trump that sent stocks lower. Today's actual print reflects that split: the S&P and Dow closed lower while the Nasdaq gained, and the dollar index sits flat rather than extending any slide. The more useful signal is in rates, where the curve flattened on a divided-government read.

Read more

Reuters reported both that futures rose as investors assessed the sweep and that stocks and bonds rallied as the dollar fell to a one-month low, while Daily Sabah's framing was the opposite, calling the session a slide and a blow to Trump. The cash session landed in between: S&P down 0.51%, Dow down 0.38%, Nasdaq up 0.83%, and DXY essentially unchanged at 121.67 rather than showing a continued slide. The 10-year yield fell to 4.91% and the 2-year rose to 4.59%, a flattening that doesn't fit cleanly into either the rally or the slide narrative on its own.

Divided government is typically priced as a reduction in fiscal-expansion and tax-extension risk, which is consistent with the long end rallying as term premium comes down. The front end moving the other way reflects that inflation at 3.72% year over year and a 4.20% unemployment rate are still live enough to keep the market from pricing aggressive near-term easing, even as political risk recedes. That combination, long yields down and short yields up, is a bear-flattener dressed as a political story.

SOFR ticked up 0.51% to 3.97%, so funding costs are edging higher at the same time long-duration paper rallies, which squeezes the carry on any trade funded short and held long. Credit held steady through this, with HY OAS tightening to 3.43 and IG OAS unchanged at 0.94, suggesting the political repricing is a rates and equity-positioning story rather than one that has reached corporate credit risk premia.

Watch whether the divided-government read holds into the next few sessions or reverses, since Reuters already flagged that the sweep adds a new twist to existing worries about Iran, AI and debt rather than resolving them. The next confirming data points are the FOMC decision StoneX flagged as a near-term catalyst and Friday's jobs report, which MarketWatch framed as capable of triggering a major repricing of the Fed's path on its own.

Sources: Reuters · Daily Sabah · Economies.com · Reuters

Eurozone Inflation at 4% Puts December ECB Hike in Play

Eurozone inflation hit 4% in October for the first time in three years, driven by energy costs, and Morningstar now frames that as boosting the case for an ECB hike in December. The FTSE 100 fell 1.52% while awaiting its own Bank of England decision, a sharper move than the 0.26% dip in the EuroStoxx. For desks pricing European leveraged paper, a validated hike changes the base-rate assumption embedded in floating-rate structures, not just the equity discount rate.

Read more

CNBC, Reuters, WSJ, Euronews and the FT all confirmed eurozone HICP rose to 4% year over year in October, its highest print in three years, with the WSJ and Euronews specifically attributing the move to energy costs. Morningstar's read is that this strengthens the case for the ECB to hike in December. In parallel, the BBC was covering what rising UK rates mean for mortgages, and Yahoo Finance UK flagged the FTSE 100 edging on the day while investors await the Bank of England's own rate decision.

An energy-driven inflation print changes the ECB's reaction function differently than a pure core-services story would, because it raises the risk that elevated headline prints feed into wage demands and inflation expectations even as growth stays soft. A validated December hike pushes up the policy rate path priced into EUR short-end curves, which raises EURIBOR-linked floors embedded in European term loan documentation and widens the base-rate component of any floating-rate instrument regardless of what happens to credit spreads.

That matters directly for leveraged credit because the all-in coupon on new issuance is base rate plus spread, and if HY OAS stays tight at 3.43 while the EUR base rate moves higher, borrowers still face a higher total cost of debt even without any deterioration in credit quality being priced by the market. The BBC's UK mortgage coverage points to the same mechanism working through UK consumer and housing-linked credit as the Bank of England weighs its own decision.

The next confirming data point is the Bank of England's decision itself, which the FTSE is explicitly trading ahead of per Yahoo Finance UK, along with whether the ECB actually delivers the December hike that Morningstar says is now being priced. A hike that doesn't materialize, or a softer core read beneath the energy-driven headline number, would be the signal that this is a one-off cost shock rather than the start of a renewed tightening cycle.

Sources: CNBC · WSJ · Morningstar · Yahoo Finance UK · BBC

AI Debt Moves From Equity Story to Credit Story

CoreWeave bonds are yielding 15%, which the Motley Fool frames as a warning sign, and M&G Credit Income chose to sit out Amazon's £4.25 billion bond debut, citing an AI borrowing binge that is shifting risk from equity to debt markets. None of that stress shows up in the aggregate numbers yet: HY OAS tightened to 3.43 and IG OAS held at 0.94, while Crescent Capital still priced a $406.9 million CLO. The gap between single-name AI credit stress and index-level spread calm is the thing to watch from a leveraged finance seat.

Read more

The Motley Fool reported CoreWeave's bonds yielding 15%, calling it a warning for AI-linked debt broadly, while QuotedData reported that M&G Credit Income specifically avoided Amazon's £4.25 billion bond debut, framing the AI borrowing binge as shifting risk from equity to debt markets. Barron's separately flagged AI debt hitting record levels at what it called the worst possible time. Against that, Crescent Capital priced a $406.9 million CLO, and index spreads stayed tight: HY OAS at 3.43, down 0.29%, IG OAS flat at 0.94, and HYG up 0.12%.

The mechanism is that hyperscalers and AI infrastructure issuers are increasingly funding capex with debt rather than equity, pushing leverage onto balance sheets backing cash flows that depend on GPU utilization and depreciation assumptions that are themselves uncertain. Bond investors are pricing that uncertainty at the name level, which is why CoreWeave's yield sits where it does, but that repricing has not yet broadened into the index-level spread measures that would signal a systemic reassessment of credit risk.

That divergence is exactly the setup that matters for structured credit: CLO managers like Crescent are still warehousing loans into vehicles priced off today's tight OAS levels, so if AI-linked credit stress broadens, CLO equity and mezzanine tranches would absorb the repricing before it shows up in the headline HY index. M&G's decision to pass on the Amazon deal is a data point of selective buyer discipline at the single-credit level, not evidence of a market-wide pullback in demand for AI-linked paper.

Watch the new-issue concessions on the next wave of data-center and GPU-backed financings, and whether HY OAS or HYG start to move with the single-name AI credit stress rather than independently of it. A broadening of that stress into the index would be the signal that financing conditions for the sector have genuinely tightened, rather than the current state where the equity story is cooling while the cash credit market still prices business as usual.

Sources: The Motley Fool · QuotedData · Barron's · Yahoo Finance

Midterm results drag crude lower

Renewables outperformed on a Democratic sweep of Congress, widening the split within the sector. Credit markets barely moved, with HY OAS actually tightening 0.29% to 3.43, signaling no stress read-through from the oil pullback.

Read more

Crude gave back ground as the market worked through two competing signals from the midterms: a Democratic sweep of Congress that raises the odds of tighter fossil fuel policy over time, and continued uncertainty over Iran that keeps a geopolitical risk premium embedded in the price. WSJ's framing of oil falling as investors weigh both midterm results and Iran risk captures the tension, crude is pricing election-driven policy uncertainty against a supply risk that hasn't resolved. The net was a 1.79% drop to $103.37, enough to pull XLE down with it but not a disorderly move by crude's own volatility standards.

The dispersion inside energy is the story worth watching. OilPrice.com's headline on renewables stocks rallying on the Democratic sweep points to money rotating toward clean energy names on the expectation of friendlier policy, while traditional E&Ps and integrateds absorbed the brunt of the crude decline. Separately, reporting on Exxon and Chevron shows profits eroding even in a period of elevated Iran-war-driven prices, a reminder that higher headline crude hasn't been translating cleanly into margin for the majors, likely a function of cost inflation and refining dynamics rather than top-line pricing alone.

There's no credit stress signal here. IG OAS was unchanged at 0.94 and HY OAS actually tightened slightly to 3.43, so energy issuers aren't seeing their financing costs move against them despite the crude pullback. That stability matters for E&P capital discipline: with spreads intact, there's no external pressure forcing capex cuts or accelerated refinancing, leaving operators free to manage production and shareholder returns off their own price assumptions rather than a tightening funding backdrop.

The swing factor from here is policy follow-through. If the Democratic sweep translates into concrete regulatory proposals targeting fossil fuel producers, expect the renewables-versus-traditional-energy split to widen further, with E&Ps and integrateds facing multiple compression even if crude stabilizes. On the commodity side, any escalation or de-escalation in Iran remains the single catalyst capable of moving WTI outside this week's range and forcing a repricing of the sector's risk premium.

The setup from here is a test of whether the Meta action is idiosyncratic or a template. The FDA approved the first generic version of lenacapavir, the long-acting injectable used for HIV prevention, but POZ's headline specifically notes the approval is not for the U.S. market, a distinction Unitaid's coverage of the same action also implies by framing it in a global-access context. That carve-out matters because Gilead's branded lenacapavir franchise (marketed domestically as Yeztugo) retains U.S. exclusivity even as a generic clears FDA review for use elsewhere, which caps the near-term read-through to Gilead's pricing power at home while potentially expanding volume in markets where the generic can launch.

For uniQure, the priority review clock now runs toward an actual approval decision that will be a binary stock event rather than a sector mover.
