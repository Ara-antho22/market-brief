Macro Update

Fed Holds at the Same Moment the Curve Pushes Through 5%

The Fed kept rates steady even as CPI hit a three-year high and the jobs report showed only 29,000 added with unemployment rising to 4.2%. The 10-year closed at 5.00% and the 2-year at 4.69%, both up modestly on the day. That combination, a hold alongside firming inflation and softening labor, is the classic setup that keeps long-end yields sticky rather than falling.

Read more

The Fed's hold, confirmed by ABC News against a backdrop of CPI at a three-year high, landed the same week the labor data showed just 29,000 jobs added and unemployment rising to 4.2%, a pairing that leaves the committee with neither a clean disinflation story nor a clean growth scare to act on. The 10-year yield closed at 5.00% and the 2-year at 4.69%, both up roughly four basis points on the session, while SOFR sits at 3.93%.

The mechanism is straightforward: a Fed that holds while inflation prints hot removes the near-term case for the long end to rally, because the market has to price a longer runway of restrictive policy rather than a glide path to cuts. At the same time, a weak jobs print of 29,000 keeps growth risk alive, so term premium gets bid on both inflation uncertainty and growth uncertainty simultaneously, which is why the curve can stay elevated without a single clean narrative driving it.

For leveraged balance sheets, a 10-year at 5% and SOFR at 3.93% means the cost of carry on floating-rate debt and the discount rate on duration-sensitive assets both stay elevated longer than a cuts-this-year narrative would imply. That matters directly for refinancing walls in leveraged loans and for any sponsor underwriting new LBO debt off a base rate assumption, since every quarter the hold persists is a quarter of carry cost that has to be absorbed rather than termed out at a lower rate.

Watch the next CPI print and the FOMC minutes for whether the committee characterizes this as a pause or a plateau. The Bank of Canada's decision this week, expected to hike on oil-driven inflation per Bloomberg's preview, is a useful cross-check on whether commodity-driven inflation is forcing other central banks back toward tightening even as the Fed stands still.

Sources: ABC News · ASBN Small Business Network · Bloomberg.com

IG Spreads Widen at the Margin While AI Debt Buildout Gets Flagged

HY OAS sits at 338bps, down about 2bps on the day, while IG OAS sits at 91bps, up about 2bps, both still historically tight in absolute terms. The move itself is noise, but it lands alongside BofA and Apollo commentary warning that the AI capex-driven debt buildout, cited at $680 billion, is a concentration risk the bond market is starting to price. That is a story about where the next repricing comes from, not about today's spread level.

Read more

HY OAS closed at 338bps, tighter by roughly 2bps, while IG OAS closed at 91bps, wider by roughly 2bps. Neither move is large by its own history, and both indices remain near the tight end of their post-crisis ranges, which is the more important fact than the day's direction. Against that backdrop, BofA published a note arguing there is value in credit tranches amid wider spreads, and Apollo warned that the AI investment boom has only paid off for a narrow slice of tech while the bond market is flashing caution on the roughly $680 billion buildout behind it.

The mechanism connecting these two facts is concentration. When AI-linked issuers account for a growing share of IG supply, any repricing of that cohort's credit risk shows up first in the IG index even while broad HY, which skews toward smaller, more diversified issuers, holds steady or tightens on its own idiosyncratic technicals. That is consistent with what the data shows today: a modest IG widening sitting on top of a modest HY tightening, rather than a uniform risk-off move across the credit complex.

For leveraged finance desks, the read-through is on the primary calendar. If IG investors start demanding wider new-issue concessions from AI-capex-linked borrowers, that repricing eventually filters into the leveraged loan and HY markets that fund the same capex chain through vendor financing, data center sponsors, and power infrastructure credits. AllianceBernstein's note arguing shorter duration is smarter in HY right now fits this picture, favoring front-end carry over duration risk in a credit market where the big new money is going into leveraged capex themes.

Confirm or break this by watching whether the IG widening is a one-day wobble or persists across the next several sessions, and whether any AI-linked issuer's new deal comes with a wider-than-expected spread versus comparable credits. A string of IG new issues from data center or hyperscaler-adjacent names pricing wide would be the clearest signal that Apollo's warning is starting to bite rather than just being commentary.

VIX rose 2.21% to 19.45, a modest uptick rather than a volatility event. A 10-year yield at 5.00% is a plausible mechanical driver of that split. Rate-sensitive and duration-heavy sectors that carry more weight in the S&P's broader composition, financials with funding cost exposure and dividend-paying defensives, absorb higher discount rates more directly, while the Nasdaq's concentration in a narrower set of large-cap growth names can hold up or outperform if their own earnings or positioning story is strong enough to offset the higher-rate discount, which is consistent with a one-day rotation rather than a market-wide repricing.

The overseas tape argues against a global growth scare: Nikkei up 1.73%, Shanghai up 0.65%, and European benchmarks essentially flat. That matters for financing conditions because a US-specific equity rotation with global markets intact is a lower-volatility event for cross-asset correlation, credit, and FX than a synchronized risk-off move would be, which is part of why HY spreads barely moved even as the S&P fell.

Watch whether the S&P's underperformance versus the Nasdaq persists through the week or proves to be a single session of rebalancing. If the 10-year holds near 5% and the S&P continues to lag while the Nasdaq and Dow hold gains, that confirms a genuine rate-driven rotation within US equities rather than noise, and it is worth tracking which S&P sectors are driving the laggard performance.
